The MVP Race Is Not Heating Up. It Was Over on Labour Day.

You are about to be told that {{LEADER}} and {{RIVAL}} are locked in a September duel for the {{LEAGUE}} MVP. September does not decide it. September watches it.

{{LEADER}} leads the {{LEAGUE}} in OPS. {{RIVAL}} is close enough that somebody has written the word “race.” The next month will be covered as though the thing is genuinely open.

It is mostly not.

What September Actually Changes

Take every league-season from 2011 through 2025, setting aside the shortened 2020 — 28 league-seasons in total. In each one, find the player leading his league in OPS on 31 August, then see who actually won the MVP.

The 31 August leader won it 15 times. That is 54% — a coin flip that lands on the leader slightly more often than not.

But the sharper number is the next one. The eventual MVP was already inside the 31 August top three in 24 of 28 seasons — 86%. Whatever September is doing, it is almost never introducing a new name.

Twenty-four times out of twenty-eight, the man who won the award was already standing in the top three when the calendar turned to September.

— The Sports Page

The Four That Got Away — and Why Two Do Not Count

Four winners came from outside the 31 August top three: Justin Verlander in 2011, Andrew McCutchen in 2013, Clayton Kershaw in 2014, and Christian Yelich in 2018.

Two of those are Verlander and Kershaw, and they expose a limit of the method rather than a September surge. They are pitchers. An OPS leaderboard cannot rank a pitcher at all, so those two were never going to appear in the top three no matter how the season went. That is the measure failing, not the race turning.

Strip out the seasons a pitcher won, and among position-player MVPs the winner came from the 31 August top three 24 times out of 26 — 92%.

What To Take Home

September coverage has a job to do, and the job is to make a month of baseball feel consequential. So the language reaches for motion: the race tightens, a challenger surges, somebody makes his case. The words describe change because change is what holds attention.

The underlying quantity mostly is not changing. By 31 August a hitter has roughly 550 plate appearances behind him; September adds about a hundred more. A fifth of the evidence arriving last cannot often overturn the four-fifths that came first, and it does not.

That is the general shape worth carrying: when most of the data is already in, late data has to be extraordinary to matter — and it usually is not. It only feels decisive because it arrives while you are watching. Whenever someone tells you a contest is tightening, the useful question is not who is gaining. It is how much is left to count.

How this was computed Leaders and award winners come from the MLB Stats API. For each league-season from 2011 through 2025, excluding the shortened 2020, the OPS leaders were pulled for games from opening day through 31 August and compared against the actual MVP. OPS is a stand-in for “who is in the conversation” rather than a model of the ballot — voters weigh defence, position and team success, and no single rate statistic reproduces that. Its clearest limitation is stated in the piece: it cannot rank pitchers, which is why two of the four outside-the-top-three winners are pitchers.
