Your Team Is Not Still Alive. It Is Just Not Yet Eliminated.

“Still alive” is one of the great load-bearing phrases in sport. It sounds like a status report. It is closer to a tautology.

A team is mathematically alive whenever the games remaining are enough to close the gap, however absurd the required run. That threshold is so low that on 1 September it described 378 of 390 teams — 97% of the league — competing for twelve places. A word that applies to almost everyone is not telling you anything about anyone.

Where The Line Actually Falls

Take every club from 2012 through 2025, setting aside the shortened 2020. On 1 September, measure how far each sat behind the nearest playoff position. Then check who actually got in.

Hold a spot on 1 September and you get in 87% of the time. Sit within three games and it falls to 24%. Between three and six, 12%.

Past six games back, the record over thirteen seasons is 0 for 175.

The deepest September comeback in thirteen years was five games. Not fifteen. Five.

— The Sports Page

Why Zero Is Not The Same As Impossible

It would be easy, and wrong, to read 0 for 175 as a law. A zero in the numerator still carries uncertainty: the honest reading is that the true rate is somewhere below about 2%, not that it is nought. Some September, a club nine games out in the last week of August will run the table and get in, and nothing here forbids it.

What the number does establish is the scale. Arithmetic says thirty teams are in it. History says the field is the dozen or so within about half a week of the line. Everyone else is not competing for a playoff place; they are playing out a schedule.

The arithmetic of the chase explains why. With the same number of games left on both sides, one game in the standings costs one net win. A club six back on 1 September, with roughly twenty-seven to play, must beat a .550 rival by six games in twenty-seven — a .772 stretch, a 125-win pace. Ten back needs .920, which over a full season is 149 wins. Nobody has ever done that for a season, and doing it for a month is not the easier version of the task. It is the same task, run without a margin for a single bad week.

The Story Nobody Runs

Here is the part the coverage almost never gets to. Over the same thirteen seasons, 18 of the 143 teams holding a playoff position on 1 September failed to reach October — about one in eight.

Set that against four comebacks from outside three games. The collapse is roughly four times more common than the charge. September's real drama is not somebody climbing in. It is somebody falling out.

We do not tell it that way because a comeback has a hero and a collapse only has a victim. But if you want to know what September is likely to do to your team, the honest answer depends entirely on which side of the line it is standing on — and the danger, if it is inside, is larger than the hope, if it is outside.

What To Take Home

The trick here is a definition doing work it was never built for. “Mathematically alive” is a statement about arithmetic possibility, and it is being handed to you as though it were a statement about likelihood. Those are different claims, and the first is nearly always true, which is precisely what makes it useless.

The general form shows up everywhere: a threshold that almost nothing fails is not a test. A drug that is “not proven unsafe,” a candidate with “a path to victory,” a business that is “still solvent” — each is a real statement, and each has been quietly swapped in for a much stronger one nobody actually made.

So when you next hear that a team is still alive, the useful question is not whether it is true. It is: how many teams does that sentence describe? If the answer is nearly all of them, you have been told nothing at all.

How this was computed Standings for 1 September of each season from 2012 through 2025, excluding the shortened 2020, come from the MLB Stats API; the actual postseason field comes from the same API's postseason schedule, which returns 10 qualifiers for 2012–2021 and 12 from 2022 — matching the real formats, which is the check that the field was read correctly. A team's deficit is the smaller of its divisional and wild-card games-back, so a club leading its division or holding a wild-card place counts as zero. That choice is deliberately generous to the chasing team and still understates the task, because a team six back of the line must pass every club between it and the line, not one. Rates are pooled across the 10- and 12-team eras; the 12-team era is a little kinder to chasers, as expected, and is reported separately in the dataset.
