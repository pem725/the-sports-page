{{TEAM}} were the better team on paper. The market said so — they were laying {{SPREAD}}. Then {{OPPONENT}} beat them {{SCORE}}, and by Monday morning somebody will have said the sentence that gets said every September: it's one game, don't read anything into it.

It is one game. You should read a great deal into it.

The Raw Number

Every NFL season from 2002 through 2025 — 760 team-seasons, both sides of every opener. Teams that won in Week 1 made the playoffs 54% of the time. Teams that lost made them 25% of the time.

A 28-point swing, off a single game in a seventeen-game season.

The obvious objection is the right one, and it is the reason most people wave this away: of course Week 1 winners make the playoffs more often — good teams win Week 1. The result isn't causing anything. It's just revealing what was already true. That would make the 28 points an artifact of who was good to begin with, not information.

It's a good objection. It happens to be wrong.

Holding "Who Was Supposed to Win" Fixed

The betting market prices every opener before kickoff. So split the teams by what the market expected — favourites and underdogs — and look inside each group. If the 28 points were really just team quality, the gap should mostly vanish once quality is held still.

A favourite that loses its opener is about half as likely to play in January as a favourite that wins one. That is the whole finding, and it survives every control we could put on it.

Why One Game Can Matter This Much

The instinct that one game out of seventeen cannot mean much comes from thinking about it as a fraction. Six per cent of a season. Rounding error.

But playoff berths are not decided by season-long averages. They are decided at a cutline, and the cutline is usually one or two games wide. Divisions get settled by tiebreakers between teams a single win apart. In a seventeen-game season with a fourteen-team field, one loss moves you a meaningful distance along a very crowded ladder — and it does it while also telling you something real about the team you actually have, as opposed to the team the projections had.

Both things are happening at once. The loss costs you position, and it updates the estimate. That is why the effect is bigger than the arithmetic suggests.

What To Take Home

“It's a small sample, don't overreact” is usually good advice, and this newsletter has given it more than once. But it is advice, not a law, and it gets repeated in places it does not apply.

A single observation is uninformative when it is mostly noise. It is extremely informative when it is a rare, high-quality look at something you had only been guessing about. Week 1 is the first real evidence in eight months of guessing — the first time the projections meet a scoreboard. Of course it moves the estimate more than Week 9 does.

So the question to carry is not “is this a small sample?” It is “small compared to what I already knew?” When you knew almost nothing, one game is a lot. {{TEAM}} just gave everyone their first real look, and it was not the one anybody wanted.

How this was computed Games and closing spreads are from the public nflverse dataset, seasons 2002–2025 — 2002 is when the league reached 32 teams and eight divisions. Both teams in every opener are counted, ties dropped. “Reached the playoffs” means appearing in any postseason game that year, under whatever field size applied (12 teams through 2019, 14 from 2020). One honest limitation: the control is binary — favourite or underdog — not the size of the spread, so a team laying one point and a team laying ten are pooled together. A finer control could shrink the gap somewhat. It would have to shrink it a long way to change the conclusion.
