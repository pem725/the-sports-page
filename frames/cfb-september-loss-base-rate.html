No. {{RANK}} {{TEAM}} lost to {{OPPONENT}}, {{SCORE}}. Within an hour somebody will have written that the season is over, and within a day somebody will have built a playoff bracket that does not include them.

That reflex was correct for a decade. It is not correct now, and the change is bigger than almost anyone has adjusted to.

What a September Loss Used To Cost

Take every team that appeared in the AP top ten during August or September and then lost a game in that same stretch. Across the College Football Playoff era — 2014 through 2025, setting aside the shortened 2020 season — that is 64 teams.

Seven of them reached the Playoff.

But the aggregate hides the whole story, because the format changed underneath it. Under the four-team field, from 2014 to 2023, 48 ranked teams lost early and exactly two made the Playoff — Ohio State in 2014 and Alabama in 2015. That is a 4% survival rate. The reflex was right: one September loss really did end it.

Then the field went to twelve.

The loss did not get less damaging. The bracket got more forgiving. Those are different things, and only one of them is about {{TEAM}}.

— The Sports Page

Notre Dame Is the Proof

In 2024 the Irish lost at home to Northern Illinois in the second week of the season — as bad a September result as a contender can produce — and reached the Playoff anyway. In 2025 they lost early again and reached it again. Two seasons, two early losses, two Playoff appearances.

Under the old format neither of those teams would have been within reach. The team did not change. The denominator did.

What To Take Home

The mistake here is not optimism or pessimism. It is using a base ratei that expired. “One loss and you are done” was a true, well-earned, evidence-backed rule — for exactly ten seasons. Then the structure it described was replaced, and the rule outlived the world that produced it.

This happens constantly outside sports. A hiring heuristic that worked when the applicant pool was 40 people. A budgeting rule from before the interest rate moved. A safety margin calibrated on an old failure rate. The rule keeps getting applied with total confidence long after the thing it was measured against has gone.

So when someone tells you what a result means, the useful question is not whether they are right. It is when were they last right, and has anything changed since? For {{TEAM}}, something did, and it changed two years ago.

How this was computed Rankings and results are from CollegeFootballData, covering 2014–2025 with the shortened 2020 season excluded. The group is every school that appeared in the AP top ten during any of the first six ranking weeks and lost a game dated in August or September of that season. “Reached the Playoff” means appearing in the final Playoff Committee Rankings inside the field of the day — top four through 2023, top twelve from 2024. The two eras are reported separately on purpose: pooling them produces an 11% figure that describes neither. Full dataset at data/cfb-september-loss-vs-playoff.json.
