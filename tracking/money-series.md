# The money series — economics, probability, and what things actually cost

Commissioned by the editor, 2026-09-25: *"Let's put together pieces that meld
economics, financial literacy, and probability. Sports numbers."*

The thesis, in one line: **sport is where people already do arithmetic about
money, so it is the cheapest place to teach them to do it well.** Every piece
here takes a number a fan already half-knows and calibrates it against something
from their own life.

`#166 The Cost of Fandom` is the first and is queued for 1 October.

---

## The rule this series runs on

**Calibrate to a thing the reader owns.** A percentage is forgettable; a
percentage expressed as *what this costs you over the years you will actually
live* is not. The editor's framing is the standard: a hundred dollars a week is
real to people in a way that "4.545% of handle" never will be.

**And never conclude "therefore don't."** The point is not temperance. A fan who
prices the entertainment honestly and keeps buying it has done the thing we
wanted. `#166` says so explicitly, and every piece here must.

## Commissioned, not yet written

### 2. How to Lie With a Winning Percentage
The editor's request, and the spine of the series. Take one real betting or
tipster advertisement and reconstruct exactly how its number was made true: the
start date chosen after the fact, the losses classified as "unofficial," the
record counted in units rather than dollars, the sample stopped at a high point.

**Done:** every technique named, with a worked example the reader can run. The
companion primer is `post-hoc`, already published.

**Trap:** do not invent an advertisement to knock down. Use a real one, quote it
briefly and fairly, or build the example from published tout records and say that
is what you did. A straw man here would be the exact sin the piece is about.

### 3. The Lottery, Priced Honestly — DONE, 3 Oct
The editor's own framing: what if the money that goes into lotteries went into
rewarding people for rising to their potential. Start with the arithmetic — a
ticket's expected value, the share that reaches education in a given state, and
what the same weekly spend does compounded over a working life.

**Trap, and it is serious:** lottery play is concentrated among people with the
least money, and a piece that reads as a lecture to the poor about their choices
is worse than not writing it. The subject is the *institution* that sells a
negative-expectation product and calls the proceeds education funding. Aim there.

### 4. Inversion: What Would Guarantee I Lose?
Munger's method applied to a fan's decisions. Rather than asking how to win a
pool, ask what would reliably lose one, then avoid those. It is the same move as
our own finding that copying the room is the worst available strategy.

**Done:** the inversion framed as a usable habit, with the pool simulation as the
worked example. Reference Munger's idea; do not reproduce his text.

### 5. The Subscription Stack
What following one team actually costs in a year: the packages, the streaming
tiers, the out-of-market fee, the parking. Then the same number compounded.

**Source:** this needs real published prices, gathered and dated, not recalled.
**Trap:** prices change and vary by market. Date every figure in the piece and say
which market it is for.

### 6. Expected Value Is Not a Promise
The concept underneath the whole series. A positive expected value can still ruin
you if the variance is large enough and you only get one run at it — which is
exactly why insurance exists and is rational despite its negative expected value.

**Done:** a concept primer, registered, with `#166` and the lottery piece pointing
at it. This is the piece that stops the series being simply "invest instead."

## The board member to use

Patrick jr works at a brokerage and sits on the editorial board. He is the right
reader for every draft in this series before it runs — not for the arithmetic,
which we can check, but for whether it would embarrass somebody who does this
professionally.

**One hard line:** nothing in this series is personalized investment advice, and
no piece may recommend a security, a product or an allocation. We describe what
happened to a broad index over real history and we state our limits. That is
journalism. Anything that tells a reader what *they* should buy is not, and we
are not licensed to write it.
