1. "DEFENSE IS EASIER TO FIX" IS ABOUT OUR RULER, NOT ABOUT FOOTBALL

Question: which is easier to move by a full standard deviation, your
offense or your defense?

The obvious answer looks right at first. Over 861 team-seasons, 1999
to 2025, how much of last season carries into this one:

   offense   .42
   defense   .30

Defense scatters more, so defense must be easier to change. That is
what everybody says and it is basically wrong.

Here is the catch. A number can fail to repeat for two completely
different reasons: the thing really changed, or we never measured it
properly to begin with. So we measured the ruler — split each season
into odd and even games and asked whether the two halves agree.

   how reliably we measure offense   .72
   how reliably we measure defense   .55

We measure defense much worse. A defense's numbers depend on which
offenses it happened to draw and on game script; leading teams face
passes, trailing teams face runs. Correct for that and the gap
essentially disappears. On yards per play it actually flips.

So: defense is not easier to fix. Defense is harder to see. Both
sides move a full standard deviation about a third of the time —
offense 33%, defense 37%.

I think this is an issue. It reframes a thing every broadcast says.

2. THE MOST LIKELY OUTCOME OF THROWING AT A RECEIVER IS NOTHING

Pem's point was that a slugger's likeliest single outcome is a
strikeout, and that batting average hides it. Football has the exact
same hole, and it is worse.

If you count only catches, a receiver looks like a 9-yard player.
But an incompletion is a play that happened — a down your team spent
on him and got nothing for. Count every ball thrown his way:

   174,738 targets, 2015–2024
   most common outcome: ZERO yards, 34.9% of the time

A third of the time you throw at a guy, you get nothing. That is the
strikeout, and no receiving statistic on any broadcast shows it.

Running backs, same treatment:

   mean 4.46   median 3.0   most common carry 2

"He averages four and a half a carry" describes a carry that mostly
does not happen. The typical one is three. The single most common one
is two.

3. WHAT WE WENT LOOKING FOR AND DID NOT FIND

The tempting next step: claim the median is the better statistic.
We tested it — does this year's median predict next year better than
this year's mean?

The point estimates said yes for receivers and no for running backs,
which even has a nice mechanical story (a back's long runs are his
own burst and repeat; a receiver's come from blown coverage and do
not).

The error bars say we have not shown anything. Both straddle zero.
On this evidence neither number predicts next season better than the
other, and the tidy flip is the kind of pattern a sample this size
coughs up on its own.

So we are not running that. The shape of the distributions is solid
and publishable. The "median is better" claim is not, yet.

One thing that is settled: they are not rivals. Mean × touches =
total yards, and medians do not add up to anything. The mean tells
you what a player contributed. The median tells you what the next
play brings. The mistake is using the mean to answer the median's
question — which is what the broadcast does every time.

WHAT I WANT FROM YOU

a) Which of the two leads? My vote is the defense one, because it
   corrects something people say out loud every Sunday.

b) The receiver zero — is "a third of throws at him gain nothing"
   the hook, or is it "your best receiver's most likely play is
   nothing"? Second is punchier, first is safer.

c) Sacks. We deliberately charged them to nobody. Pem is right that
   a blown block leading to negative yards belongs to the line, but
   public play-by-play cannot tell a blown block from a receiver who
   never got open. Do we say that plainly in the piece, or leave it
   out? I lean toward saying it — "here is a thing the data cannot
   do" is our kind of paragraph.

d) Anybody object to the target-not-catch framing? It is the whole
   finding and it is a choice, so somebody should push on it.
