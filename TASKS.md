# Work queue

For whoever picks it up next — Codex, Claude, or a person. Each task states the
question, where the data is, what "done" looks like, and the trap that is waiting
in it. **Read the trap before starting.** Most of them are recorded because we
already fell in once.

House rules that apply to everything here: `CLAUDE.md` / `AGENTS.md` for the
publishing workflow, the editorial rules, and the secret handling. Every piece
still has to pass `check_readability.py`, `check_voice.py` and
`check_headline.py`, and still has to state its limits.

**The standing rule that matters most:** when a check disagrees with the data,
suspect the check first. It has now been the check **seven** times running — the
headline selector, the digit-blind number test, the confession exception, the
rotation boundary, the OG entity pattern, the missing possessive pronouns in
`PLAIN`, and the digit-blind number test *again*, in `check_readability.py`.

That last one is the tell. The same bug existed in two files and was fixed in
only one, because nobody grepped for the pattern after fixing it the first time.
**When you fix a checker, search the other checkers for the same mistake.**

And one the checks could not catch at all: a figure's collision test compared
text to text and passed, while a leader line ran straight through a word. Bounding
boxes only find what you think to compare. **Render it and look at it.**

## B. Data we do not have and should

### B1. Pre-1999 NFL scores
The win-probability figure stops at 1999 because every source we use does.
Checked and ruled out: nflverse play-by-play, the nflverse games release,
nfldata/habitatring `games.csv`, ESPN's scoreboard API (403 on all dates
including a current control, so throttling rather than a gap).

Pro-Football-Reference has it back to 1920 and its terms do not permit scraping.

**Done:** either a licensed or openly-licensed source identified with its terms
quoted, or a written conclusion that none exists and the 1999 floor is permanent.
**Do not scrape PFR.** A negative answer, properly evidenced, closes this
honestly and is worth as much as a positive one.

### B2. College football has no broadcast-stat equivalent
`data/nfl/broadcast-stats.csv` ranks what a booth cites for the NFL — third down,
possession, first downs, explosive plays — and eight of twelve turned out to add
nothing once you know the score. **We have never run this for college**, which is
the sport this paper covers most.

**Source:** CFBD `/games/teams` gives per-game box scores and is fast, about 0.4s
a week. `scripts/build_nfl_broadcast_stats.py` is the pattern to copy.

**Done:** the same table for CFB, and an honest statement of whether the ranking
matches the NFL one. If it does not, that is the more interesting result.

**Trap:** college has FCS opponents. A game against an unrated team must be
excluded, not averaged in — see `nd_matchup.py`, which prints UNRATEABLE rather
than inventing a projection.

### B3. In-game state
Everything we have is end-of-game. We cannot answer *"down 28 at half, what are
the odds?"* — which the editorial board asked for directly — because we only
store final scores.

**Source:** nflverse play-by-play already carries `qtr`, `game_seconds_remaining`,
`score_differential`. `scripts/build_nfl_history.py` streams it and discards this.

**Done:** a team-game-state table, and then the comeback question: how often has a
team trailed by four scores at half and won, and is it genuinely more common now
than in the 1990s? The board believes it is. Find out.

**Trap:** conditioning on half-time state selects for teams that were losing,
which correlates with being worse. The honest comparison holds team strength
fixed, or says plainly that it does not.

### B4. Penalties before 2016 — and whether they explain 2019
`data/nfl/broadcast-stats.csv` carries `penalties` and `penalty_yards`, but only
back to **2016**. `data/nfl/team-games.csv` carries `home` back to **1999**. So we
can see that home-field advantage collapsed in 2019 and we have exactly three
seasons of penalty data before it did. That is not enough to say anything.

**The question is a mechanism test for a finding we already published.** Issue
#157 showed home-field advantage vanished in 2019, *before* the crowds left in
2020. If the referee-bias story is right, the home penalty edge should collapse on
the same schedule. If it does not, the story is wrong and we should say so.

**Source:** nflverse play-by-play, `penalty_team`.
`scripts/build_nfl_broadcast_stats.py` is the pattern — extend its season range
from 2016 back to 1999.

**Done:** home minus away penalty yards per season, 1999–2025, with intervals,
plotted against the home-field curve from #157.

**Traps, two, and the first has already bitten this repo.** `penalty_team` is null
on declined and offsetting penalties, so a naive credit silently undercounts and
the undercount is not random. And **2020 is not the control you want** — the empty
stadiums came *after* the collapse, which is the entire point of #157. Do not let
the pandemic season absorb an explanation it cannot have caused.

## C. Analyses already specified, not yet built

### C1. "A stat is a gauge, not a target" — concept primer
The editor's framing, from the Sep 18 meeting: *"Your cholesterol is a biomarker.
It's not something you take drugs to control — it's a gauge. Now we're trying to
train for the gauge."* That is Goodhart's Law with a better analogy than Goodhart
had, and it is the spine underneath Issues #158 and #161 without either naming it.

**Done:** a concept primer, registered, with those two issues pointing at it.

### C2. The running back median
Total rushing yards is confounded with carries, so a grinder and an explosive back
read the same. The editor proposed **median yards per carry** as the honest
alternative.

**Source:** nflverse play-by-play has every carry.

**Done:** the distribution of carries per back, median against total, and the
backs whose reputation the two measures disagree about most. Those disagreements
are the piece.

### C3. Catch rate, and what counts as catchable
Raised at the same meeting and genuinely hard: *"If a quarterback overthrows a
receiver, does the receiver get credit for a catchable ball?"*

**Done:** catch rate by receiver, and an honest account of what the public data
can and cannot separate. If "catchable" is not in the data, say so — that is a
finding about the data, and this paper publishes those.

### C4. Our own calibration curve
`data/picks-ledger.json` records every pool pick with the model's probability. By
November there will be enough resolved games to draw a calibration curve of our
own published forecasts.

**Done:** predicted probability against observed frequency, by band, with the
sample size on every point. **Do not publish before ~25 resolved games per band**
— the ledger's own report says so and it is right.

### C5. Garbage time contaminates every box-score number we have
`data/nfl/broadcast-stats.csv` counts every snap, including the ones taken with
four minutes left and a 24-point gap. Those snaps are played by different people
under different rules of engagement, and they are a large share of the season.

**The question:** do the four stats that survived in Issue #161 — yards per play,
explosive rate, completion percentage, total yards — still survive once blowout
snaps are removed? If the ranking changes, #161 needs a follow-up saying so.

**Source:** nflverse play-by-play has `score_differential` and
`game_seconds_remaining`. `scripts/rank_broadcast_stats.py` holds the partial
correlation machinery; the filter goes upstream of it.

**Done:** the same raw-versus-partial table, computed on competitive snaps only,
beside the existing one.

**Traps.** "Competitive" is a knob, not a fact — report it at three thresholds and
show that the conclusion survives all three, or report honestly that it does not.
And the filter is not neutral: it removes more snaps from teams that get blown
out, so the surviving sample is tilted toward good teams. Say so, or hold strength
fixed.

### C6. Our yards-per-play numbers do not adjust for opponent
`scripts/picks_overlay.py` prints season-to-date yards per play straight. A team
that has played three overmatched opponents looks elite, and in September nearly
everyone has. This is the most-used number in the pipeline and it is raw.

**The question:** does opponent adjustment move the 50.2% against-the-spread
result, or is the market already pricing the adjustment too?

**Done:** an iterative opponent adjustment on `ypp_margin`, then the 2024–25 ATS
backtest re-run against it. **Report by week**, not as one number — the
adjustment is unstable when everyone has played twice, and the useful finding is
*when in the season it starts to help*, if it ever does.

**Traps.** FCS opponents are unrated and must be excluded rather than scored as
zero — `nd_matchup.py` prints UNRATEABLE instead of inventing a number, and that
is the standard. And the honest null here is the raw version: if adjusted does not
beat raw, print that, because "we built a more sophisticated thing that did not
help" is a publishable result in this paper.

## E. What today exposed (2026-09-23)

### E1. The checkers share bugs, and nobody has ever grepped for that
`check_readability.py` tested for a number with `re.search(r"\d")` — the exact
digit-blind bug `check_headline.py` had already been fixed for. Two files, one
mistake, fixed in one place, because after the first fix nobody looked next door.

**Done:** a written cross-audit of all six `check_*.py` scripts. For each core
test — how it finds the headline, how it finds a number, how it finds the deck,
what it treats as a sentence, what it skips inside `<style>` and `<script>` — say
whether the other checkers do the same thing the same way, and fix the ones that
are wrong.

**Traps.** Do not grep for literal strings; the same mistake can be spelled
differently. And **measure the corpus before and after every fix**: extending
`PLAIN` today moved the flag count 43 → 39, which is what a targeted fix looks
like. A fix that flips half the archive is not a fix, it is a checker being
quietly disabled.

### E2. "Compared to what?" — audit the archive for missing baselines
Issue #182 exists because a rule that gained eleven points turned out to lose to
a hat. Nobody caught it for a day, and the reason is that we never asked what the
comparison was. That question applies to everything we have published.

**Done:** a pass over `published/*.html` recording, for each headline claim,
whether the piece names what it is being compared to. Then the list of the worst
offenders, and a recommendation on whether this becomes a seventh checker.

**Traps, and this one needs a human.** A baseline can be implicit and perfectly
sound — *"wins 29 of 100 over a season, 48 in one game"* names its comparison
without ever using the word. A regex will over-flag badly, so **hand-verify a
sample of twenty before you trust any count**, and report the false-positive rate
with the result. And do not edit published pieces off the back of this; bring the
list to the editor. What we correct in public is an editorial decision, not
cleanup.
