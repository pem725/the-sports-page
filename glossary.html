What Did the Announcer Just Say?

Every term here gets three things: what it means, why it is called that when we know, and what to watch for on TV. The stats also get their blind spot, because every number leaves something out.

#Line of scrimmagefootball

The invisible line across the field where each play starts. Nobody may cross it until the ball is snapped.

Why it is called that "Scrimmage" is an old form of the word skirmish, a small fight.

What to watch for TV paints it as a blue line. Watch the two lines of big men crash together right on it.

#Down and distancefootball

The offense gets four tries, called downs, to gain 10 yards. "Third and 4" means it is the third try and 4 yards are still needed.

What to watch for The yellow line on TV marks the 10 yards. Third down is the moment that decides most drives.

#Moving the chainsfootball

Making a first down. The offense earns four new tries.

Why it is called that A sideline crew holds two poles joined by a chain exactly 10 yards long. When the offense gains 10, the crew picks up the chain and moves it.

What to watch for When a play is close, the crew jogs the chain onto the field to measure. Fans love it; it is a real ruler.

#Red zonefootball

The last 20 yards before the end zone. Teams that score touchdowns here, not field goals, usually win.

What to watch for The field gets crowded, so passes get harder. Count how many red-zone trips end in 7 points, not 3.

#Two-minute drillfootball

A fast, hurry-up offense at the end of a half. The team throws to the sidelines and runs out of bounds to stop the clock.

Why it is called that In the NFL the clock stops for a "two-minute warning" with two minutes left in each half. College football has no such warning.

What to watch for Watch the clock and the timeouts in the corner of the screen. Smart teams save one.

#Pocket ("a clean pocket")football

The space the blockers make around the quarterback so he can throw. A clean pocket means nobody is close to him.

Why it is called that The blockers form a cup shape around the passer, like a pocket.

What to watch for Watch the quarterback, not the ball. If he has time to look twice, the blockers are winning.

#Sackfootball

Tackling the quarterback behind the line of scrimmage before he throws.

Why it is called that Hall of Fame pass rusher Deacon Jones is usually credited with naming it, after the idea of sacking a city.

What to watch for A sack on third down usually kills the drive and forces a punt.

#Shotgunfootball

A formation where the quarterback stands several yards behind the center and catches a short toss instead of a hand-to-hand snap.

What to watch for Shotgun gives the quarterback a head start on seeing the defense. Many teams use it on almost every passing down.

#Under centerfootball

The quarterback stands right behind the center and takes the ball hand to hand.

What to watch for It hides whether a run or a pass is coming, which helps fakes. Watch for it near the goal line.

#Play-actionfootball

The quarterback fakes a handoff, then keeps the ball and throws. The fake pulls defenders toward the line and opens space behind them.

Why it is called that The "action" is the motion of a running play, used as a decoy.

What to watch for Watch the linebackers. If they step forward on the fake, look deep: someone is open.

#RPO (run-pass option)football

A play where the quarterback decides after the snap. He watches one defender: if that player comes forward to stop the run, he throws; if not, he hands off.

Why it is called that RPO is short for run-pass option.

What to watch for Watch the quarterback's eyes right after the snap. He is reading a single defender, not the whole field.

#Audiblefootball

Changing the play at the line of scrimmage after seeing the defense.

Why it is called that It is called out loud, so it is audible: able to be heard.

What to watch for When the quarterback points and shouts before the snap, he may be switching the play. Or faking it.

#Blitzfootball

Sending extra defenders, not just the linemen, to rush the quarterback. It is a gamble: more pressure, but fewer players covering receivers.

Why it is called that From the German Blitz, lightning.

What to watch for If the quarterback gets the ball out fast against a blitz, there is often a big hole behind it.

#Man-to-man coveragefootball

Each defender is assigned one receiver and follows him all over the field.

What to watch for Watch a cornerback run step for step with a receiver. In man, defenders often have their backs to the quarterback.

#Zone coveragefootball

Each defender guards an area of the field, not a person. Receivers pass from one defender's zone to the next.

What to watch for In zone, defenders face the quarterback and watch his eyes. The open spots are the seams between zones.

#Cover 2football

A zone defense with two safeties deep, each guarding half the field. Five others cover the short areas.

Why it is called that The number counts the deep defenders: two of them, each with a half. The same logic names Cover 1, Cover 3 and Cover 4.

What to watch for Look before the snap for two safeties standing far back, side by side. The weak spot is the deep middle, between them.

#Cover 3football

A zone defense with three players deep, each guarding a third of the field: two cornerbacks and one safety.

Why it is called that Three deep defenders, so "3".

What to watch for One safety deep in the middle is the tip-off. Short passes near the sideline are often open.

#Nickel (and dime)football

Nickel means the defense uses five defensive backs instead of the usual four, to cover more receivers. Dime means six.

Why it is called that A nickel is five cents, so the fifth back is the nickel. Two nickels make a dime, so six backs is a dime.

What to watch for When the offense spreads out with three or four receivers, the defense usually answers with nickel.

#Field goal ("take the points")football

Kicking the ball through the uprights for 3 points. "Taking the points" means kicking on fourth down instead of trying for a first down.

What to watch for This is where coaches get second-guessed. A touchdown is worth 7 with the extra point, so settling for 3 near the goal line is a real cost.

#Going for it on fourth downfootball

Trying for a first down on the last try instead of punting or kicking. If it fails, the other team gets the ball right there.

What to watch for Teams go for it more than they used to, because the math often favors it. Listen for the announcers to argue about it.

#Turnover (interception, fumble)football

Losing the ball to the other team. An interception is a pass caught by the defense; a fumble is a dropped ball the defense picks up.

What to watch for Turnovers swing games more than almost anything else. A "pick-six" is an interception returned for a touchdown.

#Two-point conversionfootball

After a touchdown, a team can try one play from close range for 2 points instead of kicking for 1.

What to watch for The NFL snaps it from the 2-yard line; college from the 3. Late in close games, watch the scoreboard math decide which one a coach picks.

#Safety (the score)football

When the offense is tackled in its own end zone, the defense scores 2 points and then gets the ball back too.

What to watch for Rare, and a double blow: two points and a free possession. Do not confuse it with the safety, the defensive position.

#Holdingfootball

Grabbing an opponent to stop him, instead of blocking him. Offensive holding costs 10 yards.

What to watch for Look for a yellow flag after a big run. Replays often show a lineman hugging a defender.

#Pass interferencefootball

Illegally blocking a player from catching a pass while the ball is in the air.

What to watch for One of the most costly flags. In the NFL, defensive interference puts the ball where the foul happened, which can be 40 yards.

#Hail Maryfootball

A long, desperate pass into a crowd in the end zone at the end of a half.

Why it is called that After a 1975 playoff win, Dallas quarterback Roger Staubach said he closed his eyes and said a Hail Mary as he threw. The name stuck.

What to watch for Watch the tall receivers jump in a group. Most of these fall incomplete; that is why they are famous when they work.

#RBI (runs batted in)baseball

Counts runs that score because of a batter's hit, walk, sacrifice or out. A home run with two on base is 3 RBI.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: rewards driving runners home. Its blind spot: it depends on teammates getting on base first. A great hitter behind weak hitters piles up few RBI.

#Batting averagebaseball

Hits divided by at-bats. A .300 hitter gets a hit about 3 times in 10.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: measures how often a batter gets a hit. Its blind spot: it ignores walks, and it counts a single the same as a home run.

#On-base percentage (OBP)baseball

How often a batter reaches base, by hit, walk or being hit by a pitch.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: measures not making an out, which is the hitter's main job. Its blind spot: it treats a walk the same as a home run.

#Slugging percentagebaseball

Total bases per at-bat: a single counts 1, a double 2, a triple 3, a home run 4.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: measures power. Its blind spot: it ignores walks completely.

#OPS (on-base plus slugging)baseball

On-base percentage plus slugging percentage, added together into one number.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: captures getting on base and hitting for power in a single figure. Its blind spot: it adds two numbers measured on different scales, and does not adjust for ballpark.

#ERA (earned run average)baseball

Earned runs a pitcher allows per nine innings. Earned runs are those that would have scored without fielding errors.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: measures run prevention. Its blind spot: it also measures the fielders behind the pitcher, and early in a season a single bad inning can wreck it.

#WHIP (walks plus hits per inning pitched)baseball

Walks plus hits allowed, divided by innings. It counts the baserunners a pitcher lets on.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: shows how much traffic a pitcher allows. Its blind spot: a single counts the same as a home run.

#Strikeout (K)baseball

Three strikes and the batter is out.

Why it is called that Scorekeepers write it as K. The usual story credits early baseball writer Henry Chadwick, who used the last letter of "struck."

What to watch for Fans hang K signs in the stands to count them. A backward K means the batter watched strike three go by.

#Walk (BB)baseball

Four pitches outside the strike zone, and the batter goes to first base free.

Why it is called that BB stands for "base on balls," since a pitch outside the zone is called a ball.

What to watch for A walk to lead off an inning tends to cause trouble for the pitcher. Watch what happens next.

#Savebaseball

Credit for a relief pitcher who finishes a win while protecting a close lead: usually entering with a lead of 3 runs or fewer and getting at least one inning's worth of outs.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: marks a pitcher who closed out a close win. Its blind spot: a 3-run lead in the ninth is easy, while the hardest jams often come earlier and earn nothing.

#Quality startbaseball

A start in which the pitcher goes at least six innings and allows three earned runs or fewer.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: marks a start that gave the team a fair chance. Its blind spot: 6 innings and 3 runs is a 4.50 ERA, not great, and it counts the same as 8 shutout innings.

#WAR (wins above replacement)baseball

An estimate of how many wins a player adds compared with a cheap replacement from the minor leagues.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: puts hitting, fielding and pitching on one scale. Its blind spot: it is a model, not a count. The two popular versions (Baseball-Reference and FanGraphs) often disagree, and small gaps between players mean little.

#Earned run vs. unearned runbaseball

An earned run is charged to the pitcher. An unearned run scores because of a fielding error, so it does not count against his ERA.

What to watch for When the shortstop boots a grounder, watch the scoreboard: the runs that follow may not count against the pitcher.

#Pitch countbaseball

How many pitches a pitcher has thrown in a game. Teams use it to protect arms from overuse.

What to watch for TV shows it on screen. When it climbs, watch the bullpen start warming up.

#Run differentialbaseball

Runs a team scored minus runs it allowed over a season.

The stat, and its blind spot What it does: it is often a better guide to a team's real strength than its win-loss record, because close games are partly luck. Its blind spot: one blowout can bend it.
