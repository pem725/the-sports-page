The Mets Had the Third-Best Bullpen in Baseball. Nobody in Baseball Had
Fewer Leads to Protect.
A 3.1 ERA out of the pen — third of all thirty clubs. Thirty-two save opportunities — last of all thirty clubs. At the deadline the Mets sold the three arms at the center of it, and that was the correct thing to do, because a great bullpen on a 50-win team is the least useful good thing in sports.

The New York Mets are 50–66. They are 20 games out of first place and eleven and a half out of a wild card, and at Monday's trade deadline they sold nine major leaguers.
Three of them were relievers: Luke Weaver, a 1.8 ERA and a 0.82 WHIP; Brooks Raley, a 2.0; Huascar Brazobán, a 2.6. Together they threw 138 innings at a
2.2 ERA
. That is not the bullpen of a last-place team. That is the back end of a contender.
And it is not a fluke of three names. Counting only pitchers who never started a game, the Mets bullpen posted a
3.1 ERA — third-best in Major League Baseball
, behind San Diego and the Yankees and ahead of Toronto, Atlanta, and Boston.
So here is the question. How does a team with the third-best bullpen in baseball lose 66 games by the first week of August?
What a Save Opportunity Actually Counts
A relief pitcher converts skill into wins only under a specific condition: somebody has to hand him a lead worth protecting. Strike out the side in a 9–1 loss and you have done something impressive and worth nothing.
Baseball already counts the condition. A save opportunity is roughly a late-inning appearance with a lead of three runs or fewer — a game close enough that the bullpen can decide it. It is a clean measure of
how often the relief corps mattered at all.
The Mets had
32 of them through deadline day.
That is the fewest in Major League Baseball. Washington, at the other end, had 60.
Look at the clubs the Mets share the bullpen leaderboard with. San Diego got 49 chances. The Yankees got 52. Toronto 48, Atlanta 49, Boston 42, Milwaukee 49. Every other elite bullpen in the sport was handed somewhere between 42 and 58 games to close.
The Mets were handed 32.
The Outlier
Plot every team by how good its relievers were and how often they got to prove it, and the Mets do not sit in the cloud with anyone. They sit alone.

Raley went to Philadelphia, a division rival holding a wild card spot — a club that will hand him the late innings of games that matter, which is the one thing New York could not do. That is not a mistake. That is the entire point. He is worth more there.
One more thing, because it is going to come up. In the three games since the sale the Mets have won all three, scoring six, six, and thirteen runs on Cleveland. Somebody will read that as proof the bullpen never mattered. It is proof of nothing — three games is
noise, not signal
i
, and three games is precisely the sample size that fools people. What it does gently confirm is that relief pitching was never the broken thing here. A team that puts up six, six, and thirteen does not need saving. It needed those innings back in April, when it was losing games one run at a time.
What To Take Home
The instinct that a 1.8 ERA is a 1.8 ERA wherever it happens is the thing to give up here. It is a rate — a per-inning quality — and a rate only becomes value when you multiply it by the innings that decide something. The Mets had a superb numerator and almost no denominator to put it over.
This is why the
unit of analysis
i
matters more than the rate does. “Third-best bullpen” is measured per inning pitched. “Wins” is measured per game that was still in doubt. Those are different denominators, and the Mets sat at opposite ends of the two.
Carry the habit past baseball. Any time you meet an impressive rate — a conversion percentage, a success rate, a batting average, a hit rate on a sales team — the number is only half the claim. Ask the second question:
how many times did it get to happen, and did those times matter?
Excellence that never meets a decisive moment is a fact about a person, not a fact about an outcome.
The Mets had one of the best bullpens in baseball. It is the most expensive way there is to learn that being good is not the same as being useful.
How this was computed
Player and team lines are from the MLB Stats API, 2026 season through August 3. “Bullpen” here means pitchers with zero games started — an unambiguous rule applied identically to all 30 clubs. That definition matters more than it looks: widen it to include swingmen who both started and relieved, and the Mets bullpen falls from 3rd to 12th, because Sean Manaea (9 starts, 4.4) and Tobias Myers (6.3) come in with it. Both figures are honest; they answer different questions. The 3rd-place number is the right one for “how good were the arms that only ever pitched in relief,” which is the group that was traded. Save opportunities are as recorded by MLB and are a proxy for high-leverage usage, not a perfect measure of it — a bullpen can also enter close games while behind or tied, which no save statistic counts. One last caution, and it is the reason every figure here is anchored to deadline day: if you run this query yourself now, you will not get our number. You will get a Mets bullpen around 3.3, ranked fourth. That is because the league's own database credits a traded pitcher's season line to his
current
club, so the innings Raley threw in Queens now sit under Philadelphia and Brazobán's sit under Chicago. The roster moved and the history moved with it. Ours is the bullpen the Mets actually ran out there — which is the only version that can answer what they gave up.
