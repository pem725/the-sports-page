A Decimal Is a Promise.
Don’t Make One You Can’t Keep.
A projection of 81.4 wins is not more honest than “about 81.” It is less. The extra digit claims a confidence the math does not have. Communicating uncertainty is the discipline of reporting exactly as much as you know — and not a decimal more.
Tier 1 · The Two-Minute Version
The number and your confidence are two different things.
Here are two ways to tell you the same forecast. One:
“81.4 wins.”
Two:
“somewhere around 76 to 86, best guess 81.”
The first sounds sharper, more expert, more like the person has done the work. The second is the one telling the truth.
A number carries a promise. Report only as much precision as the math has earned.
A number carries a hidden promise about how sure you are — its digits
are
the confidence claim (that idea has its own primer,
Precision vs. Accuracy
). “81.4” promises you can pin the season to a tenth of a win. Nobody can. So the whole skill of communicating uncertainty comes down to one habit:
match the digits to your confidence.
If you only know the win total within five games either way, you say “mid-80s,” not 81.4. The range is not you being wishy-washy. The range is the actual answer.
Tier 2 · If You Want to Go Deeper
Two ways to lie about what you know.
There are two opposite failures, and honest forecasting threads between them.
The first is
false precision
— over-claiming. The championship odds reported as 23.6%. The live win-probability that ticks to the tenth of a percent. The projection of 81.4 wins. Each is a quantity that is genuinely uncertain, dressed up in the costume of a quantity that is exactly known. It feels rigorous. It is the opposite: it is hiding the uncertainty instead of reporting it.
The second failure is the one people reach for when they get scolded about the first:
useless hedging
— under-claiming. “Anything can happen.” “It’s a coin flip.” “On any given Sunday.” Refusing to commit sounds humble, but when a game is
not
a coin flip, calling it one throws away real information. A forecast that says nothing cannot be wrong, which is exactly why it is worthless.
The honest middle is a
best guess plus an honest range
: “76 to 86, most likely 81.” “About a one-in-four chance.” “A slight favorite, maybe 60-40.” Each says two things at once — what you believe,
and
how firmly. That second half is not an apology attached to the forecast. It
is
the forecast. A projection reported without its uncertainty is not a brave, clean number; it is an unfinished one.
