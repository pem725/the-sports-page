The Deepest Wound in Sports
Is Always the Same Shape.
A last-second field goal against the number-one team. A coin toss in overtime, and your offense never touches the ball. One strike away from the title — twice — and then not. These wounds belong to different fans, different sports, different decades. Put them under the math and they are the identical injury, inflicted at the identical spot.

Ask any fan for the worst moment of their rooting life and notice what they never say. They never name a blowout. Nobody’s deepest scar is a 42–14 loss they saw coming from the second quarter. The wound that lasts is the other kind: the game their team was winning, was supposed to win, had all but won — and then, in a single instant at the very top, didn’t. The shape is so consistent it is almost a law. And it is one.
Three wounds, one wound
Consider three, chosen because they are famous enough to need no introduction and because they could not appear more different.
The moment
What happened
Nov. 20, 1993
Boston College 41, Notre Dame 39
The number-one Fighting Irish, a week after beating the number-one team in the country, fell behind Boston College by three scores, roared back to lead 39–38 with 1:09 to play — and watched a 41-yard field goal split the posts as time expired. The national title went with it.
Jan. 23, 2022
Chiefs 42, Bills 36 (OT)
Buffalo scored to lead by three with thirteen seconds left. Thirteen seconds was enough for Kansas City to kick the tying field goal, win the overtime coin toss, and score — while a quarterback having the game of his life stood on the sideline and never touched the ball again.
Oct. 27, 2011
World Series, Game 6
The Texas Rangers, chasing the first championship in their history, came within one strike of it in the ninth inning. Then, in the tenth, within one strike again. Both times the last strike never came. St. Louis won that night, and the next.
A college kick, a professional coin toss, a bases-loaded count. Nothing links them on the surface. Underneath, they are the same event: a heavy favorite, standing at the highest-stakes moment its sport offers, losing by the thinnest margin that sport allows. That is not three kinds of heartbreak. It is one kind, three times.
Why the summit is where favorites fall
Here is the part that turns a sad coincidence into a law. A favorite’s advantage is not something it carries into any single moment — it is something that
accumulates
. Give a good team a whole season and its edge compounds across a hundred and sixty-two games until the standings are almost never wrong. But the edge grows only with the number of chances, while the
noise grows faster, relative to it, the fewer chances there are
i
. Cut the season down to a single game and most of the advantage evaporates. Cut it down to one kick, one drive, one swing — and it is nearly gone.
The numbers are unforgiving. A team good enough to win seventy-one percent of a full season — a juggernaut, a hundred-win pace — is, in a single sudden-death moment against a worthy opponent, barely better than a coin flip. Fifty-two percent. All that separation, earned over months, compressed into one instant where it counts for almost nothing.

So the cruelty is structural, not random. The championship moment is, by design, the place where the fewest chances decide the most — the single kick, the sudden-death possession, the final strike. It is the highest-
variance
i
instant in the entire season, arriving precisely when a favorite has the most to lose and the least protection. The better the team, the higher it climbs, the farther the accumulated hope has to fall — and it all gets decided by a flip the favorite was never much better than even to win.
The blowout you saw coming. The summit you didn’t. The wound is the exact height of the hope, dropped in a single instant.

Why it hurts more than losing ever should
There is a second reason these three moments outlast every ordinary defeat, and it is not about probability at all. The pain of a loss is not the loss. It is the distance between what you expected and what you got. A blowout hurts a little because you revised your hopes downward all afternoon; by the fourth quarter the disappointment was already priced in. The summit gives you no such mercy. Your hope is at its absolute maximum — you are a strike away, thirteen seconds from the whistle, one kick from the top — and the fall is the full height of it, all at once, with no warning. That gap between expectation and outcome is the
only thing a fan actually feels
i
, and the summit is engineered to make it as wide as it can possibly be.
Which is why the shape never changes. High hope, high stakes, lost by inches: it is the worst thing that can happen to a fan because it is the worst thing the mathematics can build — maximum variance at the maximum-expectation moment. Every fandom has its own version, and every version is the same version. The kick, the coin toss, the final strike. Different jerseys. One wound.
There is, of course, a mirror to all of this. If the deepest agony is the favorite losing the flip, then somewhere the sweetest joy is its exact reverse — the team nobody believed in, winning it. That is a piece for another day, and a happier one.
Notes & sources
The three games are on the record.
Boston College 41, Notre Dame 39
(Nov. 20, 1993, South Bend): No. 16 Boston College defeated the No. 1 Fighting Irish on David Gordon’s 41-yard field goal as time expired, one week after Notre Dame had beaten the No. 1–ranked team in the country.
Chiefs 42, Bills 36
(Jan. 23, 2022, AFC Divisional Round): Buffalo led by three with 0:13 left; Kansas City tied it, won the overtime coin toss, and scored a touchdown on the opening possession, so Buffalo’s offense never took the field in overtime — the game the NFL cited when it changed its postseason overtime rule that offseason.
2011 World Series, Game 6
(Oct. 27, 2011): Texas was one strike from its first title in the ninth inning and again in the tenth; St. Louis rallied both times and won Games 6 and 7.
The win-probability figures are a model, and a deliberately simple one. Treating each “chance” as an independent trial with a fixed edge, a team’s probability of winning scales with the square root of the number of trials: an edge calibrated to about 71% over a 162-game haul falls to roughly 52% in a single sudden-death moment. The exact percentages depend on the calibration; the direction — that fewer chances collapse a favorite toward the coin flip — is arithmetic, and is the same result behind the earlier issues on variance and on when to pull a hockey goalie. Real games add correlation and momentum this model ignores, which changes the numbers but not the shape.
