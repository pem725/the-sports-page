How Many Points Do You Need to Win?

Two questions on one pair of axes — with, it turns out, one answer. How many must you score — and how few must you allow? Every NFL season from 1999 to 2025.

1999
points you scored points you allowed where they cross 25–75%: the band of real doubt

How to read it. The blue line rises: the more you score, the more you win. The red line falls: the more you allow, the less you win. Both faint lines are 1999, held fixed so you can see the drift.

Why the two curves cross at 50%, and why that is not a finding. They have to. Every game in which one team scored 24 is the same game in which the other allowed 24 — so the chance of winning having allowed x points is, by construction, one minus the chance of winning having scored x. The two curves are mirror images through the halfway line, and in the data they sum to 1.000 at every score. The only slack is ties, which are 30 of 13,928 team-games. This page previously read that as evidence that offense and defense are worth the same. That was wrong — it is an identity, not a measurement, and it would hold in any league in any sport regardless of how the game were played. Corrected 25 September 2026, after a reader asked the obvious question we had not.

Is it ipsative? The outcome is. The performance is not. Worth separating, because the two get conflated constantly. A football result is zero-sum by the rules — across 6,964 complete games the two teams’ wins sum to exactly one, with 15 ties the only exception. That constraint is what the curves inherit, and it makes them ipsative in the strict sense: they correlate at −0.9999 and sum to 1.00.

But a team’s offense and defense are not ipsative at all. Over 861 team-seasons, points scored and points allowed correlate −0.15, and their sum runs from 462 to 1,011. Nothing forces a club to trade one for the other; plenty are good at both and plenty are bad at both. So the zero-sum lives in who won, never in how well anyone played.

One consequence, stated plainly: the red curve carries no information the blue one does not. It is the same measurement relabeled. Drawing both is a convenience — "how few must I allow" is a question people genuinely ask — but it is one finding shown twice, not two findings. The height of the crossing is forced; only its position along the bottom is measured.

What is actually empirical here is the shape and where it sits. The dots are the observed win rate in each band of points, with 95% intervals and a dot that grows with the number of games behind it; the smooth line is a fit to those dots and nothing more. Watch the whole thing slide right as the years run — the 50% point moves from 20.6 points in 1999 to 23.0 in 2025, tracking the league’s own scoring almost exactly.

And watch what does not change. The band where the game is genuinely in doubt — the gap between a 25% and a 75% chance — was 14.0 points wide in 1999 and 13.5 in 2025. Scoring rose by more than two points a game and the curve did not steepen at all. It moved. It kept its shape.

The band. The shaded strip runs from a 25% chance to a 75% chance. Outside it the game is mostly decided; inside it, the result is genuinely in doubt. Its width is the just-noticeable difference — how many points it takes to move a team from probably-losing to probably-winning.

It is about 13 points, and it has been for twenty-seven years: 14.0 in 1999, 13.5 in 2025, never outside 11.7 to 15.1. Two scores. That is the third thing on this chart that refuses to change — the shape does not move, the crossing sits on the league average, and the doubt is always two touchdowns wide.

Method. 13,928 team-games from nflverse play-by-play, 1999–2025. The points are observed win rates in eight bands of points scored, with Wilson 95% intervals — hover any dot for its denominator. The curves are two logistic fits per season, shown so the fit can be judged against the data rather than instead of it. Each gives the chance of winning having scored, or allowed, exactly that many points — not at least that many, which is a different and higher number. 1999 is as far back as the source reaches. thesportspage.net

How Good Do You Have To Be?

Winning a single game is the wrong target. All you have to do is make the playoffs — and the bar for that is lower than almost anyone guesses. Here is what it costs, in points, across every season since 1999.

Read the diagonals, not the axes. Both threshold lines run at forty-five degrees. That is the whole finding: move one point left and one point down and you stay exactly where you were. A point you prevent is worth a point you score, and the exchange rate is one–for–one.

Which matters more? Neither, and the margin of the result is almost comic. Predicting win rate from points scored and points allowed separately explains of the variation. Predicting it from the difference alone explains . Knowing how a club reached +7 — whether by scoring 31 and allowing 24, or scoring 17 and allowing 10 — buys you of R-squared.

So is defense compensatory? Completely. A stingy defense offsets a dull offense point for point, and a prolific offense offsets a leaky defense exactly as well. “Defense wins championships” is not wrong so much as not special — offense wins precisely as many. That is a claim this page could not honestly make from the curves above, where the crossing is an identity. It can make it here, because across these seasons a club’s points scored and points allowed correlate only −0.15: nothing forces the trade, so the trade can be measured.

What this does not say. It is descriptive. It reports what the clubs that reached January looked like, not what any club should go out and buy — a front office cannot order three points of margin. Season-long margin also cannot see a club that banked its points in three blowouts and squeaked through the rest. And the field grew from twelve clubs to fourteen in 2020, which nudges the bar down; both eras are pooled here.
