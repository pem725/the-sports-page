# Trademark prep — what to hand a lawyer

**This is research, not legal advice, and it is not a clearance opinion.** I searched
public sources; a real clearance requires a professional search covering federal
registrations, state registrations, and common-law use, plus a judgment call about
likelihood of confusion. Do not file on the strength of this document. Its purpose
is to make the first meeting with a trademark attorney shorter and cheaper.

---

## The short version

**The logo is the asset worth protecting. The name is the hard one.**

That is the opposite of most people's instinct, and it is the single most useful
thing on this page.

---

## 1. Why "The Sports Page" is difficult

A mark that merely describes the goods or services is refused under **Section
2(e)(1)** of the Lanham Act. The reasoning is that competitors must stay free to
use ordinary language to describe what they sell.

"The sports page" is the ordinary English name for *the part of a newspaper that
covers sport*. For a publication about sport, that is close to the centre of the
descriptiveness problem — it does not merely describe a quality of the goods, it
is the generic name of the category the goods sit in.

Routes that exist, none of them quick:

- **Acquired distinctiveness (secondary meaning).** Descriptive marks can register
  once the public associates them with one source. *Sports Illustrated* is the
  standard example. Evidence is advertising spend, sales, circulation, unsolicited
  media coverage — and typically five or more years of substantially exclusive use.
- **The Supplemental Register.** Weaker protection, no presumption of validity, but
  it is a real filing, it blocks later confusingly similar marks from the Principal
  Register, and it can move to the Principal Register later.
- **A composite mark.** Register the *logo including the words*. The design carries
  the distinctiveness; a disclaimer of the descriptive wording is usually required.

## 2. What is already on the register

Two searches were run independently — one here, one in a separate Claude
conversation (shared 11 Aug 2026) that had the logo attached. Together they give
a fuller picture than either alone.

**Dead registrations — these do not block anything:**

| Mark | Services | Filed | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| THE SPORTS PAGE | Bookstore services | 1977 | Cancelled 1985 |
| SPORTS PAGE | Restaurant services | 1985 (Reg. 1362666) | Cancelled 2006 |

**Live marks and common-law users — all in food and drink:**

| Name | Nature |
|---|---|
| STEVIE TOMATO'S SPORTS PAGE SPORTS GRILL | **Active federal registration**, bar/restaurant services |
| Sports Page Food & Spirits (Charlotte) | In use since 1987; likely unregistered common-law rights |
| Various local "Sports Page" bars nationwide | Common-law rights in their own trading areas |

**And the important negative finding:** neither search turned up a **live,
registered "The Sports Page" mark for sports news, data, or statistics** — which
is the lane this newsletter actually occupies. Class 41 appears to be open.

Both analyses reached the same conclusion by different routes: **the wordmark is
descriptive and vulnerable; the logo is the asset that will register cleanly.**
Independent convergence is worth something here.

### The merch makes the restaurant marks matter more than they look

The other analysis flagged that the food-and-drink overlap becomes relevant *"if
you ever expand into merch, events, or anything restaurant-adjacent."*

That is not hypothetical. Polo shirts and t-shirts carrying the mark are being
produced right now. Clothing is **Class 25**, and printed apparel sits closer to
the restaurant-and-bar promotional world than a newsletter does. This does not
make a filing impossible, but it means the Class 25 question should be asked
specifically rather than treated as an afterthought — and it interacts with the
licence problem in §4 below.

## 3. What we do own already

Trademark rights in the United States arise from **use**, not from registration.
Registration strengthens and extends them. The use here is unusually well
documented:

| Evidence | Detail |
|---|---|
| First use | **29 March 2026** — first commit, "Launch The Sports Page — Issues 1 & 2" |
| Continuity | 442 commits, **140 published issues**, unbroken to 11 August 2026 |
| Public display | thesportspage.net, live, serving the mark on every page |
| Nature of the record | Git history — dated, ordered, and hash-chained |

That last row is worth saying out loud to a lawyer. A git repository is a
timestamped, tamper-evident record of exactly when the mark was first used and that
use has been continuous since. Most small applicants cannot evidence first use
anywhere near this cleanly.

**Note:** the domain registration itself confers no trademark rights. Owning
thesportspage.net is not a trademark claim.

## 4. The awkward part: the licence

The site's licence says, in plain terms, **no commercial gain — "not directly, not
indirectly."** Federal trademark rights require **use in commerce**.

These pull against each other, and it is a real question rather than a technicality:

- For **Class 41** (entertainment and publishing services), free distribution of a
  publication can still support a filing — services rendered in commerce do not
  require a price tag — but it needs to be argued properly.
- For **Class 25** (clothing), giving shirts to founders and friends is probably not
  use in commerce. Selling them would be, and would also collide with the licence
  as currently written.

Decide what the project is before filing. Registering a mark for goods you have
declared you will never sell is a route to an unused-mark problem later.

## 5. Classes likely to matter

- **Class 41** — online publication of a newsletter; entertainment and educational
  services relating to sport and statistics. *The core filing.*
- **Class 16** — printed publications, if anything is ever printed.
- **Class 25** — clothing, only if merchandise is actually sold.
- **Class 9** — downloadable publications, if the email edition counts as one.

## 6. What to bring to the attorney

1. **The logo, as vector.** `merch/logo-badge.svg` — the badge with the S and the
   normal curve. This is the strong asset and it is already in a filing-ready format.
2. **First-use date: 29 March 2026**, with the git log as evidence.
3. **A specimen**: a screenshot of thesportspage.net showing the mark in connection
   with the services.
4. **This question, asked first:** *given the descriptiveness problem, is the right
   filing the design mark alone, the composite mark with the words disclaimed, or
   the Supplemental Register?* The answer determines everything else.
5. **The licence**, so they can advise on the use-in-commerce point above.

## 7. Honest expectation

A design-mark filing on the badge has a reasonable path. A standard-character
filing on the bare words "THE SPORTS PAGE" for a sports publication should be
expected to draw a 2(e)(1) refusal, and planning around that is cheaper than being
surprised by it.

If speed is the priority: **file the logo.** It is distinctive, it is ready, and it
protects the thing a reader actually recognises.
