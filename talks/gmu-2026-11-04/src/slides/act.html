A real case. Take every college football program that had a big season, three wins in four or better, from 2005 on: 421 of them. Before the next season, four forecasts. A coin flip, every team at .500, misses by 2.8 wins a season. The fan's forecast, same as last year, misses by 2.1. The plain base rate, the program's own average over the last ten years, misses by 1.8: better than the fan, because a big year drifts back toward the middle. Our best model, a blend we fit only on 1980 to 2004 so it never saw these seasons, misses by 1.7. That is a tenth of a win better than the base rate. Is that worth building? So ask: how uncertain should you be, and should you be that uncertain? Your uncertainty can shrink as your model gets better, and the model can still be woefully inadequate; even the best one here misses by nearly two wins. Sometimes you are better off just going with the base rate. A tool that quantifies uncertainty is no use if you don't know how to act on it. That is the judgment I want people to carry out of the sports page and into the voting booth, the mortgage office and the doctor's office. That is the order that matters: emotion, then a number, then belief, then uncertainty. And none of it works if the design and the measurement are bad, because the design sets the ceiling on what you can conclude.
