How to read it

The big number is the season. Out of every 100 regulars that same year, how many this one beat. It leads because it holds the rules, the ballparks and the strategy of the day fixed: everyone in it faced the same league.

The second line is all time. The same count against every regular season since the stat was kept. When the two disagree, the season was unusual: a low all-time number beside a high season number means the whole league struggled that year.

The third line is the league. The typical regular that season, and where that typical season ranks among all of them.

Pitchers are compared within their own job. A starter is ranked only against starters, a reliever only against relievers: their ERAs and strikeout rates live in different worlds. The line between the two jobs is blurrier before the 1950s, when a pitcher who started fewer than half his games was usually a swingman who also started, not a modern reliever. If a season ever has fewer than 20 pitchers in a group, the card says so instead of ranking a handful.

Every number reads in the direction of its sentence: a high number for ERA means a low ERA. 100 is kept for the single top value; everyone else tops out at 99. Regulars: hitters with 300 or more plate appearances; starters (at least half their games started) with 100 or more innings; relievers with 40 or more. All are scaled down for short seasons (1918, 1981, 1994, 2020), so a 50 is an ordinary regular, not an ordinary major leaguer. Strikeouts start in 1913, when both leagues kept them; saves in 1969, when they became official. Source: the MLB Stats API, rebuilt each winter.
