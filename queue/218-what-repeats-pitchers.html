226 Strikeouts, Then 227. His ERA Dropped by Almost Half.

Dylan Cease struck out 226 hitters in 2021 and 227 in 2022. His earned run average went from 3.9 to 2.2. Across every pitcher since 2015, the same pattern holds: what a pitcher throws comes back each year, and his ERA mostly does not. For a relief pitcher, it hardly comes back at all.

The question: how much of a pitcher’s ERA is the pitcher?

The answer: less than you think. Line up one season against the next, and a starter’s ERA comes back only .28 of the way. On this scale, 1 means all of it. A reliever’s comes back .05, which is about nothing. But how hard he throws comes back .93, and his strikeouts .75.

Why that is surprising: ERA is the number on the scoreboard, in the box score and on the back of the card. It is how most fans, and plenty of teams, decide who is good. You would expect the main number to be the most reliable one. It is one of the least.

What you get: a way to tell a pitcher’s skill from his luck. And a short list of stats to check before you believe a pitcher has changed.

What a pitcher controls

Think about what happens on a pitch. The pitcher decides how hard to throw and where. The hitter decides whether to swing. If he misses, or takes strike three, the pitcher did that. If he walks, the pitcher did that too. But once the ball is hit into the field, the play belongs to someone else. Seven fielders, the ballpark, the wind and a few inches of luck decide it.

So we measured how much of each pitching stat comes back. We took every starter with 100 innings and every reliever with 40, in back-to-back seasons from 2015 through 2025. For each stat we asked: did the pitchers who were high one year stay high the next?

Read the top of each group first. Fastball speed, ground balls, swings and misses and strikeouts all sit high. Those are things the pitcher does with the ball in his hand.

Now look at the bottom. Hits on balls in play come back only .23 for starters and .10 for relievers. When a ball is put in play against a pitcher, whether it falls for a hit is mostly not up to him. A baseball writer named Voros McCracken noticed this more than 20 years ago, and it surprised almost everyone. Our numbers find it again. Home runs allowed are nearly as wobbly.

ERA sits down there with them, in red, because it is a mix. It counts the strikeouts and walks the pitcher earned. It also counts every bloop single, every bad hop and every fielder who could not reach a ball.

A reliever’s ERA this year tells you almost nothing about his ERA next year.

— The Sports Page

Why relievers are the extreme case

A starter in our group throws about 150 innings in a season. A reliever throws about 60. A short season is a small sample, and in a small sample a few bad innings can swing the whole number. Two cheap hits and a home run on one bad night can move a reliever’s ERA by half a run.

So a reliever’s ERA repeats .05 of the way, and its 95% interval runs from a hair below zero to .12. Teams still hand out contracts and closer jobs on last year’s ERA. The pitcher’s velocity, at .95, would be a far safer thing to pay for.

Dylan Cease’s two seasons

Back to Cease. In 2021 he struck out 226 hitters and had a 3.9 ERA. In 2022 he struck out 227 and had a 2.2 ERA. What he controlled barely moved. The number everyone quoted fell by almost half.

Here is how to use a repeat rate yourself. Take how far a pitcher was from average. Multiply by the repeat rate. Add that to average, and you have a fair guess for next year. The average starter in 2021 had a 4.1 ERA, so Cease was 0.2 better. Multiply by .28 and you keep almost nothing. The fair guess for his 2022 was the 2022 average, about 3.8. He beat it by more than a run and a half. A guess like that is a starting point. Luck runs both ways.

What to take home

Judge a pitcher by what he controls. Velocity, strikeouts, walks and ground balls come back. Check those before you decide a pitcher got better or worse.

Treat ERA as part skill and part weather. For a starter, keep about a quarter of his distance from average. For a reliever, keep almost none. We wrote earlier about how many innings a stat needs before it settles, in Issue #32. Even after a full season, ERA has not settled.

When a number mixes skill with luck, the luck comes out first. Next year, expect the luck to go and the skill to stay. For more, see our primers on regression to the mean and signal and noise.

How we did it

Who. Starters: half their games started and 100 or more innings (about 155 innings on average). Relievers: two or fewer starts and 40 or more innings. 2015–2025, with the 2020 bars scaled to its 60 games: 1,399 starter seasons and 1,970 reliever seasons. A pair is the same pitcher, same role, in back-to-back seasons; pairs touching 2020 are left out. Starters: 641 pairs. Relievers: 761.

The repeat rate is the correlation between one season and the next. The 95% intervals use the standard Fisher method. Starters: ERA .28 (.21–.35); strikeouts .75 (.71–.78); walks .58; home runs allowed .27; hits on balls in play .23 (.16–.31). Relievers: ERA .05 (−.02 to .12); strikeouts .63; walks .55; home runs allowed .11; hits on balls in play .10 (.03–.17). Counting stats are per batter faced (and square-rooted, which steadies a rate).

Two feeds. Every box-score number here comes from the MLB Stats API and was recomputed from the Lahman database; the repeat rates match to the hundredth, pair for pair. Cease’s strikeouts and ERA, and the average starter’s ERA (4.1 in 2021, 3.8 in 2022), match on both feeds. Fastball speed (.93 starters, .95 relievers), swings and misses, ground balls and exit velocity allowed come from Baseball Savant alone. That is one feed. We publish only these summary figures, never the per-player data.

Limits. The same pitcher can appear in several pairs, so the intervals are a little too narrow. Pitchers who got hurt or lost their job are missing from the pairs. The “fair guess” rule assumes ERA spreads about the same each year. Code: research/constructs/baseball-validation/ in our repository.
