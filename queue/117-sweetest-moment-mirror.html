The Sweetest Moment in Sports Is the Same Shape Too.

Yesterday’s companion piece found that a fan’s deepest wound is always the identical injury: the favorite, at the summit, losing the coin flip. This is the happy half. Flip that sentence and you get the sweetest moment there is — the underdog winning it — and it turns out to be the same mathematics, read from the other side. It also hides the most useful lesson in sports.

The previous issue ended on a promise: if the worst thing that can happen to a fan is the favorite losing the flip at the top, then the best thing is its exact reverse — the team nobody believed in, winning it. That promise is not just poetic. It is literal. The two moments are the same statistical object, and the difference between agony and ecstasy is nothing but which side of the flip you walked in expecting.

Three miracles, one miracle

Take three that need no setup, chosen — as their dark twins were — to be as different from one another as sport allows.

The momentWhat happened
Feb. 22, 1980
United States 4, Soviet Union 3		A roster of American college kids met the Soviet team that had won the previous four Olympic golds and was, by common consent, the best hockey side on earth. At Lake Placid the students won 4–3, Mike Eruzione scoring midway through the third period, and a broadcaster asked a whole country whether it believed in miracles. Two days later they took the gold.
Feb. 3, 2008
Giants 17, Patriots 14		New England arrived at the Super Bowl 18–0, one win from the only perfect season in the modern game. The heavy-underdog Giants ended it — a quarterback wriggling free of a certain sack, a receiver pinning the ball to his own helmet, a touchdown with thirty-five seconds left. The perfect season died one game short.
Oct. 16, 1969
Mets win the World Series		A franchise that had never finished above ninth place, that had lost a hundred games in five of its first seven years, opened 1969 at 100-to-1. It then won a hundred games, swept into the Series, and beat a Baltimore team that had won a hundred and nine. The Amazin’ Mets, from nowhere, champions.

Amateurs over a superpower, a nobody ending perfection, a laughingstock winning it all. On the surface, three unrelated joys. Underneath, one shape — and it is precisely the shape of the three heartbreaks from the companion piece, turned inside out. There, a favorite met maximum variance at the summit and it destroyed him. Here, an underdog met the same maximum variance at the same summit — and it set him free.

Why the summit is the underdog’s friend

The engine is identical to yesterday’s, running in reverse. A favorite’s edge only shows up with repetition; strip the season down to a single game, a single possession, a single shot, and most of that edge disappears. For the favorite, that is a catastrophe — the protection of the long haul, gone. But the underdog is the favorite’s mirror, and the very same collapse toward the coin flip that guts the favorite lifts the underdog by exactly as muchi. A team with a twenty-nine percent chance over a full season — a clear inferior — is nearly a coin flip, forty-eight percent, in one sudden-death game. Sudden death does not merely fail to protect the favorite. It actively hands the underdog the best odds it will ever see.

The lesson hiding in the joy

Here is where the happy half earns its keep, because the mirror is not only about probability. Recall why the favorite’s loss hurt so much: the pain of any outcome is the gap between what you expected and what you goti, and the favorite expected to win, so the fall was the whole height of that certainty. Now run it the other way. The underdog expected nothing. Its fans had been told all season they had no business here. So when the flip comes up their way, the joy is the full distance from that near-zero expectation to the summit — the widest gap in the other direction, felt all at once.

Which means the two feelings are governed by the same subtraction, and the sign is set entirely by where you started. High expectation exposes you to the deepest wound. Low expectation opens you to the sweetest joy. It is the identical coin flip, at the identical summit; your prior — what you walked in believing — simply decides which face of it you are standing under.

Same coin, same summit. The favorite is standing under the side that can only fall; the underdog, under the side that can only rise.

Low expectations are a superpower

Which is why the fan who seems congenitally happy with a mediocre team is not simple. He has solved the problem. By expecting little he has stood himself under the rising side of every coin, where variance can only surprise him upward — rarely wounded, because he never carried the towering hope a wound requires, and often delighted, because every good bounce lands as a gift instead of a debt repaid.

That is not a trick for faking joy over a bad team. It is a real claim about where joy lives: not in outcomes, which no fan controls, but in the gap between outcomes and expectations, which every fan sets. The Miracle on Ice was a miracle because no one thought it possible. Ask a little less of the coin, and it has that much more room to astonish you.

Notes & sources

The three moments are on the record. United States 4, Soviet Union 3 (Feb. 22, 1980, Lake Placid): a United States team of amateur and college players beat the four-time defending Olympic gold medalists in the medal round, Mike Eruzione scoring the winner midway through the third period; the Americans clinched the gold two days later against Finland. Giants 17, Patriots 14 (Feb. 3, 2008, Super Bowl XLII): New England entered 18–0 chasing a perfect season; the underdog Giants won on a late touchdown four plays after David Tyree’s “helmet catch,” ending the bid one game short. 1969 World Series: the Mets, a 100-to-1 preseason long shot who had never finished above ninth, went 100–62 and beat the 109-win Baltimore Orioles four games to one, clinching on Oct. 16.

The win-probability figure is the same simple model as the companion piece, now showing both teams: treating each chance as an independent trial, a team’s probability of winning scales with the square root of the number of trials, so a matchup calibrated to 71%–29% over a 162-game haul narrows to roughly 52%–48% in a single sudden-death moment. The favorite’s fall and the underdog’s rise are the same movement, mirror-imaged about the 50% line. Real games carry correlation and momentum the model leaves out, which shifts the numbers but not the symmetry.
