Outliving the Owner Won’t Save You. Ask the Raiders.

Since 1980, 28 NFL owners have died while running their teams. Eighteen of those teams are still in the family. A Jets fan of 60 will very likely outlive Woody Johnson, and still has worse than even odds of seeing the Jets leave the Johnsons.

Will you outlive your team’s owner, and would it help? Every long-suffering fan has done this grim little sum. Woody Johnson is 79. A Jets fan of 60 thinks: I’ve got him by nineteen years. Just wait.

Here is the answer, with its number. Since 1980, 28 NFL owners have died while still in charge. Eighteen of those teams never left the family. Only three were sold within a year.

That is the surprise: the fan is betting on the wrong event. The owner dying is close to a sure thing. The team leaving the family is a coin flip. By the end, you will know the difference, and why the Raiders already learned it.

Ask the Raiders

Al Davis ran the Raiders as owner and general manager for nearly forty years. He won three Super Bowls, then spent most of his last decade losing, and a lot of Raiders fans quietly did the same sum you are doing now. Davis died in October 2011, at 82. The team went to his son, Mark. It is still his.

Dallas fans should not get their hopes up either. Jerry Jones, 84, has run the Cowboys as owner and general manager since 1989. In 2016 he said his succession plan “has been in place almost from the first interview.” His children already work at the top of the club.

What Usually Happens

We went through every change of control in the NFL since 1980, 67 of them, and pulled out the 28 times an owner died in charge. Eighteen teams went to a widow, a son, a daughter or a family trust. The Bears, the Steelers, the Giants, the Chiefs and the Colts are all on that list. Ten were eventually sold to outsiders, and the median wait was about three years. Paul Allen died in 2018; the Seahawks were not sold until this August.

Now the Jets’ own story, which is the best joke in the piece. The last time the Jets changed hands, it was because an owner died. Leon Hess’s will said the team must be sold, and barred his own family from buying it. That is how Woody Johnson got the Jets in 2000, for $635 million. Notice what that means: the Johnsons own the team because of exactly the event you are hoping for. And no plan to sell has been reported. Woody has said two of his sons sit in on team meetings, as an “apprenticeship.”

The owner dying is close to a sure thing. The team leaving the family is a coin flip.

The Two Bets

Look at the gap between each pair of bars. A fan of 60 outlives a 79-year-old man 86 times in 100. But the team leaves the Johnson family in that fan’s lifetime only about 45 times in 100. That lower bar counts both ways out. One is a sale after Woody’s death. The other is a plain sale while he is alive, which NFL owners do about once in every 55 years of ownership.

Is a fan of 30 better off? Yes, but mostly not because of Woody. A young fan simply has more years for any sale at all to happen.

One more twist, and it cuts against you. Very rich men live longer than the life table says. If we give Woody the life expectancy of the richest 1% of American men, his median rises from about eight more years to about thirteen. The fan of 60 now outlives him 74 times in 100, not 86.

What to Take Home

When you catch yourself doing the owner’s-age math, ask the question underneath it. Not “will he die?” but “will the team change hands?” Those are two different events, and only the second one helps you. The data says it is closer to a coin flip than a sure thing, even with time on your side.

And hold onto this: a new owner is not a new team. The Patriots’ owner did not change when their fortunes did; the coach and the quarterback did. Whoever signs the checks, the seasons are decided by the people he hires. (The Packers, by the way, are owned by their fans. Green Bay is the one place where this math has no answer at all.)

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Owners: every change of controlling ownership of an NFL team from 1980 to October 31, 2026, 67 in all, each confirmed by two independent sources where we could (59 of 67). Twenty-eight owners died in control: 18 teams stayed in the family, 10 were sold, 3 within a year and 8 within five. Among deaths with five years of follow-up, 32% were sold within five years. Living owners sold at about 1.8% a year (22 sales over 1,300 franchise-years). Sources include AP, ESPN, the NFL and the teams; the Seahawks’ $9.6 billion sale was approved on August 26, 2026.

Lifetimes: the Social Security Administration’s 2023 period life table for men. Woody Johnson was born in 1947; his age is confirmed by CBS and ESPN. The national table gives a man of 79 a median of 8.3 more years. The wealth adjustment cuts the death rate at every age by the same share, enough to match the life expectancy that Chetty and colleagues (JAMA, 2016) found for the richest 1% of men at age 40, 87.3 years; it gives a median of 13. The fan is a man on the national table; a woman fan lives longer, so her odds are better.

The ranges (90%): team leaves the family in the fan’s lifetime: fan of 30, 56 to 76%; fan of 45, 46 to 68%; fan of 60, 35 to 56%. With the wealth adjustment, 33 to 52% for the fan of 60.

What it cannot tell you: the Johnsons are not an average family, and history is a base rate, not a plan. A reported sale, a will that orders one, or a change in the family would move these numbers more than any table. Lifetimes are treated as independent, and the model holds the sale rate steady across decades.
