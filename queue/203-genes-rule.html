Good Teams Answer Back. The 7–2 Bears Rarely Do.

Gene’s rule: you can tell a good team by how fast it scores after the other side does. We checked about 30,000 answer drives since 1999. Gene is right that good teams answer. The reason is plainer than it feels: they score after a touchdown about as often as they score any other time.

Answers by the 7–2 Bears

Does a team that answers a score show you something special, or just that it is good? Gene, a longtime reader, has a rule for it. The other team scores a touchdown. Now watch the next drive. A good team gets off the mat and scores right back. We will call a club that does that a Gene team, and his rule Gene’s Rule; the idea is his, all of it.

The answer: Gene teams are good teams, and the numbers back him. But the answer is not a separate gift. Since 1999, NFL teams have scored on 33 of every 100 drives right after giving up a touchdown, and on 34 of every 100 other drives that started with a kickoff. Same field position, same team, same rate.

Why that is surprising: we all feel the answer drive reveals character. You can see it on the sideline. Baseball and hockey say the same thing football does, and so does this year’s standings page. What you get: a better way to watch that drive, and a reason not to grade a team on 18 of them.

The answer is a score

Notice how flat that picture is. In baseball, a team that has just been scored on puts up a run in its next turn 28 times in 100. In every other half-inning it does so 28 times in 100. The 1986 Mets, the best comeback team many of us ever loved, answered 29 times in 100, and scored in 29 of 100 other half-innings. They were not rising to the occasion; they were a good lineup, every inning.

Hockey says it too. After a goal, the next goal goes to the team that just gave one up 41 times in 100, and to the team that just scored 42 times in 100; the rest of the time, nobody scores again. The 2022–23 Rangers got the next goal after 113 of the 216 goals scored against them, one of the best marks of the decade. They also scored 56 of every 100 goals in their games. A team that wins the answer battle is a team that wins the goal battle.

So why does Gene’s Rule feel true?

Because it is true, at the level Gene meant it. Across 861 NFL team-seasons, the clubs that answered more often won more often (a correlation of .45). But the clubs that scored more on all their other drives won more still (.61). The answer drive is a small slice of the offense, taken at the moment you are watching hardest.

Here is the test that settles it. Take how much better a team answers than it usually scores; call it the answer premium. If answering were its own skill, the premium would track winning, and a team that had it one year would tend to have it the next. It does neither. Its link to winning is −.01; from one year to the next it is .01. Zero, twice. Baseball gives the same pair of zeros.

Nobody rises to the occasion. Good teams were already there.

The 2026 Gene teams

Six clubs have answered at least half their chances this season (15 or more chances, through Nov. 12). Three have winning records. Three have losing records. And the 49ers, Broncos and Bears, at 7–1, 7–2 and 7–2, are not Gene teams at all.

Why can a 7–2 team fail the rule? It has had only 18 chances. Flip a fair coin 18 times and you will often see 6 heads, or 12. A good team also gets scored on less, so it gets fewer chances to show you anything. After nine weeks, point differential forecasts the rest of an NFL season about four times as well as the answer rate does, and adding the answer rate to it adds almost nothing.

Use it on Sunday

Inside one game, Gene’s Rule is a fine guide to your mood. When the other team scores the game’s first touchdown, the team that answers on its next drive goes on to win 42 times in 100; the team that does not wins 27. Answer with a touchdown and it is 48 in 100; that is a tied game, and a tied game is a coin flip. The score is doing the work, not the spirit.

What to take home

Gene’s Rule is right, and it is right for a plain reason: an answer is a score, and good teams score. Watch the answer drive; it is real evidence. Then watch the other ten drives too, because they are the same evidence and there is more of it. When one vivid moment seems to tell you everything about a team, ask how often that moment happens on an ordinary drive. Usually the answer is “just as often.”

How we measured it

Football. An answer chance is a drive that comes right after the other team scores a touchdown (on offense, defense or a return), in the same half, with at least two minutes left. It is answered if it ends in a touchdown or field goal. The comparison is the same team’s other drives that began with a kickoff. Same game, same opponent, the gap holds: 34 against 35. Source: nflverse play-by-play, regular season, 29,864 answer chances; 2026 counts checked against ESPN.

Baseball. A chance is the half-inning right after the other team scores. Source: Retrosheet line scores. The information used here was obtained free of charge from and is copyrighted by Retrosheet. Interested parties may contact Retrosheet at www.retrosheet.org.

Hockey. After every goal, whose goal comes next. Shootouts left out. Source: NHL play-by-play.

Limits. A trailing team plays differently, and an answer drive is usually played from behind; we do not adjust for that. The 2026 table is nine weeks; treat it as a snapshot, not a verdict. Code and data: research/gene-rule.
