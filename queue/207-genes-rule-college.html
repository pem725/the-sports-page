In College, the Answer Drive Comes Up Short. Even No. 1 Georgia’s.

Gene’s rule: a good team answers a touchdown with a score of its own. In college football since 2014, teams scored on 32 of every 100 drives right after giving up a touchdown. On their other drives that began with a kickoff, they scored on 35. The answer came up short in all 13 seasons. Most of that gap is the scoreboard, not the heart.

Does Gene’s Rule hold on Saturdays? Gene, a longtime reader, says you can tell a good team by how fast it scores after the other side does. In Issue #232 we tested it in the pros: good teams answer, but no better than they score the rest of the time. If any football should reward heart, it is the kind played by 19-year-olds.

The answer: it runs the other way, a little. In about 9,000 games between FBS teams, the answer drive scored 32 times in 100 and the comparison drive 35. Why that is surprising: you would expect the gap to favor the answer, or at worst to vanish, as it does in the pros. What you get: the reason, and this year’s top 12.

Short every year

Notice that the rust dot sits left of the steel one in every row. That many seasons in a row is not luck. So do college teams fold after a punch? Ask first who just punched you.

You get an answer chance only when someone scores on you, and in college that someone is often much better. The average answer drive starts 8 points behind; the average kickoff drive, 2. Compare drives at the same score and the gap shrinks to 32 against 34. Compare inside the same game, same opponent, and it is 34 against 36. What is left is about the size of the NFL’s one-point gap. An answer drive is played against a team that is, that day, scoring on you.

The drive after a touchdown is just a drive. It only feels like a verdict.

— The Sports Page

Gene teams are still good teams

Gene is right in the way he meant it. Across 1,500 team-seasons, clubs that answered more often won more often (a correlation of .52). But clubs that scored more on all their other drives won more still (.71). Then the test that settles it, the answer premium: how much better a team answers than it usually scores. Its link to winning is −.05. From one year to the next it is .06. Two zeros, the same pair the pros gave us. Answering is scoring, and scoring is the skill.

This year’s top 12

Half of the committee’s top 12 have answered at least half their chances. The other half have not, and that half includes No. 1 Georgia (5 of 13) and unbeaten Utah and UCLA. Georgia scores on 60 of every 100 other drives. That team is not short of nerve; it has had 13 tries at one kind of drive.

Why can No. 1 fail the rule? Ten to 20 chances is a coin flipped ten to 20 times. Alabama’s 14 of 19 is the best mark here, and Alabama has lost twice. Halfway through a season, point differential forecasts the rest four times as well as the answer rate does. Add the answer rate to it and you gain nothing.

Use it Saturday

Inside one game, the rule is a fine guide to your mood. Say the other team scores the game’s first touchdown. The team that answers on its next drive goes on to win 45 times in 100; the team that does not wins 22. Answer with a touchdown and it is 49 in 100, a coin flip. The pros give almost the same three numbers: 42, 27 and 48. The score does the work, not the spirit.

What to take home

Gene’s Rule is right about good teams and wrong about why. Watch the answer drive; it is real evidence. Then remember who it is played against: the team that just scored on you, often the better team that day. When one moment seems to show you a team’s soul, ask how that team does on an ordinary drive. In college, the honest answer is “a bit better.”

How we measured it

An answer chance is the drive right after the other team scores a touchdown (on offense, defense or a return), same half, two or more minutes left. It counts if it ends in a touchdown or field goal. The comparison is the same team’s other drives that began with a kickoff: the opening drive of each half and the drive after the other team’s field goal. FBS against FBS, regular season, 2014 to Nov. 17, 2026: about 50,000 answer chances. Games whose drive scores do not add up to the final were left out (145 of about 9,000). Source: CollegeFootballData; 2026 top-12 counts and records checked against ESPN.

Limits. Trailing teams play differently; matching the score handles some of that, not all. The 2026 table is ten games; treat it as a snapshot. Code and data: research/gene-rule.
