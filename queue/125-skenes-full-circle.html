The Pitcher We Started With Is “Struggling.” The Numbers That Matter Say He Isn’t.

This newsletter was born, on Opening Day, on a Paul Skenes number: a 67.5 ERA that looked like the end of the world and meant absolutely nothing, because it came from a single ugly inning. More than a hundred issues later, Skenes is back in the headlines with a new number that lies to you — a 9–9 record and an ERA that has doubled. And once again, the number is telling you about everything on the field except the man on the mound.

The surface reads like a fall from grace, and the headlines have duly written it. In his first two seasons Paul Skenes posted earned-run averages of 1.96 and 1.97 — back-to-back, a Rookie of the Year and then a Cy Young, about as good as a young pitcher has ever been. This year the same pitcher sits at 9–9 with a 3.90 ERA, essentially double what it was. If you read only those two numbers — the record and the ERA — you would conclude that something has gone wrong with Paul Skenes. Those are, unfortunately, exactly the two numbers you should trust the least.

Look at what a pitcher actually controls

A pitcher cannot bat. He cannot field the seven positions behind him. He cannot make his teammates score. What he can do, more or less by himself, is miss bats and avoid walks — and that is precisely where nothing has changed. Skenes is striking out about eleven and a quarter men per nine innings, the same elite rate he has posted every year of his career. He is walking about two and a third, as stingy as ever. He is putting on base about one and a tenth runners an inning. The core skills of the pitcher — the strikeouts, the walks, the baserunners — are sitting exactly where they sat when he was winning the Cy Young. The engine did not break. Only the scoreboard changed.

Figure 1 · Everything Skenes controls held flat — and his ERA doubled anyway

So where did the ERA come from?

It came from the space between the pitcher and the scoreboard — the part that isn’t his. A few more of the balls in play found grass instead of gloves. A few more fly balls carried over the fence, and home runs are among the streakiest, most luck-tinged things in the sport. The sequencing turned against him, the singles bunching into innings instead of scattering harmlessly. ERA is often described as a pitching statistic, but it is really a blend — the pitcher, plus his defense, plus the ballpark, plus the coin-flips of which inning the hits arrived in. When a man’s strikeouts and walks are untouched and his ERA still doubles, the extra runs did not come from him. They came from the dice.

And the won-lost record is worse than the ERA, because it adds one more thing the pitcher cannot touch: whether his own team bothers to score. This is the single most misleading number in baseball. Skenes can throw seven shutout innings and lose one-to-nothing, and the box score will hang an L on him as if he had failed. On a Pittsburgh club that has given him next to nothing to work with, a genuinely great pitcher drifts to 9–9 and gets filed under “disappointing.” The record is not measuring Paul Skenes. It is measuring the lineup behind him, with his name on the label.

The won-lost record and the ERA are the two most-quoted numbers about a pitcher, and the two that tell you least about the pitcher.

The number will come back to him

Which is why this is not a story about decline but about regression waiting to happeni. When the things a player controls stay excellent while the things he doesn’t turn sour, the sour part is the temporary one. Elite strikeout-and-walk numbers are a promise that the runs will come back down; they always have. Bet on the skill, which is real and stable, over the results, which are noisy and borrowed. The most likely thing that happens next with Paul Skenes is that the fly balls stop carrying, the singles start scattering, and the ERA sags back toward the man underneath it.

There is a nice symmetry in the founding subject of this newsletter turning up again to teach the founding lesson. Issue No. 1 was a warning against a number from too small a sample — a 67.5 ERA off one bad inning, meaningless because there was nothing behind it. This is the grown-up version of the same warning, and a subtler one. A number can lie to you not only when there is too little of it, but when it is quietly measuring the wrong thing — the offense, the defense, the day’s luck — and charging it all to one man. Paul Skenes has not lost anything. His teammates stopped hitting and a few baseballs found seats. Watch the strikeouts. They never left, and they are the only part of the story that was ever really his.

Notes & sources

Skenes’ figures are from the MLB Stats API for the 2024–2026 seasons, reconciled to his won-lost record per this newsletter’s standard. In 2024 he went 11–3 with a 1.96 ERA (Rookie of the Year); in 2025, 10–10 with a 1.97 ERA (Cy Young); in the current 2026 season he stands at 9–9 with a 3.90 ERA across 23 starts and about 124⅔ innings. His strikeout rate (about 11.3 per nine), walk rate (about 2.3 per nine), and WHIP (about 1.11) are in line with his first two seasons — the skill metrics a pitcher most controls have not declined, while the ERA and record, which fold in defense, luck, and run support, have. Home-run and sequencing variance and weak run support are the usual culprits for exactly this gap; public advanced metrics have also pegged his expected ERA well below his actual mark this year.

The distinction at the heart of the piece — between a player’s own skill and the team-and-luck outcomes credited to him — and the expectation that results drift back toward skill are developed in the primer on Regression to the Mean (Concept No. 3). The 67.5-ERA reference is Issue No. 1, this newsletter’s first, published on MLB Opening Day 2026; find it in the archive.
