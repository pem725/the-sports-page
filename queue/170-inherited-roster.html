He Inherited the Roster. He Wins One Game Fewer Than It Should.

Everybody says give him three years. Nobody says what changes in those three years. So we counted the recruiting classes on every roster since 2000. When does a team stop being the last guy's team? Everybody has an opinion and nobody has a number. So we built one.

A college roster is roughly the last four recruiting classes. A class belongs to the new coach only if he signed it. That gives a share with no judgment in it. In his first season none of the roster is his. In his second, a quarter. Then half, then three quarters, and by his fifth season all of it.

Then the question becomes answerable. Do coaches beat the talent they were handed, and does the answer change as the talent becomes theirs?

The answer, across 2,685 seasons

First, the exchange rate. One standard deviation of recruiting — roughly the gap between the twentieth-best class in the country and the fiftieth — is worth 0.086 of winning percentage. About one win a season. That is what talent buys.

Now watch what coaches do against it.

A first-year coach finishes 8.3 points of winning percentage below what his inherited roster should have produced. Over twelve games that is a full win. He is not just failing to improve the team; he is getting less out of it than the talent on hand says he should.

Then it climbs, steadily, and it stops being negative at almost exactly the point where half the roster is his. By the fifth season he is 4.5 points above par. The swing from first season to fifth is about a win and a half.

The first year is not a small sample of the coach. It is a clean measurement of somebody else's players learning a new system.

— The Sports Page

The obvious objection, and why it does not hold

You might think this is survivorship; bad coaches get fired before their fourth season, so of course the men still standing in year five look good. The curve could be selection and nothing else.

So we tested it inside careers. Take only the 265 coaching spells that actually reached a fifth season, and look back at what those same men did earlier:

The men who survived five years were also underwater in year one. The climb happens inside their own careers, so survival is not creating it. Even the coaches who turned out to be good looked bad with somebody else's players.

So what does a first season tell you?

Less than the shouting suggests, and something different from what people think they are watching. A first-year record is mostly a measurement of the roster, run through a staff that has not had a signing day yet. The number to compare against is not .500. It is whatever that roster should have won, minus about a win.

So the honest version of "give him three years" is not patience; it is arithmetic. Three years is roughly when the roster crosses from mostly his predecessor's to mostly his. That is the first season whose record describes the man you hired.

What to take home

Before you judge anybody's first year at anything, ask what they inherited and how much of it they chose. Not as generosity; as measurement. A new manager, a new principal, a new head of a department — the outcomes you see in year one were mostly set in motion before they arrived.

The useful question is never "did it get better." It is "did it get better than what they were handed should have produced". Then, separately: how much of that did they pick?

Where this is generous to coaches, and it is generous in three places. Recruiting rankings are a market forecast, not a measurement, and they price the same hype a big-name hire generates. They ignore transfers completely, which in the portal era is a large and growing hole. And attrition is not random: struggling teams lose their best players. Every one of those pushes toward crediting a coach for talent he did not develop, so read the whole curve as flattering.

And one result we are not publishing as a finding. Beating your roster persists better on an inherited one (r = 0.45) than on your own (0.31), which is backwards from what you would expect. The likely explanation is uncomfortable. On an inherited roster, part of what we measure is a stable gap between a school’s recruiting rankings and its real quality. That is a property of the school, not the coach. We are working on it.
