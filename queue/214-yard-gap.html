Same 4.4 Yards a Carry. Not the Same Carry.

College football and the NFL each average about 4.4 yards a carry. But college counts a sack as a run, and the NFL counts it as a pass. Count both leagues the same way and a college carry goes about a yard farther, in every season since 2014.
By The Sports Page · The Professor · Lesson: is it the same ruler?

The question: is a yard per carry in college worth the same as a yard per carry in the NFL?

The answer: no. Count sacks as runs in both leagues, the college way, and college runs gain 4.4 yards to the NFL’s 3.4. Count them as passes, the NFL way, and it is 5.0 to 4.3. Either way, the college carry goes farther: about a yard the first way, about four-fifths of a yard the second.

Why that is surprising: the two numbers on the screen look the same. In 2024, both leagues averaged 4.4 yards a carry. You would assume a 4.4 is a 4.4. It is not, because the two leagues are not measuring the same thing.

What you get: a question to ask before you compare any number across two leagues, two eras or two countries. Psychologists call it measurement invariance. You can call it “is it the same ruler?”

Where the sacks go

When a quarterback is sacked, somebody has to be charged with the lost yards. In college, the sack goes into the team’s rushing. In the NFL, it goes into passing.

That one bookkeeping rule moves a lot. A sack costs about 6.6 yards in either league. A college team is sacked about two dozen times a season, and all of those losses land on its rushing line. An NFL team is sacked more, about 40 times a season, and its rushing line never sees one of them.

So start by putting both leagues on one ruler. We took 12 seasons, 2014 through 2025: 1,523 college team-seasons and 384 NFL team-seasons. Then we counted the sacks both ways.

Look at the middle pair and then the right pair. Notice that the gap never goes away; it only changes size. Counted as runs, the gap is 1.0 yards (0.9 to 1.1). Counted as passes, it is 0.8 (0.7 to 0.8). It shows up in all 12 seasons. The smallest gap, 2022 counted the NFL way, was still half a yard.

The left pair is the one you see on television. It hides the gap because the two rules push in opposite directions. College loads its sacks into its rushing, which drags its average down to meet the NFL’s.

Two numbers can match on the screen and still be measured with different rulers.

— The Sports Page

Same ruler, same zero?

Fixing the sacks is the easy part. The harder question is whether yards per carry measures the same skill in both leagues. Here we borrowed a test that psychologists use on questionnaires. Before you compare people in two countries, you check that the test works the same way in both. There are three steps, and each one asks more than the last.

1. Same pieces? We asked a computer to sort seven team stats into groups, in each league separately. Both leagues gave the same answer: a passing game and a running game. Yards per carry and rushing touchdowns go together in both. Passed.

2. Same weight? Does yards per carry follow a team’s running game just as closely in both leagues? It does. A good running team shows a high average in college and in the NFL alike. Passed.

3. Same zero? Does the same running game give the same number in both leagues? It does not. Even with the sacks counted the same way, the running stats sit at a different level in college. So did completion rate: at the same strength, college passers complete fewer passes. Failed.

Think of two bathroom scales. Both go up when you gain a pound, and by the same amount. But one of them reads five pounds heavy. Each is fine for tracking your own weight. Neither is safe for comparing yourself with your neighbor.

That is yards per carry. Inside one league it is a good measure. Use it to rank Big Ten teams or NFC teams, and it does its job. Use it to compare a college back with a pro back, and you are reading two scales that do not share a zero.

Why would a college carry go farther?

We do not know yet, and we would rather say so than guess. One thing we can measure: college teams are far more uneven. The spread between strong and weak college teams in yards per carry is about one and a half times the NFL’s. A college schedule hands a good offense some badly outmatched defenses, and the NFL rarely does. That is a reason to suspect mismatches, not proof. The next step is to test it on running backs, one player at a time, where sacks never touch the numbers.

What to take home

Before you compare a number across two places, ask how each place counts it. College and the NFL disagree about where a sack goes, and that one rule hides a yard a carry. Era changes, scoring rules and park sizes do the same to other stats.

Then ask whether the same skill makes the same number. A stat can rise and fall with a skill in exactly the same way in two places and still sit at a different level. That is the “same zero” test, and it is the one most comparisons skip.

A stat can be good inside a league and poor between leagues. Yards per carry is both. Notice which comparison you are making before you trust it.

How we did it

Data. Team-season offense, 2014–2025. College: 1,523 FBS team-seasons from CollegeFootballData (/stats/season). NFL: 384 regular-season team-seasons from nflverse. Second feed: ESPN’s team statistics for every team and season (1,555 college and 382 NFL team-seasons). The two feeds agree within a hundredth of a yard on every average both can compute: college as counted, NFL as counted, NFL with sacks as runs, and the gap. The college feed from CollegeFootballData includes bowl games; ESPN’s regular season does not. One check confirmed where college sacks go: Kent State’s 2024 rushing (no bowl game) matches on both feeds, with its 43 sacks inside it.

Two ways of counting. Sacks as runs: NFL rushing yards plus sack yards, over carries plus sacks. Sacks as passes: college rushing yards with sack yards taken back out, over carries minus sacks. College sack yards come from ESPN alone. So the sacks-as-passes college figure (5.0) and its gap (0.8) rest on one feed. The two feeds do agree on the sack count where we checked it (Kent State, 43). Kneel-downs count as carries in both leagues, both ways.

The invariance test. We fit a two-factor model in both leagues at once. Passing: yards per pass, completion rate, touchdown rate, interception rate, sack rate. Running: yards per carry, rushing touchdown rate. We added two bookkeeping links: a touchdown is either a run or a pass, and a pass is either caught or intercepted. Fit (CFI, where 1 is perfect): same pieces .95; same weights .94; same zeros .83, a clear failure.

Uncertainty. Intervals are 95%, from 4,000 resamples of team-seasons. They cover chance only. Gaps, college minus NFL: sacks as runs 1.0 (0.9–1.1); sacks as passes 0.8 (0.7–0.8); as each league counts 0.1 (0.05–0.2).

Limits. The running factor has only two stats, so the test cannot say which of them broke, only that the pair did. These are team averages, which mix talent with game plans and with the score. Defense is left out. Code and full results: research/constructs/football/ in our repository.
