The Radar Gun Knows Before the ERA Does. Some Numbers Predict; Most Just Revert.

A companion issue argued that Paul Skenes isn’t really declining — his ugly ERA and .500 record are mostly noise. A sharp reader pushed back with one word: velocity. It is the perfect objection, because it opens the single most useful door in statistics. Some numbers become trustworthy almost the moment you begin counting. Others never do. Telling them apart is the whole difference between seeing a decline coming and being fooled by a mirage.

Every statistic is a mixture of two things: the player’s real skill, and the luck of the particular sample you happened to watch. Early on, the mixture is nearly all luck — a hitter is four-for-eight, a pitcher has faced twelve men. As the sample grows, skill slowly drowns out the noise, until at some point the number becomes a fair reflection of the player. Sabermetricians even measured where that point sits for each stat — the sample where it stabilizesi, where about half the variation you see is finally real. And the striking discovery is how wildly that point differs from one number to the next.

The ladder of trust

A pitcher’s strikeout rate stabilizes after about seventy batters faced — three starts. His walk rate takes about a hundred and seventy. But the number of hits that fall in against him — his batting average on balls in play — does not stabilize until around two thousand balls in play, which is to say never, not within a single season and barely within several. His home-run rate needs well over a thousand batters. These are not trivia; they are a ranking of how much of each number is the pitcher and how much is the dice. The stats that stabilize fast are the ones a pitcher controls with his own arm. The stats that stabilize slowly — or never — are the ones the defense, the ballpark, and blind luck are secretly writing for him.

Why velocity wins the argument

Now put the reader’s objection where it belongs on that ladder, at the very top. A fastball’s velocity is the fastest-stabilizing number in the sport, because it is not a bounce of the ball at all — it is a physical fact about the arm, readable on a radar gun the very first time out. You do not need a season, or a month, or even a full start to know how hard a man is throwing; a dozen pitches will do. That is why a velocity dip is the earliest honest whisper of decline. It arrives long before the results, and it does not politely reverse itself the way a run of bad luck does. When Paul Skenes’ fastball slid from near ninety-nine to under ninety-seven over three seasons, that was not the dice turning against him. That was the one part of the ledger that cannot lie, telling the truth early.

So the reader’s question — can you predict a decline before the outcomes show it? — has a clean answer: yes, but only from the top of the ladder. You predict with the numbers that stabilize fast, because those are the ones already made mostly of skill. You do not predict from the bottom — from ERA, from won-lost record, from the hits that happened to fall — because those are still mostly luck and will drift back on their own. The companion piece was right that Skenes’ ugly ERA is noise headed for reversion. The reader was right that his velocity is signal headed nowhere good. Both can be true at once, because they live on opposite ends of the same ladder.

The numbers that revert can’t warn you, and the numbers that warn you don’t revert. To see a decline coming, read only the second kind.

The rule that travels

Strip the baseball off and a habit of mind is left that works on any number anyone ever waves at you. Before you believe a statistic, ask the quiet question underneath it: how much of this is there, against how much it needs? A hitter batting .400 in April is a ghost — batting average needs the better part of a thousand at-bats to mean much, and he has had forty. A stock up thirty percent this quarter, a poll that moved three points, a website’s conversion rate over a slow Tuesday: all the same trap, a small pour of a slow-stabilizing number mistaken for a finished truth. The discipline is not to distrust all numbers. It is to know, for each one, how much you are allowed to believe yet — and, when you want to see what’s coming, to keep your eye on the measures that firm up first. In baseball that measure is bolted to a radar gun behind home plate, and it was reading Paul Skenes correctly while the box score was still arguing with itself.

Notes & sources

The stabilization points are from the sabermetric work of Russell Carleton, popularized by FanGraphs and Baseball Prospectus: the sample at which a statistic correlates about 0.7 with a separate, equally-sized sample of the same player — roughly the point at which half its variation is repeatable skill rather than luck. For pitchers (measured in batters faced or balls in play): strikeout rate about 70, walk rate about 170, batting average against about 630, home-run rate about 1,320, and BABIP against about 2,000 — the last effectively unreachable within a season. Exact values vary slightly by study and metric definition.

Velocity is not one of Carleton’s plate-appearance stabilization stats because it is measured per pitch, not per outcome; it is shown here at roughly one start to place it on the same axis, but its reliability is higher still — a pitcher’s average fastball velocity is trustworthy within a few dozen pitches, faster than any results-based number. The Skenes velocity figures (four-seam averages of about 98.8, 98.2 and 96.9 mph across 2024–2026) are from MLB Stats API pitch-arsenal data, and were the subject of the companion issue on his 2026 season.
