We Blamed the New Pitcher. We Were Wrong.

After a manager pulls a pitcher in the middle of an inning, 66 runs score per 100 changes. An average inning gives 50. But the new man walks into the jam the old one made, and that jam was worth 67. Once you count the jam, the change itself adds nothing, in the 5th or the 9th.

Do runs go up after a pitching change? You have seen it. The manager walks out, the new man warms up, and two batters later the inning is on fire. Our editor bet it happens most in the 4th to 6th, when the middle relievers come in.

Here is the answer, with its number. We looked at every pitching change from 2023 to 2025: 13,966 made in the middle of an inning. For every 100 of them, 66 runs scored before the inning ended. An average inning gives 50. So yes, runs go up.

Now the surprise. The base runners and outs he inherited were worth 67 runs per 100 on their own. The change added nothing. Our editor was wrong, and so is the feeling. By the end you will be able to spot this trap anywhere someone is sent in to fix a mess.

Why the Fire Was Already Burning

Think about when a manager makes the walk. Nobody pulls a pitcher who just got two quick outs. He gets pulled because men are on base and the hitters are squaring him up. In our data, 8 in 10 mid-inning changes came with at least one runner already on.

So comparing those innings with an average inning is unfair. You picked them because trouble was coming. That is conditioning on the consequence, and it makes almost any rescue look like the cause of the damage.

The fair question is this: given the outs and the runners he walked into, did he allow more than an average pitcher would? Baseball has a ready yardstick. For each of the 24 outs-and-bases spots, we counted how many runs usually score from there to the end of the inning, using the same three seasons. Bases loaded, nobody out is worth about 2.4 runs. A man on first with two out is worth about a quarter of a run.

Look at the pairs. In every part of the game, the runs that scored sit right beside what the spot was worth. In the 4th to 6th, the editor’s window, it was 67 against 68, from 6,239 changes. The 8th and 9th look the same. What makes the red bars tall is the dashed line below them: those innings were never average innings.

The One Real Effect Is Small, and It Points the Other Way

There is a second kind of change: a fresh reliever who starts an inning clean. Those pitchers start where every inning starts, so the comparison is fair from the first pitch. In the 7th, 8th and 9th they allowed about 48 runs per 100 innings, against 50 for an average inning. That is 2 or 3 fewer runs every 100 innings. It is real, but you would never notice it from the couch.

The new pitcher did not light the fire. He was sent in because it was already burning.

— The Sports Page

What to Take Home

When somebody is sent in to fix a mess, do not judge them against a normal day. Judge them against the mess. Ask what the situation was already worth, then ask whether they did better or worse than that.

This works off the field too. A new doctor gets the sickest patients; a turnaround boss gets the failing store. Their numbers will look worse than average almost every time. Hold them to the spot they walked into, not to the spot you wish they had.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Data: play-by-play for all 7,289 regular-season games of 2023–2025 from the MLB Stats API, the pitch-clock seasons. A change is any plate appearance whose pitcher differs from the one before in the same half-inning (13,966), or a new pitcher starting a half-inning (28,513).

The yardstick: for each of the 24 outs-and-bases spots, the average runs scored from that spot to the end of the half-inning, over all 123,861 complete regulation half-innings. Ranges are 95% bootstrap ranges over whole games. Mid-inning changes overall: runs minus the yardstick was −0.4 per 100 (−2.2 to +1.5). In the 4th to 6th: −0.6 (−3.5 to +2.3). Starting the 8th: −2.6 (−4.5 to −0.6).

What it cannot do: extra innings and the bottom of the 9th are left out, because the free runner and walk-offs change the rules. The yardstick is for an average pitcher against an average lineup; late relievers often face the top of the order. A runner who steals before the new man’s first pitch counts against him.
