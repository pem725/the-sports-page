Everybody Stopped Hitting. One Team Did Not.

Three of the four clubs left alive are scoring more than a run a game below their own regular-season rate. The fourth is scoring more than it did all summer — and it is the one that won the fewest games. October does something to offense, and measuring it against the right yardstick changes who looks like they are collapsing.

Is the best team in baseball collapsing, or is this just what October looks like? Los Angeles is scoring 1.78 fewer runs a game than it did all summer, and the temptation is to call that a collapse.

Then you check the other three. Milwaukee is down 1.47. Tampa Bay is down 1.29. Three of the four clubs still alive are scoring more than a run a game below their own regular-season rate — which means the thing happening to the Dodgers is happening to nearly everyone, and most people would have called it a Dodgers story.

By the end of this you will have the habit that separates those two readings, and it costs us one extra column of arithmetic. Watch what it does to the standings.

Measure a Team Against Itself

A raw postseason number tells you almost nothing. The clubs left in October are not a random sample, and neither are the pitchers they face. Compare each club against its own summer instead.

Do that and the ranking inverts. Cleveland is scoring 0.81 runs a game more than it managed over six months — the only club in the final four that improved. And Cleveland had the worst record of the four at 85–77.

Everyone else got worse. The Guardians got better. That is the story, and the raw scoreboard hides it.

Three clubs are hitting worse than they did all year. The one that is hitting better won the fewest games.

— The Sports Page

Milwaukee Is Not the Underdog

Stop here a moment, because the framing everywhere is Brewers-as-upstart against the two-time champions, and we have been repeating it too.

Milwaukee went 103–59. Los Angeles went 100–62. The Brewers scored 5.14 runs a game, the Dodgers 4.94. Milwaukee was the better team in the regular season by record and by offense, and they are up two games to none.

That is not an upset. It is the better club winning, dressed up as a surprise because one of them has rings and the other does not. A reputation is not a priori you should be using.

About Ohtani

He is 3 for 20 with no extra-base hits and no runs batted in. His slugging is .150 against a regular-season mark of .512, which is another way of saying all three hits were singles.

That is bad, and you are right to notice it. But do the arithmetic before we build a theory on it. For a .275 hitter, going 3-for-20 or worse has about a one in six chance in any given stretch. He had roughly twenty-six separate twenty-at-bat windows this season, and you would expect about four of them to be this bad.

So a slump this deep is close to an annual event for him. The only unusual thing is where it landed. October did not break Shohei Ohtani; October is just the stretch where everybody is watching.

What to take home

When a number looks alarming, ask what it should be compared with, and prefer the subject's own history over anybody else's. “Los Angeles is scoring 3.17” means nothing. “Los Angeles is scoring 1.78 below its own rate, in a month where almost everyone is down more than a run” means something, and it means something different.

The same move rescues Ohtani from a narrative and hands Cleveland the credit nobody is giving them.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Regular-season records and run totals from the MLB Stats API as of the final day, 27 September. Postseason rates from every completed game through 12 October: six each for Milwaukee, Los Angeles and Cleveland, four for Tampa Bay.

These are tiny samples and the piece does not pretend otherwise. Four to six games cannot establish that a club has changed; it can only establish what has happened so far. Cleveland’s +0.81 could evaporate in two nights. The point is not that the Guardians have become a better offense — it is that the comparison most people are making, against nothing at all, is worse than the comparison against a club’s own season.

Nor does this explain WHY October scoring falls. Shorter rotations, better bullpens pitching earlier, colder weather and tighter umpiring have all been offered. This measures the drop; it does not diagnose it.
