18 Interceptions One Year. 6 the Next. Same Quarterback.

Josh Allen threw 18 interceptions in 2023 and 6 in 2024. Across every NFL starter since 2018, a quarterback’s interception rate barely carries over from one season to the next. His scrambles do, almost exactly.
By The Sports Page · The Professor · Lesson: how much of a number is the player?
.18
how much a QB’s interception rate repeats next season (.02–.32)
.80
how much his scrambles repeat (.74–.85)
187
pairs of seasons by the same starter, 2018–2025

The question: does a quarterback’s interception total tell you how careless he is?

The answer: barely. Measure the same quarterback in two seasons in a row, and his interception rate repeats at .18. On this scale, 1 means it repeats perfectly and 0 means not at all. His scrambles repeat at .80.

Why that is surprising: an interception feels like the most personal mistake in football. He threw it; nobody else did. So you would expect the careless passers to stay careless. Mostly, they do not.

What you get: one question to ask of any stat: how much does it repeat? The answer tells you how much of the number is the player, and how far to pull it back toward average.

What a repeat rate means

Step on a bathroom scale, step off, step on again. A good scale gives you nearly the same number twice. A cheap one jumps around, and you learn to trust it less.

A stat works the same way. Take every starter who threw at least 150 passes in two straight seasons, and line up year one against year two. If the good ones stay good and the poor ones stay poor, the stat repeats. If the order shuffles, most of what you saw was the season, not the man.

Here is every quarterback stat we tested, from the most repeatable to the least.

Notice what sits at the top: scrambles and time to throw. Those are style. A quarterback who likes to hold the ball and run holds it and runs every year. Notice what sits at the bottom: interceptions, all alone.

Why interceptions shuffle

There are not many of them. A starter throws about nine in a season. Two tipped balls, one receiver who slips, one heave at the end of a half, and a careful passer has a careless-looking year.

They do not belong to any skill we could find. When we sorted every quarterback stat into groups, accuracy formed one group, moving the chains formed another, and holding the ball formed a third. Interceptions did not join any of them. A pick is partly the throw, partly the receiver, and partly the defender who guessed right.

Josh Allen shows it in one career. From 2021 to 2025 he threw 15, 14, 18, 6 and 10 interceptions, on 460 to 650 passes a season. His scrambles in those seasons: 46, 51, 48, 43 and 48. Same arm, same legs. One number wandered and one held still.

The interception is the most personal mistake in football, and the least personal number.

— The Sports Page

How far to pull it back

A repeat rate tells you how much to trust the gap between a player and the average. Multiply the gap by the repeat rate, and that is what you should expect next year.

The typical NFL starter throws about 2.2 interceptions per 100 passes. Allen’s 2023 rate was 3.1. The gap is about 0.9, and 0.9 times .18 is about 0.2. So the best guess for his 2024 was about 2.3 per 100: barely worse than average, not a turnover machine. He came in at 1.2. The bad year was mostly the year.

The voters do not do this. Take an All-Pro quarterback and a starter who was not, from any season since 2018, and bet on the one who threw fewer picks per pass. You win that bet about 86 times in 100. The voters lean on the most wobbly number on the sheet.

Not every skill is as steady as scrambling. Accuracy and moving the chains repeat at about .45: half signal, half everything else. A quarterback shares his numbers with his line, his receivers and his play-caller, and they change from year to year too.

What to take home

Ask how much a stat repeats before you judge a player on it. A number that repeats at .8 is mostly the player. A number that repeats at .2 is mostly the season.

Do not judge a quarterback on one season of interceptions. Pull the number most of the way back to average, and look at what repeats: how accurate he is and how often he moves the chains.

Style repeats better than results. How a player plays, holding the ball, running, throwing deep, comes back every year. What happens to the ball afterward depends on everybody else too.

How we did it

Data. NFL regular seasons 2018–2025. Quarterbacks with at least 200 dropbacks and 150 passes in a season: 294 seasons. They make 187 pairs of back-to-back seasons by the same player. Counts come from nflverse play-by-play by player id (a dropback is a pass, a sack or a scramble; spikes are left out). Time to throw and completions over expected come from Next Gen Stats; bad throws and throwaways from Pro Football Reference charting. Those are one feed each.

Second feed. ESPN’s season stats for the same players give an interception repeat rate of .17 (.03–.31), against our .18. For touchdown passes, both give .38. Josh Allen’s interceptions match on both feeds in every season from 2021 to 2025; their pass counts differ by a few. His scramble counts come from nflverse alone, since ESPN does not separate scrambles from designed runs. League interception rate: 2.2 per 100 passes over our 294 seasons.

The repeat rate is the correlation between a stat in one season and the same stat the next season. Intervals are 95%, by Fisher’s z, and cover chance only. The groups of stats come from a factor analysis, confirmed on a second half of the players. All-Pro lists, 2018–2025, are from Wikipedia (one source); the “86 in 100” is the area under the curve for fewer interceptions.

Limits. Only quarterbacks who kept a job for two seasons are in the pairs, so the worst ones are missing, which can shrink a repeat rate. A team change between seasons counts as the same player. Code and full results: research/constructs/football/qb_wr/ in our repository.
