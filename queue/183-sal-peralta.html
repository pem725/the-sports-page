The Mets Let Him Go. Then He Got Worse. Then He Won the Pennant.

For four starts in Tampa his ERA was 7.2. Then came ten outings with five earned runs, an ALCS MVP, and a World Series against the team that traded him to the Mets. The gods did not move him. August did.
By Sal · Guest Columnist

Ah yes. The Mets.

Did the Mets trade away a great pitcher, or did they trade away a bad month? Every Mets fan asked it this week, the way Mets fans ask everything. Which god did we anger? What does it want? Some even had a theory: the ball through Buckner’s legs.

We went through every outing he made this year, so here is the answer, with its number: the trade fixed nothing. In his first four starts as a Ray, Freddy Peralta gave up 17 earned runs in 21 innings. That is worse than anything he did in Queens. The magic, such as it is, started three weeks later and has lasted ten outings. That is the surprise. You were told a story about a change of scenery. The scenery changed, and for a month he got worse.

By the end of this you will know what to say the next time your team sells low, which, if you root for the Mets, will be soon.

The Season, One Start at a Time

Let us set the scene, since the gods clearly did. In January the Mets sent two young players to Milwaukee for Peralta, who had gone 17–6 with a 2.70 ERA the year before. He was theirs for one season. He made 22 starts in orange and blue and posted a 4.99 ERA. (If you are keeping score at home, that is the number a fan remembers; it is also the one he should not.) On August 2, with the club at 47–66, the Mets sent him to Tampa for three minor leaguers.

Look at the first blue bars. Seven runs to the Rockies. Five to the Orioles. If the gods were punishing Flushing, they had an odd way of rewarding Tampa. Then, from late August on, the bars nearly vanish: 34 innings and three earned runs to end the season, one of them against — of course — the Mets. Then 17 innings in October with two more. Then the ALCS MVP.

The gods did not take Freddy Peralta from the Mets. They took him from August, and the Mets happened to own August.

What Changed, and What Merely Bounced

Some of it is real. As a Ray he walks fewer men, about 7 in 100 batters against 9 in New York. He gives up fewer home runs. Notice what he does not do: strike out more. He strikes out fewer, about 20 in 100 against 22 as a Met, and both are well under the 30 he struck out over his Milwaukee years.

The rest is the ball in play. As a Met, hitters batted about .31 when they put the ball in play. As a Ray, about .23. His career mark is about .27. That is the kind of number that bounces; a pitcher has some say in it, but his fielders and the hops have more. Two-thirds of a great run, it turns out, is often the fielders and the hops.

And the sample is small. Ten outings is about 50 innings. You would not judge a restaurant by one dinner, and a pitcher’s ERA does not settle down for well over a hundred innings. Fifty good innings tell you he is not broken. They do not tell you he was fixed.

As for Buckner

Now, about that theory. On October 25, 1986, Mookie Wilson’s slow roller went through Bill Buckner’s legs, the Mets won Game 6, and two nights later they won the World Series. It is said that the gods keep accounts. If so, I have looked at the ledger, and I must report that the Buckner ball is entered on the Mets’ side as a GIFT. One does not anger the gods by accepting a present. Boston had the grievance, and Boston collected four titles since.

The Mets’ trouble is plainer and, I am sorry to say, cheaper to fix. They sold a pitcher at the bottom of his slump. That is regression to the mean, and it does not care who signs your checks: a pitcher who has just been worse than his talent is likely to be better next, wherever he goes. The Mets sold the dip and the Rays bought the bounce.

What to take home

When a player leaves your team and turns into a star, check two things before you light a candle. First, look at his first month in the new uniform. If he was bad there too, the new team did not fix him; time did. Second, count the innings. Fifty is a hot streak. Five hundred is a pitcher.

And to the gods, should they be reading: the Mets have suffered enough, though I suspect they will check the arithmetic first.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Every 2026 outing is from the MLB Stats API game logs, regular season and postseason. His Mets and Rays lines were checked against the same feed’s season splits. The trades and the return are from the MLB transaction log and Associated Press reporting. The ALCS MVP is from MLB’s awards record. “Ball in play” is hits minus home runs, divided by batters faced minus strikeouts, walks, hit batters and home runs. That is close to the usual definition, not identical.

What this cannot tell you: whether Tampa changed something real, like a pitch mix or a grip, that a box score cannot see. The fewer walks and home runs hint that they may have. Nor can it say what he would have done in New York in September. Nobody gets to see that game.
