The Playoff Has One Favorite and Eleven Coin Flips

We played out the rest of the college season and the 12-team bracket 40,000 times. Georgia won the title in about 34 of every 100. Nobody else got past 12, and most of the other contenders are close to even with each other.

Who wins the national title, and how much better is No. 2 than No. 11? The committee put out its new top 25 on Tuesday. We took it, played out the last two weekends, the title games and the bracket 40,000 times, and counted.

The answer: Georgia wins about 34 times in 100. It is the clear favorite, and it still loses two times in three. No other team tops 12 in 100. Below Georgia the field is close to a pile of coin flips. Pick any two of the other eleven teams in the top 12 and put them on a neutral field; 46 of those 55 games are 60–40 or closer.

Why that is surprising: a ranking looks like a ladder, and No. 2 sounds a long way above No. 11. In the ratings it is not. Wake Forest, at No. 11, rates higher than Utah at No. 2. What you get: a way to read the bracket on Selection Day, and a reason to stop treating a seed as a forecast.

Why the favorite usually loses

Georgia is the best team by a wide margin. SP+ is a rating in points: how much better than an average team a club plays. Georgia sits at 31. Notre Dame is next at 26, and the rest of the top 12 run from 18 to 24.

Against any of the other eleven, Georgia would win between 64 and 79 games in 100. Call it 7 in 10. Now notice what a top-four seed has to do: skip the first round, then win three straight. Seven in 10, three times in a row, is about 1 in 3. A team that wins most of its games still loses most of its tournaments. You are watching what a short bracket does, and it would do the same to any favorite.

The middle of the bracket is flat

Look at seeds 5 through 12 as the committee has them today, and ask yourself how far apart they really are. The best rates 26 and the worst rates 18; that is about 8 points. One college game lands about 17 points away from its expected margin on a normal day. So the luck in a single game is about twice the widest gap in the middle of this bracket.

You can see it in the first round, played on the higher seed’s campus. Seed today’s top 12 straight down the list and Notre Dame beats Wake Forest 63 times in 100. Miami beats BYU 71 times, and Indiana beats Tennessee 60. Home field is worth only a few of those points.

The ranking is not wrong; it is doing a different job. The committee rewards what a team has done, and Utah and UCLA are unbeaten. By rating, though, they are the 8th and 10th best of the twelve. If the season ended today, both would still skip the first round. Here is the part you should hold onto: in 37 of every 100 of our seasons, the champion played in the first round.

Two weekends still move the field

The bracket is not set. Ohio State is 14th in the ranking but rates fourth best in the country; it makes the field only about 26 times in 100. Boise State, 17th, is unbeaten in Pac-12 play. That makes it the likeliest team to take the spot saved for the best champion outside the four big conferences. It gets in about 67 times in 100 and wins it all about 2 times in 1,000. Texas Tech, holding No. 12 today, gets in only about 45 times in 100.

What to take home

A ranking puts teams in order. It does not tell you how far apart they are. Before you ask “who is ahead?”, ask “by how much?” This year the answer is one team, then a crowd. When your bracket comes out on Selection Day and No. 2 looks safe against No. 11, remember that the rungs on that ladder are about a field goal apart, and one Saturday’s luck is bigger than that.

How we measured it

The format. From the College Football Playoff’s own site. Five conference champions get bids: the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12 and SEC champions, plus the highest-ranked champion of the American, Conference USA, MAC, Mountain West, Pac-12 or Sun Belt. Seven at-large teams fill the rest. Notre Dame gets in if it is ranked in the top 12. The top four are seeded 1–4 and get byes. Seeds 5–12 play on the higher seed’s campus. A champion ranked outside the top 12 goes to the bottom of the seeding, and there is no reseeding.

The model. Win chance comes from the SP+ gap through a normal curve with a spread of 16.5 points, plus 2.5 points for the home team. We simulate every remaining regular-season game, send the top two in each conference to its title game, then rank the teams. Our stand-in for the committee starts from Tuesday’s order. Each new loss drops a team about six places (half that for a title-game loss), and conference champions move up a little. That part is judgment. Doubling or halving the penalty moves Georgia only between 32 and 35 in 100.

Limits. Ratings are held fixed, so a team that gets hot or hurt is not followed; that makes the favorites look a bit surer than they are. Conference tiebreakers are settled at random among tied teams. Records were checked against both ESPN and CollegeFootballData. Code and data: research/cfp-committee/coinflip.
