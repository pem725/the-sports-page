Notre Dame Gets Talked About All Summer. So Does Everybody Else.

In a dead June, Notre Dame draws 18,884 Wikipedia lookups. In a live October it draws 70,836. That is a floor of 27%. Across twelve blue bloods the median floor is 26.8%. Every program keeps the same share of its attention when nothing at all is happening.

The question: every Notre Dame fan believes the Irish get talked about all year, however small the news. Are they right?

The answer: yes, and it is not special at all. In the dead months of March to June, Notre Dame holds 27% of the attention it gets from September to December. The median across twelve blue-blood programs is 26.8%. Notre Dame ranks seventh of twelve.

Here is the surprising part. How much attention a program gets varies a lot; Alabama’s in-season months run 2.6 times Nebraska’s. The shape hardly varies at all. Every ratio lands between 21% and 31%, and whether a program is big or small predicts nothing about it (r = +0.01).

What you get from it: a way to tell real distinctiveness from the ordinary feeling of caring a lot.

What we measured, and why this one

You cannot count hype. So we need a behaviour that stands in for it, and Wikipedia lookups are a good one; somebody had a thought about Notre Dame and went to read about it. Nobody looks a thing up by accident.

So take twelve programs, five and a half years of monthly pageviews, and split the calendar in two. September through December is the season. March through June is the dead zone. No games, no playoff, nothing but recruiting talk and spring practice.

Divide one by the other and you get a floor: the share of a program’s attention that survives having nothing to report.

Look at the bars and the markers one at a time; they say opposite things.

The bars spread out: Alabama draws about 87,000 lookups in a median in-season month, Nebraska about 33,000. That is a real 2.6-fold gap, and it is roughly what you would guess.

Now look at where the marker sits inside each bar. It sits in the same place every time. Twenty-one percent at the low end, thirty-one at the high, and ten of the twelve between 25 and 29.

Notre Dame sits at 27%, which is the middle of the middle.

Why we expected otherwise

The belief starts from something true. Notre Dame does get covered in June, and you have seen it. What the belief adds, with no evidence, is the word only.

And we cannot see the comparison, because we do not spend our Junes reading about Nebraska. You notice your own team’s small news because you are the one reading it. Eleven other fan bases are having the same experience about their own club, and all of it is invisible to you.

When something feels distinctive, check whether you are measuring the thing or measuring your own attention to it.

What to take home: distinctiveness needs a comparison group

This is a mistake that goes well past football. A thing feels unusual, and we never gather the group it would have to be unusual against.

Your commute, your job, your family, your team’s die-hards. All of them feel unusual from the inside; most sit within a point or two of the middle.

So before you call a thing unusual, ask the dull question. Unusual next to whom, and did anybody count them?

Notes & sources

Pageviews are English Wikipedia, user traffic only (bots excluded), monthly, January 2021 through August 2026, from the Wikimedia REST pageviews API. The article for each program is its football page, not the university page. In-season is the median of all September–December months; dead season is the median of all March–June months; the floor is the ratio. Medians rather than means, because a playoff run puts a long tail on the in-season months.

Floors: Nebraska 30.9%, Texas 29.1%, USC 28.2%, Clemson 27.8%, Ohio State 27.5%, Alabama 27.0%, Notre Dame 26.7%, Michigan 26.5%, Georgia 26.4%, Penn State 25.5%, LSU 25.1%, Oregon 20.8%. Correlation between a program’s in-season level and its floor: r = +0.01, 95% CI −0.57 to +0.58, i.e. nothing.

One limit worth stating. This measures people looking a program up, which is not the same behaviour as watching a video about it. The specific claim that Notre Dame’s YouTube ecosystem is unlike anyone else’s is untested here — YouTube publishes view counts only through an API key we do not hold, and their robots file puts the feeds out of bounds. It remains a live question, not a settled one.
