A Poll That Never Existed. A Backtest That Cheated. Both Ours.

We caught ourselves twice this week. One was a ranking we cited before it was published — it did not exist. The other was our own pick model returning a perfect 15 for 15, which is not a triumph but a symptom. Here is how both got through, and what changed.

What We Got Right

The Wins

Be honest about the shape of this week. Four of the six issues carried no forecast at all. They reported base rates, history, and a bias measured from results. A scorecard cannot grade those, and pretending otherwise would be the exact vice this section exists to resist. If you came for a win-loss record this week, you are owed the reason there is not much of one.

The one call we will stand behind is the week-one signal in #165. We expected the effect to be talent in disguise — good teams win openers, good teams win seasons. It survived inside every talent band, bottom quarter to top, at 1.4 to 1.9 wins. That is the pattern holding where we most expected it to dissolve. Watch it through October; if it survives a full season you can use it.

What We Got Wrong (or Aren’t Sure About)

The Misses and Uncertainties

We cited a poll that had not happened. Last Sunday’s edition led with South Carolina climbing to No. 10 in the AP poll. There was no poll. Week one ran through Labor Day, so the first in-season AP rankings did not appear until the Tuesday, and when they did South Carolina was not in them.

Notice what made it survive. Every fact around it was true — the 57–0 score, the opponent, our own ranking of them at 33rd. A fabrication wrapped in accurate context is far harder to catch than an invented story, because everything you check checks out. Notice how little protection your own scepticism gives you there. The edition was assembled before dawn and asked a search engine what the new poll said, and a search summariser will answer that question whether or not the poll exists.

The fix is not to be more careful; careful is what failed, and you should distrust anyone who offers care as a remedy for it. Nothing may now grade a forecast unless it traces to a primary feed, no ranking may be cited before its scheduled release, and check_poll_claims.py tests every ranking claim in a draft against the CollegeFootballData feed before it can publish.

Then our own model lied to us. We backtested the pick method on a week already played and it went 15 for 15 against the spread, in the right order of confidence. The correlation between our edge and the actual result was +0.842, where an honest pregame model sits near +0.10. The ratings we fed it were current ones, updated after the games had been played. We had noticed that drift ourselves two days earlier and used them anyway.

Rebuilt so that cheating was structurally impossible, the same method wins 51.0% of 2,140 games. Break-even is 52.4%. The interval runs 48.9% to 53.1% and contains 50. Thursday’s issue is that result in full.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

\g<0>

Under-Reactions

We under-reacted to our own 15-for-15. The first instinct was to check the arithmetic, not the data lineage, and the arithmetic was fine. It took measuring the correlation at +0.842 to see it. A perfect score should have been the alarm on sight, not after the second pass.

What to take home, and the road ahead

What to take home: be suspicious of your best result. A number that comes back better than it has any right to be deserves the same scrutiny as one that comes back wrong, and it almost never gets it. Ask what your data knew, and when it knew it. If a rating updates, it has read the newspaper; if a survey was scored after the outcome, it saw the answer. The past is remarkably easy to predict once it has already happened.

Wednesday, where football talent actually comes from — more than five thousand American high schools produced nobody. Thursday, the backtest in full.
