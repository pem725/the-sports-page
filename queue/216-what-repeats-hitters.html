Speed Comes Back Every Year. Half a Batting Average Does Not.

Line up a hitter’s numbers from one season against the next, and they do not come back equally. How fast he runs comes back almost all the way. How often he strikes out comes back most of the way. His batting average comes back about halfway. The other half was never really his.

The question: when a hitter has a big year, how much of it is really him?

The answer: it depends on which number you look at. Some numbers come back almost whole the next season. Sprint speed comes back .95 of the way, on a scale where 1 means all of it. Strikeouts come back .83. Batting average comes back only .47, and OPS .49.

Why that is surprising: the back of a baseball card treats every number the same way. Same type, same size, same line. Most fans read them all as the player. But the numbers we print biggest, batting average and OPS, are the ones that come back least.

What you get: one number for any stat, its repeat rate. It tells you how much of a season is the player. And it tells you how far to pull a hot or cold year back toward average before you bet on it.

Shoe size and green lights

Think about two numbers in your own life. Your shoe size this year tells you your shoe size next year almost exactly. It is you. How many green lights you hit on the way to work this week tells you almost nothing about next week. You drove the same way. The lights did the rest.

Most sports numbers sit somewhere between those two. Here is how to place them. Take every hitter who came to the plate 300 times in back-to-back seasons, from 2015 through 2025. That is 1,625 pairs of seasons. For each number, ask one question: did the hitters who were high on it one year stay high the next? The answer runs from 0, no pull at all, to 1, every hitter in the same place.

Read the chart from the top. Notice that the things a hitter does sit high. How fast he runs. How often he swings and misses. Whether he chases pitches out of the zone. How hard he hits the ball. Those are his shoe size.

Now look at the red dots at the bottom. On-base percentage, OPS and batting average are not one thing. Each is a mix of several, and one part of the mix is where the ball happens to land. A hard line drive can find a glove. A weak flare can drop in. That part does not come back.

And notice doubles, at .25. A double is often a single that rolled into the gap, or a fly ball the outfielder just missed. Of all the hits, it is the most green light.

The numbers we print biggest are the ones that come back least.

Bryce Harper’s two seasons

In 2015 Bryce Harper won the National League MVP. He got 33 hits for every 100 at-bats. In 2016 he got 24. That looks like a different player.

Look at his strikeouts instead. In 2015 he struck out on 20 of every 100 trips to the plate. In 2016, 19. The part of Harper that comes back every year came back. The part that depends on where the ball lands did not.

Here is how to use a repeat rate yourself. Take how far a player was from average. Multiply by the repeat rate. Add that to average, and you have a fair guess for next year. The average hitter in 2015 got about 26 hits per 100 at-bats, so Harper was 7 above. Half of 7 is about 3. So a fair guess for his 2016 was about 30. He got 24, worse than the guess. Luck runs both ways, and a guess only says where to start.

Do the same with his strikeouts and almost nothing changes, because strikeouts repeat .83 of the way. That is what a high repeat rate buys you: a number you can trust next year.

What to take home

Judge a hitter by what he controls. Speed, swings and misses, chases and how hard he hits the ball all come back. Lean on those when you size up a player, and treat batting average as part skill and part weather.

Pull every number toward average by its repeat rate. A stat that repeats .47 of the way keeps about half of its distance from average. Most of a hot streak in batting average is the half that goes away. We wrote earlier about how many tries a stat needs before it settles, in Issue #32. This is the other side of the coin: even after a full season, some numbers are still mostly luck.

Big type is not the same as big signal. The more things a number mixes together, the more room luck has to get in. For more, see our primers on regression to the mean and signal and noise.

How we did it

Who. Every hitter with 300 or more trips to the plate in a season, 2015–2025 (the 2020 bar scaled to its 60 games): 3,048 seasons. A pair is the same hitter in back-to-back seasons; pairs that touch 2020 are left out. That leaves 1,625 pairs.

The repeat rate is the correlation between one season and the next across those pairs. The 95% intervals use the standard Fisher method. Counting stats are taken per trip to the plate (and square-rooted, which steadies a rate). Batting average .47 (.43–.51); OPS .49 (.46–.53); on-base .54 (.50–.57); strikeouts .83 (.82–.85); walks .71; home runs .64; singles .64; doubles .25 (.20–.30).

Two feeds. Every box-score number here comes from the MLB Stats API and was recomputed from the Lahman database; the repeat rates match to the hundredth, pair for pair. Harper’s seasons match on both feeds (654 and 627 trips to the plate). Sprint speed (.95), swings and misses (.88), chases (.84) and exit velocity (.77) come from Baseball Savant alone. That is one feed. We publish only these summary figures, never the per-player data.

Limits. The same hitter can appear in several pairs, so the intervals are a little too narrow. Only hitters who kept their jobs are in the pairs, and players who slumped out of the lineup are missing. The “fair guess” rule assumes the spread of a stat is about the same each year, which holds closely here. Code: research/constructs/baseball-validation/ in our repository.
