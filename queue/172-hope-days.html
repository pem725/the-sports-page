The Mets Got One Day of Hope. The Cubs Got 126.

Which is worse — a season that was over by June, or one that carried you to October and then died in forty-eight hours? We can measure half of that question. Add up a club's playoff probability across every day it was alive and the Cubs banked 126 days of real hope. The Mets banked one. Both finished with nothing.

Which season would you rather have lived through? One that was settled before the Fourth of July, or one that carried you all the way to October and then ended inside forty-eight hours?

You can measure half of that. Since April this paper has stored every club's playoff probability on a weekly grid. Add those probabilities up across the days they cover and you get a number: hope-days. From 15 April to 26 September the Cubs banked 126 of them. The Mets banked one.

Most people assume the Cubs fan got the worse deal, because the ending was so much uglier. Look again. The ending was identical. Both clubs finished with no championship, same as twenty-eight others. Everything that actually differed between those two seasons happened in the middle. By the end of this you will have a way to price any season you have sat through — by its area, not by how it stopped.

What a Hope-Day Is

Hold a 50% chance for a hundred days and you have had fifty hope-days. That is the same belief fifty days of certainty would have given you. It is just the area under the curve of your club's playoff odds. Nothing clever. But it measures the thing a season hands you day by day, instead of the thing you remember about it afterward.

The ceiling is 164, because that is how many days our grid covers. The Braves came closest at 162 — they were never once in doubt. Here is what the two clubs in question actually received.

Notice what the Cubs curve does in June. It falls to 23%. For about three weeks that season looked finished, and then it climbed back and sat above 95% from the second week of August onward. That recovery is not a footnote. It is more hope, not less: they got the collapse and the comeback, and both of them counted.

The Mets curve does not do anything. It opens at 7% on 15 April and reaches a hard zero by 24 June. Your instinct said July. The arithmetic says the Mets were mathematically finished a week before the All-Star rosters were announced, and functionally finished in April.

Hope is not the same thing as winning. The Mets won sixteen more games than the Rockies and ended up with less of it.

The Ending Was the Only Part That Matched

That pullquote is the strangest number in the file. Colorado lost 103 games. New York won 73, sixteen more, and still finished dead last in hope-days — one, against Colorado's two. The Mets' highest playoff probability all season was 7%, the lowest peak of any club in baseball.

That is the whole lesson in one line. Wins you collect while nobody is watching the standings do not convert into anything. Hope needs a race to live in.

Meanwhile the Cubs went to San Diego and lost 8–0 and 4–1. One run in two games, and out. It was a miserable way to finish and it is the part everyone will remember.

Why Your Memory Disagrees With the Arithmetic

Your memory is not lying to you; it is summarizing. People retrieve an experience by two things: its most intense moment and its final one. The peak-end rule, it is called. By that accounting the Cubs season is a disaster, because the end was an annihilation and it is the freshest thing in the file.

But peak-end is a rule about recall, not about what happened. The sum — what you actually lived through, hour by hour — says something else entirely. Normally these two measures agree enough that nobody notices. Here they disagree as hard as they can. One season held a hundred times the hope of the other, and both finished in the same place.

So the honest answer to "which fan is happier" depends on a question nobody asks out loud: what are you adding up? That is an objective functioni problem, and almost every argument about fandom is really an unstated fight over which one counts.

Pick the ending and you want the Mets season, because at least it did not ambush you. Pick the area and it is not close. The Cubs fan got 126 days of a thing worth having. None of it gets clawed back on elimination night. You do not get a refund on the vacation because the flight home was bad.

What We Cannot Tell You

Four things, and the first one cuts against us hardest.

Our grid starts on 15 April, so opening-day optimism is not in these numbers. Every club begins a season somewhere near its forecast, and for a club that collapses early that lost hope was real and we did not count it. The Mets almost certainly started March with more than one day's worth. Read every figure here as "from mid-April onward."

Second, probability is not feeling. Fans are not calibrated and do not want to be. Somebody sitting at 2% may believe completely; that belief is most of what being a supporter is. We measured what the standings delivered, not what anyone experienced. Third, the grid is weekly, so a crash and recovery inside a single week is invisible to us. And fourth, playoff odds are only one kind of hope — a club out of the race can still sell you a rookie, a chase, a rivalry night.

What to take home

When you catch yourself grading a season, a job, or a year by how it ended, stop and ask what the area under it looked like. Endings are vivid and they are one data point. The sum is almost always the larger number, and it is the one you actually lived.

And the next time somebody tells you a disappointing season was wasted, ask them what the alternative was. For 2026 the alternative is sitting right there, in the same division race, with one hope-day to its name.
