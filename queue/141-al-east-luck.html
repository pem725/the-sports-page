Tampa Bay Leads the AL East by Six. By Runs Scored, They Are Third.

The Rays have outscored their opponents by 47 runs. Both clubs chasing them sit near +100. The team in first is the third-best team in its own division — and it is going to win the thing anyway.

The question: how can the best team in a division have almost no chance of winning it?

The answer: because a deficit is a fact and superiority is only a rate. Boston is genuinely the better club. That edge pays out at 0.046 games per game, and six games of arrears takes 131 games to clear.

Here is the surprising part. We read a standings table as a ranking of how good teams are. By late August it is mostly a record of what already happened. And what already happened does not change, however well you play from here.

What you get from it: two questions to ask about any team said to be surging.

Three teams, two completely different stories

Tampa Bay leads at 79–54. New York is 75–57. Boston is 73–60. That is the table, and it looks orderly.

Look at the scoreboards instead. The Rays have outscored people by 47 runs; the Yankees by 104; the Red Sox by 101.

The club in first has the worst run differential of the three, and it is behind both of them by more than fifty runs. That is not a rounding error.

Tampa Bay has been winning the games that could go either way

Watch the division rearrange itself when we convert those runs into the record each club has earned. The Rays should be about 71–62. They are 79–54, which is seven and a half wins better than their scoring deserves.

Boston should be about 78–55. They are 73–60, nearly five wins short. Sort the division by earned record and the order is Boston, New York, Tampa Bay — exactly upside down.

That is not an accusation. Close games have to be won by somebody, and Tampa Bay has won an unusual share of them. But close games are the least repeatable thing in baseball, which is why the runs are the better guide to what happens next.

Being better is a rate. Being behind is a fact. Rates need time, and time is the thing running out.

The Professor

So why does none of it save them?

The arithmetic is brutally simple, so let us do it in public.

By runs, Boston plays at about a .583 clip and Tampa Bay at about .537. Line them up and Boston gains 0.046 of a game on every game played — roughly one game every three weeks.

They are six games behind. At that rate, catching up takes 131 games. Hold onto that number.

The season has 29 left.

So we ran the whole thing out sixty thousand times. Boston wins the division in 4.2% of them; Tampa Bay takes it 72.1% of the time and New York 23.8%. The best team in the division is a one-in-twenty shot to finish first, and nothing about that is unfair. Superiority has to be paid out in instalments, and the bank is closing.

The consolation, which is larger than it sounds

All three of them are going to October anyway. In our simulated seasons Boston makes the playoffs 99.3% of the time, and the Yankees and Rays essentially always.

So this race was never about survival; it is about seeding. Who skips the first round, and who has to play a short series instead. And in a short series, as we have said before, the best measure in baseball picks the winner about 58% of the time.

Which is the real reason to want the division. Not the banner. The bye.

What to take home: two questions about any surge

When somebody tells you a team is surging, ask how much better and how many games are left. You need both, and people almost always supply only the first.

Notice how the two work together. How much better tells you the rate; how many games tells you whether the rate has room to work. Boston is a clear answer to the first question and a hopeless one to the second.

The habit is worth carrying off the sports page. A fund beating its benchmark by a point a year cannot repair a thirty-point hole before you retire. A candidate closing by half a point a week cannot close six points in a fortnight. In each case the rate is real and the deficit is real. The only question that matters is whether the calendar lets the one consume the other.

Being right and being out of time is the most ordinary tragedy there is.

Notes & sources

Standings and results from the MLB Stats API through the morning of August 27, 2026. AL East: Tampa Bay 79–54 (591 runs scored, 544 allowed), New York 75–57 (583/479), Boston 73–60 (595/494), Baltimore 65–68, Toronto 65–69.

Earned records use the Pythagorean expectation with a 1.83 exponent: Tampa Bay 71–62 against an actual 79–54, New York 77–55 against 75–57, Boston 78–55 against 73–60. The rate comparison uses those Pythagorean winning percentages, .537 and .583, whose difference of .046 is the expected games gained per game played. Six divided by .046 gives 131.

Division and playoff odds come from 60,000 simulated seasons over the 426 remaining league games, with team strength a 50/50 blend of actual and Pythagorean winning percentage regressed toward .500 by 69 games, game probabilities by log5 with a .535 home-field adjustment, and ties broken at random rather than by the real tiebreaker rules. Every club’s games played plus games remaining was verified to equal 162 first.

Two days earlier this piece said Boston had the division’s best run differential. New York has since passed them, +104 to +101, which is why the argument here rests on the gap between the leaders and Tampa Bay rather than on which chaser is fractionally ahead. Numbers this close move daily; the seven-and-a-half-game gap between Tampa Bay’s record and its runs does not.

One limit. The 131-game figure assumes both clubs keep playing at their season-long rate against average opposition, which no schedule obliges them to do. It is a way of sizing an edge, not a forecast. The simulation, which does use the actual remaining schedule, reaches the same verdict by a different road.
