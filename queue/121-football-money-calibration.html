Ohio State Football Cleared $33 Million. The Athletic Department Lost $38 Million. Both Are True.

The football team made a fortune. The department it belongs to ran a deficit. These are not contradictory numbers — they are the same number, viewed at two different altitudes, and the gap between them is where nearly every argument about “college sports losing money” goes wrong. Once you calibrate it properly, the real spending that has lost the plot turns out not to be in the stadium at all.

Start with the two numbers in the headline, because holding both in your head at once is the entire skill. In its 2024 fiscal year, Ohio State’s football program brought in about $111.6 million and spent about $78.6 million — a profit north of thirty-three million dollars. In the same year, the Ohio State athletic department took in about $254.9 million and spent about $292.3 million, for a loss of $37.7 million. The team that prints money and the department that bleeds it are the same organization. The only thing that changed between the two sentences is how far back you stood.

The “loss” is a subsidy wearing a disguise

So where did football’s profit go? Into the roughly three dozen other sports that lose money by design. Of all of Ohio State’s teams, only two turned a profit last year; football was one of them, and its surplus — along with the basketball money and the media checks — is precisely what pays for the swimmers, the rowers, the fencers, the twenty-six million dollars in athletic scholarships, and every other program that will never sell a ticket worth its cost. The athletic department is not a business that lost money. It is a redistribution enginei that took the football team’s winnings and spent them, on purpose, on everything that doesn’t pay.

Which means the two most common claims about the subject are both, strictly, false. “College football loses money” is false: at a place like Ohio State it is one of the most profitable enterprises on campus. “The athletic department is a self-sustaining business” is also false: as a whole it runs a planned deficit, covered from reserves and future earnings. The truth only appears when you say which box you drew around the money — the program, or the department. Draw it in the wrong place and you will “discover” whichever answer you started out wanting.

Now calibrate it against the campus

Here is where the exercise earns its keep, because the football money that dominates every conversation looks enormous only until you set it beside the institution it belongs to. Ohio State’s athletic department, all of it, is about six and a half percent of a university that runs on roughly $4.5 billion a year. And football’s celebrated $112 million in revenue — the crown jewel, the thing fans and trustees fight over — is a rounding error next to the parts of the university nobody argues about. The research enterprise spends about $1.68 billion a year. The Wexner Medical Center takes in more than four billion. On the scale of the actual institution, the football program is not the big money. It is barely visible.

So where did the money actually lose the plot?

Which brings us to your instinct that somewhere on campus, spending has lost sight of itself — because that instinct is right, and the calibration tells you exactly where to point it. It is not the athletic department, which is comparatively lean and, football-first, largely pays its own way from the revenue it generates. The runaway is somewhere far quieter and far larger: administration. At Ohio State the ratio of full-time non-instructional staff to full-time faculty is more than six to one. The university employs, by one tally, some 1,700 administrators and around 21,000 non-teaching staff against roughly 48,000 students — close to one non-teaching employee for every two undergraduates. Measured per thousand students, Ohio State carries about 482 non-instructional staff; the University of Texas at Austin carries 281, Wisconsin 323. Nationally, over four decades the ranks of administrators and professional staff grew by 164 and 452 percent while the faculty grew by 92.

That is the number that should keep a trustee up at night, and almost nobody watches it, because it is invisible and unglamorous. The football coach’s salary is public, debated, and printed in the paper; it is also, in the end, paid for by a program that turns a profit. The steady multiplication of associate vice provosts is none of those things. The most scrutinized dollar on campus is the one that pays its way, and the least scrutinized is the one quietly compounding in the org chart.

The most-watched money on a campus is the football team’s, and it pays for itself. The least-watched is the administration’s, and it doesn’t stop growing.

The Columnist

The lesson is older than the budget

None of this is really about football, or even about Ohio State. It is about the oldest move in reading any number: before you decide whether something is expensive, you have to say expensive compared to what, and you have to name the box you are drawing around the money. Football “loses money” or “prints money” depending only on where you put the walls. The athletic budget is a scandal or a rounding error depending only on what you set it beside. And the department everyone loves to scold turns out to be the thrifty one, while the cost that is genuinely out of control wears a suit and never takes the field. Calibrate first. Then, and only then, decide who to be angry at.

Notes & sources

Athletics figures are from Ohio State’s 2024 fiscal-year report to the NCAA (July 2023–June 2024), as reported by Sportico, Eleven Warriors, The Lantern and others: department revenue about $254.9 million, expenses about $292.3 million, deficit about $37.7 million; the football program about $111.6 million in revenue against about $78.6 million in expenses, one of only two Ohio State sports to turn a profit that year; athletic student aid about $26 million. FY2024 was a lighter revenue year — six home football games rather than eight — so the deficit is larger than in a typical season, but the structural point (football’s surplus subsidizing the non-revenue sports) holds every year.

University scale: Ohio State’s operating budget is roughly $4.5 billion (FY2025); research expenditures were about $1.68 billion (FY2025, up from $1.58 billion the prior year); the Wexner Medical Center reports operating revenue above $4 billion and rising. Administrative figures — a non-instructional-to-faculty ratio above six to one, about 482 non-instructional staff per 1,000 students versus 281 at Texas and 323 at Wisconsin, and national growth of 164% (administrators) and 452% (professional staff) against 92% (faculty) from 1976 to 2018 — are from an American Enterprise Institute analysis of federal staffing data. Functional expense splits (instruction versus institutional support versus plant) are reported in the university’s financial statements and IPEDS filings; this issue characterizes administration by staffing rather than asserting a dollar line it cannot independently verify.

The reasoning tool at the center of this piece — that the answer changes with the box you draw around the data — is developed in the primer on The Unit of Analysis (Concept No. 22). The archive is at thesportspage.net.
