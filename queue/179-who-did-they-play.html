How hard is Notre Dame’s schedule — and can anybody know yet? On Friday, Kirk Herbstreit called it “an embarrassment,” with two real tests: BYU and Miami. By the AP poll of 11 October it has three: No. 8 BYU, No. 3 Miami and No. 24 SMU. That is the same count as No. 1 Georgia and Indiana, last year’s champion. Ohio State has five; Texas has six.

Most people assume a schedule’s strength is a fact, fixed the day it is printed. The polls say otherwise.

So he is half right. Notre Dame’s first six opponents were unranked in August and are unranked now. But here is the part you will not hear on television: nobody knows how hard any schedule is in October. You find out in January.

By the end of this you will be able to answer any schedule argument with one question — as of when? — and you will see why that question settles more than the shouting does.

A Schedule Is a Belief

Every August, 25 teams are ranked. Over the last twelve seasons, about 11 of them were gone from the final poll. Never fewer than 8; in 2022, 15.

That means a schedule judged in August is judged on rankings that are close to half wrong. Watch what that does to the champions. We took every College Football Playoff winner since 2014 and counted its ranked regular-season opponents twice: once by the August poll, once by the final one.

The totals barely move: 44 ranked opponents by August’s poll, 42 by the final one. But only 28 are the same teams. Sixteen August “tests” turned out not to be, and fourteen teams nobody ranked became one. Georgia in 2022 played five ranked teams by the end; four of them were unranked when the season began.

The number of hard games stays about the same. Which games they are, nobody knows until it is over.

Why College Football Argues and the NFL Does Not

The NFL works more like an experiment. Thirty-two teams, a schedule built to balance them, and a playoff where everyone who matters plays each other. You settle it on the field.

College football works more like an observational study. There are more than 130 teams, twelve games each, and most conferences barely touch. You almost never see the two teams you want to compare on the same field; you compare them by proxy, through the teams they both played. That gives you breadth and very little precision — so what fills the gap is belief.

That is not a flaw to be ashamed of. It is just the situation Bayesian thinkingi was built for. The August poll is the prior. Every Saturday is evidence. The schedule’s strength is a belief that updates; you should expect it to move, and you should hold it loosely until it stops.

What to take home

When someone calls a schedule soft or brutal, ask as of when? In August it is a guess that will be close to half wrong. In October it is a better guess. In January it is a fact.

Here is the honest verdict on Notre Dame today. The front half was light. The back half has three ranked teams, and any of them can still fall out. We will count again when it is over, and so should Herbstreit.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

AP Top 25 polls and game schedules from CollegeFootballData. “August” is the preseason poll; “the end” is the final poll after the playoff; 2026 figures use the poll of 11 October, before Notre Dame played BYU. A ranked opponent is any team in the Top 25 at that moment. Regular season only, conference title games included. Champions are taken from each season’s title game.

Top 25 is a hard edge. No. 26 does not count and No. 25 does; SMU, at No. 24, is one bad week from vanishing. A champion that beats everybody also drags its opponents’ final rankings down, so the “after” counts run a little low for the very best teams. And a poll is a vote, not a model: it leans on record and brand. It is the public’s belief, which is the thing being argued about.
