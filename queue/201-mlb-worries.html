We Called the Rays Lucky in July. They Won 98.

We graded 15 things this paper worried about in baseball’s first half. Seven held up, four washed out, and four were half right. Worries about a team’s record held. Worries about a player’s first week mostly did not.

Which of this spring’s baseball worries were real, and which were just noise? The season is over, so you and we can finally check. We went back through every storyline we ran from Opening Day to the All-Star break and graded each one against the final numbers.

Here is the answer, with its number. Of 15 worries, 7 held up and 4 were nothing. The rest were half right.

You would expect a paper that preaches small samples to dodge the small-sample traps. We did not. We even wrote the rule, in Issue #32, and then broke it twice. By the end, you will know which worries to keep next April and which to let go.

The Ones We Got Wrong

Start with the worst, so you can see we mean it. In mid-July the Rays were 56–37. In Issue #110 we said their first place was “bought on credit.” By the runs they had scored and allowed, they should have won about five fewer games, so we expected them to fade. They went 42–27 the rest of the way and won 98. Their runs said about 90. Luck is not a loan; nobody sends a bill. That is one of the four worries that turned out to be nothing.

On day three, in Issue #2, we gave Pete Alonso a 60% chance of never hitting .250 this year. He hit .267 with 43 home runs. And in Issue #38 we said the Mets would win about six of their next 17. “They will not win ten.” They won ten of the 15 they played.

One more, and this one is a plain error. Issue #45 said Devin Williams had a 9.95 ERA. That was his ERA on April 21. By the day we printed it, after six straight scoreless outings, it was 5.68. That piece now carries a correction.

The Ones That Held

Look at the green column. You will notice what it has in common. The Mets worries were all about a record: 7–14 after 21 games, 10–21 after 31, and 20–26 needing .578 baseball to catch up. They played .466 instead and finished 74–88. You could see it after a month.

The other two that held were injuries. Juan Soto’s calf cost him 15 games; we had said 10 or more was more likely than not. Cal Raleigh’s 0-for-38 came with a sore oblique, and he hit .182 for the year. An injury is not a small sample. It is news.

What We Never Wrote

Eleven of these 15 storylines were about the Mets. Meanwhile the White Sox went from 60 wins to 84, the biggest jump in baseball, and the Braves went from 76 to 94. Neither got a single issue. If you worry about one team, you will miss the rest of the league. So did we.

Trust a record after a month. Ignore a player’s first week. Treat an injury as news.

— The Sports Page

What to Take Home

Next April, sort every worry by what it rests on. A team’s record after 30 games is worth worrying about. A hitter’s first two games are not, even when the number is loud. An injury belongs in its own pile, because it changes the player, not just the sample.

And be careful when you hear “lucky.” Winning more than your runs say is not a debt that must be repaid. Sometimes the luck just keeps going, and sometimes the team was better than the runs showed.

How this was graded, and what it cannot do

The list: every MLB issue from Opening Day (March 27) to late July that made a claim or a worry the season could settle; 15 in all. Pieces with nothing to check, like history or method pieces, are left out.

The outcomes: final 2026 standings, season stats, game logs and injured-list moves from the MLB Stats API. The Rays’ 98–64 was also checked against ESPN and Baseball-Reference, Alonso’s line and Williams’s game log against ESPN. The full ledger, with each claim and outcome, is in data/mlb/worries-2026.csv; code: scripts/mlb_worries.py.

What it cannot do: the grades are ours, and “half right” is a judgment call. Fifteen worries is too few to say how often we are right in general. And the White Sox and Braves are measured against their 2025 records, not against what anyone expected in March.
