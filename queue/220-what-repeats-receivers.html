7 Scores, Then 17, Then 8. Same Player.

Ja’Marr Chase caught 7 touchdown passes in 2023, 17 in 2024 and 8 in 2025. Across every NFL receiver since 2018, touchdowns and drops barely carry over from one season to the next. Getting open does.
By The Sports Page · The Professor · Lesson: how much of a number is the player?

how much a receiver’s drop rate repeats next season (.01–.23)

The question: does a receiver’s touchdown total, or his drops, tell you what kind of receiver he is?

The answer: not much. Measure the same receiver in two seasons in a row, and his touchdown rate repeats at .23 and his drop rate at .13. On this scale, 1 means a stat repeats perfectly and 0 means not at all. How far he gets from his defender repeats at .60, and how deep he is thrown to at .67.

Why that is surprising: touchdowns and drops are what everybody remembers. “He has bad hands.” “He’s a red-zone threat.” You would expect those to be the most personal numbers a receiver has. They are among the least.

What you get: one question to ask of any stat: how much does it repeat? It tells you how much of the number is the player, and how far to pull a big year back toward average.

What repeats for a receiver

Think of a bathroom scale. Step on, step off, step on again. A good scale gives you about the same number twice. A cheap one jumps, and you learn to trust it less. A stat that repeats is a good scale for the player.

Here is every receiver stat we tested, from the most repeatable to the least, for wide receivers with at least 50 targets in back-to-back seasons.

Notice what sits at the top: how deep he runs, how often he is thrown to, how open he gets. That is the receiver’s job and his speed, and it comes back every year. Notice what sits at the bottom: touchdowns and drops.

Why touchdowns and drops shuffle

There are not many of them. The typical receiver in our data catches about five touchdown passes and drops a handful of balls in a season. Two goal-line calls that go to the tight end, or one wet afternoon, and the season looks different.

Other people decide them. A touchdown needs a play-caller who calls his number inside the 10 and a quarterback who sees him. A drop needs a ball that was catchable in the first place.

Chase shows it in one career. In 2023, 2024 and 2025 he was thrown to 145, 175 and 185 times. His touchdowns went 7, 17, 8. How open he got, measured in yards from the nearest defender at the catch, barely moved: 3.2, 3.0 and 3.1.

“Bad hands” is mostly a story about a few plays.

— The Sports Page

How far to pull it back

Multiply the gap between a player and the average by the repeat rate, and that is your best guess for next year.

The typical receiver scores about 5 touchdowns per 100 targets. Chase’s 2024 rate was nearly 10. The gap is about 5, and 5 times .23 is about 1. So the best guess for his 2025 was about 6 per 100, or roughly 11 touchdowns on 185 targets. He scored 8. The 17 was a great receiver having a lucky year.

The skill nobody votes for

Here is the strange part. Getting open is one of the steadiest things a receiver does, and it does not show up in his yards. Hold fixed how deep he runs, how sure his hands are and what he does after the catch. Then more separation adds nothing to his yards per snap. The most open receivers tend to run the shortest routes, where a defense will give up the catch and make the tackle.

The voters do not see it either. All-Pro voters reward volume: sort an All-Pro and a receiver who was not by receiving yards, and the All-Pro comes out ahead 98 times in 100. Sort them by how open they got, and it is 50 in 100, a coin flip.

What to take home

Ask how much a stat repeats before you judge a player on it. A number that repeats at .6 is mostly the player. A number that repeats at .2 is mostly the season.

Pull touchdown totals and drops most of the way back to average. A receiver who scores 17 will probably score fewer next year. A receiver with a bad year of drops will probably have fewer.

What a player does every play repeats; what happens at the end of the play shuffles. Routes, depth and targets come back. Touchdowns depend on everybody else too.

How we did it

Data. NFL regular seasons 2018–2025. Wide receivers with at least 50 targets in a season: 674 seasons. They make 401 pairs of back-to-back seasons by the same player. Targets, catches and touchdowns come from nflverse play-by-play, and snaps from nflverse snap counts. Separation, cushion and depth of target come from Next Gen Stats, and drops from Pro Football Reference charting. Those last two are one feed each.

Second feed. ESPN’s season stats for the same players give a touchdown repeat rate of .22 (.13–.30). That set also includes tight ends. Ja’Marr Chase’s catches, targets, yards and touchdowns for 2023–2025 match on both feeds. His separation is from Next Gen Stats alone.

The repeat rate is the correlation between a stat in one season and the same stat the next. Intervals are 95%, by Fisher’s z, and cover chance only. “Adds nothing to his yards”: in a model of yards per snap on four receiver skills found by factor analysis, the getting-open skill’s weight was −.11. Separation and depth of target correlate at −.57. All-Pro lists, 2018–2025, are from Wikipedia (one source); “98 in 100” and “50 in 100” are areas under the curve.

Limits. Only receivers who kept a role for two seasons are in the pairs. No route counts are public, so targets per snap stands in for targets per route. A change of quarterback between seasons counts as the same receiver. Code and full results: research/constructs/football/qb_wr/ in our repository.
