The Best Hitter in Baseball Finished Fifth in Runs Batted In.

In 2025 Salvador Perez and Carlos Correa hit almost alike. Perez drove in 100 runs; Correa drove in 52. Most people assume RBI measure the hitter. They also measure the men batting ahead of him and his manager — and only one of those is him.

What does a run batted in actually tell you? It is the oldest argument-winner in baseball. “He drove in 120 runs” ends a lot of bar fights.

Here is the answer, with its number. We took the 145 men who played every day in 2025. How well each one hit explains only 43% of the spread in their RBI. Add one more fact — how often he came to bat with runners on base — plus his power, and you can explain nearly 90%.

That should bother you a little. Most fans read RBI as a hitter’s skill, maybe even his nerve. A big part of it is a seat at the table: who is on base when he walks up. By the end of this you will know how to read an RBI total. You will also have a new idea, one this whole series is built on: the difference between the number and the thing.

This is the first of a series we are calling Box Score Anatomy. Each one takes a single stat and asks four questions. What is it? How do you get one? What is it a sign of? And what else is a sign of the same thing?

What It Is, and How You Get One

You get an RBI when a run scores because of what you did at the plate. A hit counts. So does a sacrifice fly, a groundout that scores a man from third, or a walk with the bases loaded. A home run counts for everyone who scores, you included. You get nothing if the run scores on an error, or if you ground into a double play.

Now notice the catch. Only one of those ways needs nobody else: the home run, with the bases empty. Among our 145 hitters, just 29% of all their RBI were the batter driving himself in. The other 71% needed a teammate already on base.

Look at the right edge. Pete Alonso came up with runners on base 365 times, more than anyone in baseball, and drove in 126. Aaron Judge had the best season at the plate of anyone, by a lot. He came up with runners on 300 times. And pitchers walked him on purpose 36 times, more than anyone; 31 of those came with men on base. A walk with first base open drives in nobody. Judge finished fifth.

An RBI is a team stat with one man’s name on it.

What It Is a Sign Of

Now the pair from the top. Perez and Correa had nearly the same overall line. But they got there differently. Correa reached base more often; Perez hit for more power, 30 home runs to 13. Perez also came up with runners on base 295 times to Correa’s 264.

So RBI rewarded Perez twice: once for a bigger seat at the table, and once for power, which turns a runner into a run. It gave Correa nothing for the times he got on base himself. Those runs show up in somebody else’s RBI.

Here is a way to picture it. Statisticians draw the things they cannot see as ovals and the number they can count as a box. Five ovals feed this box.

The things in the ovals are what we actually argue about. Statisticians call them constructs. The box is an indicator: something we can count because we think it moves with a thing we care about. RBI move with power and contact. They also move with your teammates and your lineup spot, which is the problem.

What Else Is a Sign of the Same Thing

If you want the hitter alone, look at numbers that leave the teammates out. On-base percentage asks how often he reached. Slugging asks how much damage he did when he hit the ball. Put them together and you get OPS, the measure that made Perez and Correa twins. None of them cares who was on base.

And the thing many fans really mean by RBI — coming through when it counts — is its own construct, with its own indicators. That one is coming soon in this series, and it is a fight.

What to Take Home

When someone wins an argument with RBI, ask two questions. Who was on base, and where did he bat? A cleanup hitter behind two men who reach base will pile them up. The same bat leading off, behind the bottom of the order, will not.

Then ask the bigger question, the one this series will keep asking. What is this number a sign of, and what else makes it go up? Every stat in the box score is a window onto something you cannot see directly. Some windows are clean. This one has your teammates’ fingerprints all over it.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Data: the 145 hitters who qualified for the 2025 batting title, from the MLB Stats API: season totals plus the “runners on” and “scoring position” splits. Totals for traded players are combined. Perez and Correa were checked against ESPN; the RBI leaders against ESPN and MLB.com.

The 43% and the nearly 90%: simple regressions across the 145 hitters. OPS alone explains 43% of the variation in RBI. Home runs plus plate appearances with runners on explains 88%; slugging plus those plate appearances, 84%.

What it cannot tell you: plate appearances with runners on is a rough count of chances; it does not say how many runners, or where they stood. One season of qualified hitters leaves out part-timers. And this says nothing about whether “clutch” is real — that question gets its own issue.
