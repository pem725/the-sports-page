One Tool Would Have Cost Us 83 of Our 207 Answers.

We took every issue this paper has published and asked: what if all we had was the t-test and the analysis of variance, the tools most people learn first? Even letting that tool forecast with a plain average, 83 of 207 conclusions would have changed or could not have been reached. Without that allowance, 121.
By The Sports Page · The Professor · Lesson: pick the tool for the question, not the other way around

The question: if this paper had used only one statistical tool, the t-test and its big brother ANOVA, how many of its conclusions would have changed?

The answer: about 2 in 5, even being generous. We went through all 207 regular issues. Strictly, 121 answers would have flipped or could not have been reached. Our editor objected that an average is a kind of forecast, so we let the tool forecast with one and counted again: 83 still changed or went missing. Many more landed on the right answer by a wrong road.

Why that is surprising: the tool is not bad. When the question was the one it was built for, comparing two groups, 20 of its 23 answers held up. The trouble is that most questions are not that question.

What you get: a habit you can use on any study, poll or broadcast graphic. Before you ask whether the answer is right, ask whether the tool fit the question.

Meet the one-tool analyst

Picture an analyst who learned one thing well. Ronald Fisher gave the world the analysis of variance in his 1925 book for research workers. Our analyst read that part. Our analyst never opened Fisher’s 1935 book, The Design of Experiments, which is the part where Fisher insists that how you collect the data decides what it can tell you.

So here is how our analyst works. Every question becomes a comparison of averages between groups. A number like payroll gets chopped into “high” and “low.” Every row counts as independent. A p-value under .05 means “real,” and over .05 means “nothing there.” Nobody asks how the groups formed, or whether a stat means the same thing everywhere.

You have met this analyst. Most of us were this analyst once, including an old version of the person writing this. It is the most common way statistics gets done.

Where the one tool breaks

We sorted every issue by the kind of question it asked. Then we counted how often the one-tool answer changed or went missing.

Look at the top bar first. Ask the tool its own question, “do these two groups differ?”, and it does fine. Now look down the chart. Ask it whether one thing causes another, and 13 of 19 answers change. Ask it what a stat really measures, and 22 of 27 do.

Notice what does not help those two rows: being generous. Letting the tool forecast with an average rescued most of the forecasts. It rescued almost nothing about causes or measurement. An average can stand in for a prediction. It cannot tell you which way an arrow points, or whether a number means the same thing in two places.

The hammer is a fine tool. The trouble starts when everything looks like a nail.

— The Sports Page

Two flips, run for real

Counting is one thing. So we took two old issues and ran the one tool on the same data, nearly 14,000 NFL team-games from 1999 on.

Time of possession. Winners held the ball almost four minutes longer than losers. The t-test calls that overwhelming. Split teams into thirds by time of possession and the top third won 73 games in 100; the bottom third won 27. The one-tool verdict: control the clock and you win. But Issue #179 showed the arrow points the other way. Teams that are ahead run the ball to burn the clock. The lead makes the possession. The tool cannot see that, because it never asks how the groups formed.

Turnovers. Now ask whether a team that wins the turnover battle early keeps winning it. The one-tool analyst runs a paired t-test, first half of the season against the second. The average change is zero, so the verdict is “turnover margins are stable.” Here is what really happened. The best third of teams went from +0.8 a game to +0.1. The worst third went from −0.8 to −0.2. Nearly everyone slid back toward zero. The averages matched only because the good teams fell as far as the bad ones rose. Issue #181 had it right: turnovers win games, not seasons.

Same tool, two answers

One more thing surprised us. The one tool does not even give one answer. Our analyst often had more than one t-test to choose from, and the choices disagreed.

Take the coaches who went home to coach their old school, Issue #210. Compare their win rates to other coaches and the t-test finds no edge. Compare the same coaches before and after the move and it finds a drop, which is really regression to the mean. Same tool, same data, opposite stories. Knowing one tool well does not tell you which way to point it.

The editor pushed back

Before this ran, our editor checked 20 of the codes himself, five of each kind. He agreed with 18. His two doubts were the same doubt: an ANOVA hands you group averages, and an average is a forecast. “Two of every three questionable players play” is just the average of yes-or-no outcomes.

He was right, so we re-coded every forecast question that way, with two new coders working apart. That is where the 83 comes from. It also turned up something worth knowing. In 11 of those questions, the average lands on the right number and then states it as a sure thing. Our issue said 70 in 100; the average says “it will happen.” Seventeen questions still could not be asked at all: a whole simulated season, a ranking, how many starts until a pitcher recovers.

The editor has been making this argument for a long time. In 2010 he and three colleagues, Michael Johns, Patrick McGovern and Julius Najab, wrote that social science had drifted into “a rigid routine involving a limited set of tools,” with ANOVA as the usual example. Their remedy was to start from the problem and pick the tool after. This piece is that argument, run on our own work.

And a shout-out to one of those co-authors. Patrick McGovern goes by PJ Magoo to his friends, and he is an Oregon Duck fan through and through. PJ, this one is for you.

What to take home

Name the question before you pick the tool. Is it a comparison, a cause, a forecast, or a question about what a number measures? Each one needs a different tool. The t-test answers only the first one well.

“Not significant” is not “nothing there.” It was the second most common failure we found, 33 times. A test that cannot see an effect has not shown that the effect is zero.

Measurement comes first. If a stat does not mean the same thing in two places, no test will rescue the comparison. How you measure decides what you can learn, before any model gets a vote.

How we did it

The issues. All 207 regular issues published through Nov 25, 2026 (Sunday Editions, which grade other issues, were left out). For each, a script pulled the headline, deck, question, answer and methods text; the full issue was read whenever that was not enough.

The coding. A written codebook defined the one-tool analyst (t-tests and ANOVA only; median splits; every row independent; p < .05 means real; no design, no measurement) and four verdicts: same, same for the wrong reason, changed, and could not ask. Coders were told to be fair to the analyst: if a sensible t-test gets the right answer, that is “same.” Four coders took about 52 issues each. The coders were AI models working from the codebook, which is why we checked them three ways. A fifth coder independently re-coded a random 52; the two agreed on 81 of 100 verdicts (Cohen’s kappa .74) and on 90 of 100 “did the decision survive” calls (kappa .80). We reran two issues by hand on the original data, and both matched their codes. The editor audited 20 codes drawn at random, five per verdict, and agreed with 18; both of his doubts concerned forecasts, which led to the sensitivity count below.

The second count. All 63 issues first coded “could not ask” were re-coded by two new coders, working apart, with the analyst allowed to forecast with a group average (a point, no interval). They agreed on 78 of 100 verdicts (kappa .70). An issue counts as changed or lost only if both said so.

Uncertainty. Intervals are 95% Wilson intervals on the counts. Changed or lost, with forecasting by averages: 83 of 207, 40 per 100 (34–47); if either second coder's call is used, 93. Without forecasting: 121 of 207, 58 per 100 (52–65). Right for the right reason: 45 to 62 of 207, depending on the count. The intervals cover the luck of which issues we wrote, not disagreement about a code; the reliability numbers speak to that.

Limits. These are judgments about what a hypothetical analyst would conclude, not 207 reruns. The one-tool analyst is a caricature on purpose; a careful user of ANOVA would do better. The reruns use nflverse team-game data, 13,898 team-games, ties left out. Codebook, codes and code: research/one-tool/ in our repository. The 2010 paper: McKnight, Johns, McGovern and Najab, “The pitfalls of a tool-based science and the promise of a problem-focused science,” Journal of Methods and Measurement in the Social Sciences 1(2), 1–14.
