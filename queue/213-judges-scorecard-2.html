Alabama Won 47–0 and Fell Three Spots. Neither Computer Moved It.

The committee dropped Alabama from third to sixth in a week it beat Chattanooga 47–0. Our math kept it second. A second computer, Kenneth Massey’s, kept it where it was too. The judges usually agree with a résumé more than with raw power. This week, Alabama is the exception.

The question: why would the judges drop a team three spots for winning 47–0?

The answer: they mostly didn’t drop Alabama; they lifted the teams around it. Texas beat Arkansas 45–3, Tennessee beat LSU 27–13, and Notre Dame beat SMU 24–3. Tennessee also beat Alabama 26–10 in October, and the judges seem to be honoring that. But neither computer we checked sees Alabama as sixth. Ours has it second.

Why that is surprising: you’d guess a committee leans on the eye test and the computers lean on cold numbers. This committee agrees with our numbers more often than not. Take any two ranked teams: the committee and our math put them in the same order 86 times in 100.

What you get: the difference between “who is best” and “who earned it”, and why the judges are answering the second one.

Two computers, two different questions

Every ranking answers a question, and they don’t all answer the same one. A power rating asks: if these two teams played tomorrow, who wins, and by how much? A résumé asks: given who you played, how much did you accomplish? A 9–2 team can be the better team and still have the thinner résumé.

Our math is a blend of both. Half is how much a team outscores people, adjusted for schedule. The other half is how many more games it won than a top-12 team would have won against the same opponents; that half is pure résumé. Massey’s power rating is the first kind only: a prediction. (You can see his full ratings at masseyratings.com; we don’t reprint them.)

Now look at the bottom bars. The committee orders teams like our blend 86 times in 100, and like Massey’s pure power rating 74 times in 100. That fits a panel that is judging résumés. Be careful, though. With only 25 teams, the intervals overlap, and the gap could be close to zero. Call it a lean, not a law.

Then look at Alabama in the top chart: the longest red line. The committee has it sixth. Our blend, which already counts résumé, has it second. Massey’s power rating has it in the top four. Alabama’s two losses came to Georgia, by three, and to Tennessee. Losing by three to the best team in the country is about the best loss a résumé can carry.

Where the judges and the math split most

Notre Dame (10–1) went the other way: up from sixth to fourth after a 24–3 win over SMU. Our blend has the Irish seventh. Their one loss, 28–24 at BYU, is respectable, but the schedule is thinner than the SEC teams around them. Massey’s power rating, which ignores résumé, likes them more than we do.

UCLA (10–1) fell from fourth to ninth after losing 18–16 at Michigan. One two-point loss cost five spots. Our math moved UCLA two, from eighth to tenth. The committee had been the generous one, and it corrected hard.

Ole Miss and LSU are the biggest gaps on the board. The committee has Ole Miss 22nd and leaves LSU out; our math has them 12th and 14th. Every Ole Miss loss, and four of LSU’s five, came against teams in the committee’s top 25. LSU lost two of them by a single point, to Kentucky and Texas. A résumé panel sees 7–4 and 6–5. A margin-based number sees teams that play good opponents close.

Who has been picking winners?

Here’s the scorecard part. After each ranking, we take every game between two teams in either top 25 and ask which list picked the winner. The higher-ranked team is the pick. Since the first ranking, the committee’s order has picked 15 of 19. Ours has picked 12.

That sounds like a beating. It isn’t much of one. The two lists disagreed on only three games: Texas at Missouri, Alabama at LSU, and Iowa at Northwestern. The committee’s team won all three. Three coin flips land all heads 1 time in 8. So the judges are ahead, and we can’t yet tell skill from luck. We’ll keep counting.

What this weekend can change

Two weekends of games remain before Selection Day, Dec. 6: rivalry week and the conference title games. Our ratings make most of the top teams clear favorites this week. Alabama wins the Iron Bowl about 88 times in 100. Texas wins at Texas A&M, Friday night, about 79 in 100. Notre Dame wins at Syracuse about 94 in 100.

The one to watch is USC at UCLA. Our ratings make UCLA about a 7-in-10 favorite. The point spread has USC favored by almost a touchdown, which works out to about 65 in 100. A spread is a crowd’s guess, and a crowd hears news a rating can’t, like who is hurt. When the two split this far, one of them is about to look smart. We’ll grade it Sunday, either way.

What to take home

Before you argue with a ranking, ask what question it answers. “Who would win tomorrow” and “who earned it” are different questions. They give different lists, and both can be right.

A judge who agrees with you 86 times in 100 is not a judge with a thumb on the scale. The other 14 are where the argument belongs. This week, the biggest one is Alabama.

Three games is not a verdict. The committee is 3 for 3 when we disagree. That’s worth watching, not worth crowning.

How we did it

Data. The Nov. 24 CFP ranking, from CollegeFootballData, checked against ESPN’s; every rank matched. Scores from CollegeFootballData through Nov. 21.

Our math. Each team’s schedule-adjusted scoring margin, averaged with its wins above what a top-12 team would expect against the same schedule; small schools get the six-point bowl haircut and the SEC its 3.5, both from Scorecard No. 1.

Massey. Kenneth Massey’s power ratings, through games of Nov. 21, read from masseyratings.com with his permission. We report only what we inferred from them, never the ratings themselves.

Agreement. For the 300 pairs from the committee’s 25 teams, the share each computer orders the same way (ties dropped). Intervals: 4,000 resamples of the 25 teams. Win chances come from our frozen pre-week ratings, with a home edge of 2.2 points and a spread of 16.5 points around the projection.

Limits. Nineteen games is a small sample. The committee’s ballots aren’t public, so we grade the panel, never a judge. Code: research/cfp-committee/judges/ in our repository.
