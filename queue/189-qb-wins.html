Allow 28 Points and Even Tom Brady Loses Six in Ten.

Across 13,934 NFL starts since 1999, quarterbacks whose defense held the other team under 17 points won 86% of the time. When the defense gave up 28 or more, they won 16%. Most people assume a quarterback’s record measures the quarterback. A big piece of it measures the men he never shares the field with.

Is a quarterback’s win-loss record really his? You hear it every Sunday. “He just wins.” “Look at his record as a starter.” No stat in football ends more arguments.

Here is the answer, with its number. We took every regular-season start from 1999 to 2025, 13,934 of them. When the defense held the other team under 17 points, the quarterback’s team won 86% of the time. When it gave up 28 or more, 16%. The quarterback was the same kind of player in both piles. The defense was not.

That should bother you a little, because the quarterback never plays a snap of defense. By the end of this you will know how to read a quarterback’s record, and you will see the same lesson the first issue of this series found in baseball’s runs batted in: a team stat with one man’s name on it.

What It Is, and How You Get One

There is no official rule for a quarterback win. The league credits wins to teams. Fans, broadcasts and record books then hand each one to whoever started at quarterback. So the “stat” is really a label: the team won, and he was the starter.

To get one, your team needs more points than the other team. You can help with the first part. The second part belongs to the defense, the kicker and the schedule.

Read it left to right. Give up fewer than 10 and almost anybody wins. Give up 35 and almost nobody does. The slope is steep, and the quarterback did not draw it.

A quarterback’s record is half a quarterback and half a defense, with a kicker on top.

Even the Greatest

Take the quarterback with the most wins in history. When Tom Brady’s defense held teams under 17, his teams won about 97 in 100. When it gave up 28 or more, they won 23 of 61, fewer than four in ten. Patrick Mahomes, Aaron Rodgers and Drew Brees show the same cliff.

Now watch the same man across two seasons. We took every quarterback with at least 12 starts in back-to-back years, 398 pairs. When his record changed from one year to the next, the change tracked his defense’s points allowed a little more closely than his own offense’s points scored. His record moved with players he never meets in the huddle.

What It Is a Sign Of

Now the picture we have been building in this series. The things you cannot see are ovals; the number you count is the box.

Quarterback wins are what statisticians call a composite: many causes flow into one count. That is why the number wobbles. Of the quarterbacks in the top third by record one season, only about half were there the next. Home runs, by comparison, stay put about two times in three.

What Else Is a Sign of the Same Thing

If you want the passer, look at what he controls. Yards per pass attempt. How often he throws an interception. How often he gets sacked, which is partly the line and partly him holding the ball. None of these is perfect, but each one leaves out the defense.

The record still matters for one thing: it tells you whether the team won. Just do not hand all of it to one man.

What to Take Home

When someone wins an argument with a quarterback’s record, ask one question: how good was his defense? A great quarterback on a bad defense will lose games he played well. An average one behind a great defense will win games he did not.

And keep the bigger lesson from this series in your pocket. Before you credit a person with a number, count how many other people put their hands on it.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Data: every NFL regular-season game from 1999 to 2025 in the nflverse game records, which list each team’s quarterback and the final score: 13,934 starts. Ties count as half a win. Tom Brady’s 23 wins in 61 games when his defense allowed 28 or more were checked against ESPN’s scores.

A data trap we hit: the quarterback listed for a game is not always the man who started it. In one 2007 Colts game, Peyton Manning started and left early, and the records list his backup. A handful of such games will not move rates built on thousands of starts, but it is why we did not quote any one quarterback’s perfect record.

What it cannot tell you: points allowed is not purely the defense — a quarterback who throws an interception for a touchdown, or keeps his defense off the field, changes it. The season-to-season comparison is a correlation, not a controlled test. Across team seasons, points scored explains 55% of the spread in winning percentage and points allowed 44%; the quarterback shapes the first far more than the second.
