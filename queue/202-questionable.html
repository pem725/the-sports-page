Will Toney Play? Two of Every Three Questionable Players Do.

Miami’s best player hurt his ankle Wednesday, three days before No. 1 Miami plays at Notre Dame. In the NFL, where every injury report is on file, a player listed questionable has played 9,634 times out of 14,346 since 2016. That is two in three. The betting line moved half a point.

How far the line moved, Sunday to Wednesday

Malachi Toney hurt his ankle at practice Wednesday. Will he play Saturday night, and how much should it change what you expect? Start here: in the NFL, a player listed questionable plays about two times in three. Most fans hear “questionable” as a coin flip, or worse. It leans the other way. And the people with money on the game barely moved.

By the end you will be able to read an injury report the way a bookmaker does. Start at two in three. Then ask the one question that can move you.

What We Know, as of Wednesday Night

Toney hurt his ankle in practice Wednesday, the Associated Press reported. Miami said he is “undergoing further evaluation by the team’s medical staff.” The school did not say whether he will play. ESPN’s Pete Thamel reported that his status is uncertain and that Toney is expected to push to play. He has had ankle trouble earlier this season and has not missed a game.

Here is why anyone cares. Toney has 73 catches for 1,027 yards and 13 touchdowns in eight games. We checked that two ways, against ESPN’s box scores and against CollegeFootballData, and they agree to the yard.

That is all anybody knows. Everything past it is a guess, ours included.

What “Questionable” Has Meant

College football keeps no public archive of its injury reports. The NFL does. So we matched every NFL final injury report since 2013 to that week’s snap counts, and counted who actually took the field.

Look at the red bar. Two in three. Now look at the bar for ankles: the same. The body part changes nothing; what changes it is practice. A questionable player who sat out his last practice of the week played 44% of the time, less than half. One who practiced, even on a limited basis, played 71%.

So the practice report tells you more than the injury label does. Hold onto that for Thursday and Friday.

One more thing to notice. Before 2016 the NFL had a “probable” tag, and 95% of those players played. The league dropped it, so many of the players it would once have called probable now get called questionable. That is part of why the word leans toward playing. These are NFL players and NFL labels; a college staff may use words differently. Use the number as a starting point, not a verdict.

What the Money Did

DraftKings opened Notre Dame as a 7½-point favorite on Sunday. By Wednesday night, with the news out, it was 7. Half a point, toward Miami. The other bet moved the other way: strip out the bookmaker’s cut and Notre Dame’s chance to win went from about 68% to 69%. Those are mixed signals; neither is bigger than one point.

We do not have a measured number for what one receiver is worth on the scoreboard, and we will not make one up. We can tell you what a point is worth. In our ratings, for a game this close, each point of spread moves the win chance by two or three percentage points. Our ratings are less sure of Notre Dame than the market is, 57% against 69%. Sunday will tell us who read it better.

Every Story Fits

You have heard them already, or you will by Friday. He is really hurt. It is gamesmanship, so Notre Dame has to prepare for two offenses. It is an excuse loaded in advance if Miami loses, or a medal waiting if Miami wins without him.

Notice that “uncertain, pushing to play” fits all four. When a piece of news fits every story, it cannot tell you which story is true. That is the idea behind information and surprisei. So do not spend your week arguing about the story. Start from the base ratei and wait for the one fact that can move it: did he practice?

What to Take Home

Questionable means “probably plays.” Two in three, across more than 14,000 NFL cases.

The practice report beats the injury label. 71% for players who practiced, 44% for those who sat.

Watch the line, not the talk. Half a point is not panic.

A report that fits every story tells you only the base rate. Wait for the fact that does not fit them all.
