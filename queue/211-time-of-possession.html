Does Keeping the Ball Win Football Games? Check Which Came First.

In 7,000 NFL games since 1999, the team that held the ball longer won 68 of every 100. That sounds like a recipe. It is mostly backward. Up by nine or more in the fourth quarter, teams run the ball 67 times in 100; down by nine, 22. Winning makes possession time, not the other way around.

The question: does keeping the ball win football games?

The answer: mostly, it is the other way around. Teams that get ahead start running the ball, and running eats the clock. They pile up possession time because they were already winning.

Why that is surprising: the broadcast puts time of possession on the screen as if it were a recipe, and a computer agrees with it loudly. In every NFL game since 1999, the team that held the ball longer won 68 times in 100. A number that steady feels like a cause.

What you get: a picture you can draw on a napkin, called a causal diagram, and a habit: before you believe a number explains a win, ask which way the arrow points.

Draw the arrows first

Here is the whole story in five boxes. Each arrow means “causes.” Follow them from the left.

Start with the box you cannot see. A good team has a better offensive line than the defensive line across from it, and it blocks better. No box score records that. It does two things. It gets the team a lead. And it makes running plays work.

Now a team has a lead. A lead wins games, plainly. But a lead also changes the play calls: ahead, you run, because a run keeps the clock moving and every minute that passes is a minute the other side cannot use. Running fills up the time-of-possession column. So time of possession sits at the end of a chain that started with winning. It can sit there, perfectly correlated with the result, without causing any of it.

Every arrow, measured

We checked each solid arrow against ten seasons of NFL play-by-play, 2016 through 2025, about 330,000 runs and passes.

Lead → run. In the fourth quarter, a team ahead by nine or more runs 67 times in every 100 plays. A team behind by nine or more runs 22 times. Notice that the team behind is not choosing to pass because passing is better; it is passing because it has run out of time.

Run → clock. After a run, the next snap comes about 36 seconds later. After a pass, about 25, because an incomplete pass stops the clock. Late in a game the gap is even wider: ahead by nine, a team snaps the ball every 34 seconds; behind by nine, every 23.

And the run is not chosen because it gains more. It gains less: 4.5 yards a play, against 6.2 for a dropback, sacks included. A team that could run as well as it passes would be remarkable. Teams run when ahead to spend the clock, not to gain yards.

Time of possession can be perfectly correlated with winning and cause none of it.

— The Sports Page

What happens when you put it all in

Here is where it gets strange, and where the diagram earns its keep. We gave a computer about 14,000 NFL team-games, 1999 through 2025, and asked what predicts a win.

Give it the number of plays alone, and more plays means more wins. Give it time of possession alone, and more time means more wins. Give it both at once, and the number of plays flips: now more plays means fewer wins, and the computer is more certain of that than it was of the first answer.

Nothing about football changed between those two runs. What changed was the question. Plays and possession time move together: the more time you have, the more plays you get. So putting both in asks something narrower. among teams that held the ball the same length of time, did the one with more plays win more? And a team that squeezes extra plays into the same time is hurrying, and a team hurries when it is behind. The diagram tells you that before the computer does.

Notice what the big sample did not do. With 14,000 team-games, every one of those answers clears any test of statistical significance you like, including the two that contradict each other. A huge sample makes a number precise. It cannot make it mean what you hoped.

What to take home

Draw the arrows before you trust the number. When a stat travels with winning, ask whether it could be a result of winning. Time of possession, rushing attempts and a quarterback’s kneel-downs all go up when a team is ahead. None of them is why it got ahead.

Adding more numbers can make the answer worse. Two measures of the same thing, put in together, can turn each other upside down. More data, more variables and a smaller p-value are not the same as understanding.

The most important box is often the one you cannot see. Here it is line play: who wins up front, play after play. It drives the lead, the running game and the result, and it is in nobody’s box score. When a model leaves out the thing that matters most, whatever it does include will take the credit.

How we did it

Data. Team box scores for 1999–2025 regular-season games from nflverse (data/nfl/team-games.csv): plays, yards, time of possession, turnovers, result; ties left out, 13,898 team-games. The team with more possession time won 4,693 of 6,942 games. Play-by-play for 2016–2025 from nflverse, runs and passes only (sacks count as passes; kneel-downs and spikes dropped), 330,000 plays.

The models. Logistic regressions of win on same-game plays and time of possession, alone and together. Plays alone: positive, z = 15. Time alone: positive, z = 45. Together: plays turns negative, z = −30. Seconds to the next snap are measured between consecutive plays by the same offense in the same quarter, capped at 60.

Limits. The diagram is our claim, not something the data proves; data can rule arrows out more easily than in. Time of possession may still help a little in its own right (a tired defense, fewer chances for the other side), and nothing here says it is worth zero. It says the raw link is mostly the scoreboard talking. Code: research/kitchen-sink/ in our repository.
