Will We Still Cheer for Humans in 90 Years? Start With 1936.

Jesse Owens won the 1936 Olympic 100 meters in 10.3 seconds. The 2024 winner was only about 5% faster. But in 1936, big-league pitchers finished 43 of every 100 games they started. This season, about 4 in 1,000 did. Ninety years changed the games far more than the people playing them.
By The Sports Page · 1936 to 2026

Dedicated to the man who was not a sports fan. Not at all. But he was a good sport.

The question: how different is sport from 90 years ago, and will we still be cheering for humans 90 years from now, when machines can do most of it better?

The answer: the bodies changed less than the games did. Owens ran 10.3 seconds in Berlin; Noah Lyles won Paris in 9.79. That’s about 5%. Meanwhile, the complete game went from 43 of every 100 starts to about 4 in 1,000.

Why it’s surprising: you’d expect the athletes to be the big change. They’re the smallest one. The rules, the strategy and the math moved far more. What you get: a way to think about the AI question with numbers instead of dread.

Twelve numbers, 1936 and now

Read the chart from the middle line. A dot on the line means nothing changed. The 100-meter dash sits right next to it. The dots far from it are mostly choices people made.

Baseball: the pitcher stopped finishing

In 1936 the American and National Leagues had 16 clubs. Pitchers finished 1,075 of 2,476 starts. In 2026, with 30 clubs, they finished 21 of 4,858. Batters struck out in about 8 of every 100 trips to the plate; now it’s 22. Home runs per game doubled, and batting averages fell from .284 to .244.

None of that is because arms got weaker or bats got worse. Teams learned, mostly by counting, that a tired starter facing a lineup a third time is a bad bet, and that a strikeout is a fair price for a home run. Notice that the math changed the game before any machine did.

One more change belongs here. MLB’s own records now count seven clubs from the 1936 Negro National League as major league. In 1936 those players weren’t allowed in the American or National League. Their clubs scored 6.3 runs a game.

College football: half the games had a zero

In 1936, the year the AP poll began, a major-college team scored about 11 points a game. Nearly half of all games ended with one team held scoreless, and 7 or 8 of every 100 ended in a tie. This season, teams score 26 a game, a shutout happens in about 3 games in 100, and nobody ties. The poll’s first champion was Minnesota.

Hockey and the track

The 1935–36 NHL had eight teams scoring about 2.2 goals a game; last season’s 32 scored about 3.1. That spring, Detroit beat the Montreal Maroons 1–0 in a playoff game that ran six overtimes. Mud Bruneteau scored the only goal, 116½ minutes into overtime.

On the track, Son Gi-jeong, a Korean runner entered under Japan’s flag, won the 1936 marathon in 2:29:19. Tamirat Tola won Paris in 2:06:26. That’s 23 minutes, about 15%, a big gain for a runner. Still, it’s small next to what happened in the pool, and tiny next to the complete game.

In the pool: get out your best time

Swimmers, this one’s for you. Japan led the 1936 pool: it won 11 of the 33 swimming medals, more than any other country, and 4 of the 11 golds. The Netherlands also won 4, and the United States won 2.

Now find your own best time. Ferenc Csík of Hungary won the men’s 100-meter freestyle in 57.6 seconds. Rie Mastenbroek of the Netherlands won the women’s in 1:05.9. If you’ve beaten those in a 50-meter pool, you’d have stood on the top step in Berlin. The 2024 winners swam 46.40 and 52.16.

Look how much farther the pool moved than the track. The Olympic sprint got about 5% faster in 88 years. Winning swim times got 19% to 27% faster. Swimmers didn’t grow 20% stronger. They got better strokes, better training, and pools built to keep the water calm.

The players changed a little. The decisions changed a lot.

— The Sports Page

And the next 90 years?

Here’s the honest part: we have no model for 2116, and anybody who hands you a number is guessing. What 90 years of data can tell you is where change comes from. The numbers that measure a person against a clock moved least. The big moves came from people deciding to play differently, often because somebody did the math. Machines are joining that part now; they’re already in the dugout and the film room.

Will we still cheer for people? A car could beat Son’s marathon time the day he ran it, and nobody stopped watching the marathon. IBM’s Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, and people still play for the world chess title. We don’t cheer because a human is the fastest thing on earth. We cheer because it’s hard for a person. Our guess, and call it a guess: in 2116 the machines will be in the press box, doing what we try to do here. The people will still be on the field.

The limits, plainly. Track times in 1936 were timed by hand, to a tenth of a second, and Owens’s final had a following wind. “Major college” is drawn a little differently by our two college sources: 469 games in one, 429 in the other, with the same 11 points a team.

What to take home

When someone says “the game has changed,” ask which part. Look at the clock first. Then look at the choices. In 90 years, the choices moved the numbers ten times as much.
