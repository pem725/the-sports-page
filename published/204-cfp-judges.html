Is the Playoff Rigged Against Small Schools? Ask the Bowl Games.

Over 12 seasons, the committee ranked small-conference teams about five spots lower than their scores earned. Then those teams met the big conferences in bowl games and fell six points short of the same math. The judges knew something. This week’s real argument is Miami, not Texas.

The question: is the playoff committee judging like a figure-skating panel, with a thumb on the scale? You’ve heard the charge. Notre Dame beat Boston College 41–7 on Saturday and slid from fifth to sixth, and Texas moved past them.

The answer: across 70 rankings since 2014, the judges put small-conference teams about five spots below where their scores put them. That looks like a fix; it isn’t one. Here’s the surprise: when those same teams played the big conferences in bowl games, they lost by six points more than the math expected. You’d expect a biased panel to be wrong. This one was mostly right.

What you get: a way to tell a judge with a thumb on the scale from a judge who knows something your spreadsheet doesn’t. We’ll run this scorecard every Tuesday until Selection Day, December 6.

Who the judges are, and what we can’t see

The 2026 committee has 13 members. Six are sitting athletic directors: Arkansas (the chair, Hunter Yurachek), Nebraska, Utah, Virginia, Middle Tennessee and App State. Four are former head coaches: Gus Malzahn, Jeff Tedford, Mark Dantonio and Mike Riley. Two are former players, Randall McDaniel and Wesley Walls, and one is a writer, Ivan Maisel.

An athletic director leaves the room when their own school comes up. Utah is No. 2 this week, so Utah’s AD doesn’t vote on Utah. The committee publishes its rules: seven rounds of voting build the top 25. It does not publish anyone’s ballot. So you can’t grade one judge. You can grade the panel.

How we graded the panel

For every ranking since 2014, we built our own from the scores alone, using only games already played that week. We used two numbers. The first is how much a team outscores people, adjusted for who it played. The second is how many more games it won than a top-12 team would have won against the same schedule. Average the two and you get our ranking. Then count the spots between ours and theirs.

Notice the open circles first. The judges rank the big conferences zero to three spots above our math, and the small schools five below. The AP poll’s reporters do nearly the same thing, about five spots below for small schools. So this isn’t a committee habit; it’s everybody’s habit.

Then we checked the judges’ work

A gap between a judge and a formula proves one of them is wrong. It doesn’t say which. So look at the games that settle it. In 81 bowl and playoff games between a small-conference team and a big-conference team, our math expected the small school to win by about 1.5 points. It lost by about 4.5 and won 34 of the 81. SEC teams, in 134 postseason games against other leagues, beat our math by 3.5 points.

Give the small schools that six-point haircut, give the SEC its 3.5, and run it again. Now look at the filled dots. The small-school penalty is gone. The SEC lands about three spots lower than the corrected math.

Why did our math miss it? It learns how strong a conference is mostly from a few games in September between leagues. That’s a small sample. The judges’ eyes, and their memory of last January’s bowls, fill the gap.

Who rides along, and who gets left out

About 7 of every 100 ranked spots, 120 of 1,750, went to teams our math had outside the top 30. The repeat riders weren’t Alabama or Ohio State. They were Oklahoma State, ten times, and Northwestern, nine. The teams left out while our math had them in its top 15 were all small schools: James Madison in nine rankings, Marshall in seven, App State in six.

So what about this week?

With the bowl correction in, Texas is fifth on the committee’s list and sixth on ours, and Notre Dame is sixth and fifth. The swap that annoyed you is one spot. Call it a coin toss. The real gaps:

  • Miami (9–1): seventh by the committee, third by the corrected math.
  • Boise State (9–1): 17th by the committee, tenth by ours.
  • USC (7–3): 18th by the committee, 29th by ours.
  • South Carolina and Oklahoma (both 6–4): 21st and 22nd by the committee, 28th and 27th by ours.

The limits, plainly. Our bowl correction comes from the same 12 seasons we’re grading, and it’s loose: give or take about two points. Bowl games have their own mess, with players sitting out and coaches leaving. Read this as “mostly right,” not “proved right.”

What to take home

When a judge disagrees with your numbers, check the judge’s record before you call it a fix. These judges mark down small schools, and the bowl games say they mostly should. Ask for the ballots anyway. A panel this good has nothing to hide.
