Two Hundred Seventy Division Champions. Not One Finished Below .500. Now One Does.

Entering today, both the Astros and Rangers are 80–81. The AL West winner will be the worst division champion in 57 years of baseball. Two days ago, we said the probability of this was 70%. Saturday’s results made it 100%. That is what a working prediction looks like.

The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. Five issues, one gradeable prediction: one hit, zero missed, zero still pending from this week. Four pieces made no forward forecast at all — they were methods and retrospective analysis, and that is legitimate work. But the one piece that did make a prediction made a big one.

Here is the surprising part. The most interesting thing this week is not a miss. It is a hit that was hiding in plain sight: on Friday we published a 70% probability that the AL West division champion would finish at .500 or below for the first time in 57 years. Saturday’s results — both teams winning — removed the only scenario where that prediction fails. The 70% is now 100%. Not because we were lucky. Because the math was right.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story.

  3. #180Sep 24 — The Crowd Splits Every Game in Half. You Still Lose a Thousand Dollars. — A point spread is a fair estimate of the outcome. The price is not. Both sides at −110 add to 104.76%, and that 4.76% surplus never plays a down. Break-even requires being right 52.4% of the time.
  4. #181Sep 25 — Turnovers Win Games. They Do Not Win Seasons. — A season’s turnover margin explains 3% of the next season’s. Yards per play explains 15.4%. Turnovers feel like character and behave like weather. Across 288 team-seasons, a team that wins the turnover battle one year wins it again the next only slightly more than randomly.
  5. #182Sep 26 — Two Teams Are Tied With Losing Records. One Is Going to the Playoffs. — Houston and Texas both 79–80 with three games left. Across 270 division winners since 1969, not one finished below .500. We gave a 70% probability that one of them would. They both won Saturday.

The Call That Came True: Issue #182

What the Math Said. What Happened.

On Friday, we built a simulation of the remaining schedule from 79–80 with three games each. Houston was facing the Athletics (63–96), Texas was facing the Twins (74–85). We gave Houston a 58% chance per game, Texas a 51% chance. You can read the full piece at Issue #182. Here is what the distribution said:

82-80 (0.506): 30% — would tie the worst ever (2005 Padres) 81-81 (0.500): 52% — first ever .500 division champion 80-82 (0.494): 18% — first ever below-.500 champion 79-83 (0.488): 1% — historically unprecedented

The 30% path — the only historical precedent — required both teams to sweep their remaining three games. Both teams won on Saturday, moving to 80–81. The sweep path is now closed. The champion cannot exceed 81–81.

We said the probability of an unprecedented finish was 70%. It is now 100%. The winner today will be either the first .500 division champion ever, or the first below-.500 one. Both are new. Check the standings; the game starts at 2pm ET.

What We Got Right

The AL West Call

Issue #182 on Friday was the paper’s most precise probabilistic forecast since the Soto injury call in the spring. We gave a 70% probability of something that had never happened in 57 years of baseball. Both teams then won on Saturday, which removed the only path to a historical outcome and locked in the unprecedented one. The forecast was right and it was right for the right reason: we did not say “one of these teams is bad”. We computed from game odds and remaining schedule that the 82–80 finish required a sweep by teams averaging less than 50% across the season. The math did the work.

Notice also what the model said was most likely: 81–81 at 52%. That is now the ceiling. The single most probable outcome in Friday’s simulation is now also the best possible outcome. When a model’s most likely scenario becomes the ceiling of reality, that is a sign the model understood the situation. You can hold onto that: when the most likely bar becomes the upper bound, you were not lucky. You were right about where the probability lived.

What We Got Wrong

South Carolina Is Trending the Wrong Way

Nothing from this week’s issues was materially wrong. The methods pieces hold. The JUCO piece is observational and accurate. The big prediction hit.

But the carry-over from Issue #143 — our projection that South Carolina would win 7.8 games out of 12 — is showing early strain. They are 2–2 and 0–2 in SEC play after a 49–18 loss to Alabama. That is not a fatal result for a 7.8-win projection; they have eight games left and need to go 5–3. But 0–2 in conference play against Mississippi State and Alabama is not the opening the model expected. Notice what the loss to Alabama tells you: a 31-point margin against a team you had projected to beat roughly half your conference games is meaningful evidence. We are not calling a miss yet. We are watching, and saying out loud that the projection has harder work to do now.

What Yesterday’s College Football Showed

Week 4 of the college football season ran Saturday, and the numbers carried two stories worth naming.

The first is Penn State. They beat Buffalo 55–13 last week. That is a 42-point margin, a result that should make Penn State a comfortable home favorite this Saturday. Instead, Wisconsin came to State College and won 24–20. Penn State lost outright as what was almost certainly a double-digit home favorite. Issue #175, published September 19, made a directional claim: teams winning by 20 or more points cover the spread only 47.9% the following week. Penn State is the cleanest possible data point for that thesis. One game is not a pattern. But one game is not nothing either, especially when the result is an outright home loss.

The second story is Texas A&M. They went to LSU and lost 35–6. Last week, you will remember, Kentucky beat them at home as a 16.5-point underdog — the largest upset of Week 3 by raw probability. The market repriced A&M immediately; they were an 8.5-point road underdog at LSU rather than a double-digit favorite. Then they lost by 29. Texas A&M is now 1–3 on the season. The market was right to downgrade them. An upset is always explained afterward. But sometimes the explanation is simply: the team is not good.

The Improbability Lesson

Notice the difference between those two stories. Penn State being upset by Wisconsin is evidence for the blowout-winner regression thesis, but it is still noise in a one-week window. Texas A&M being outscored 35–6 in the game after an 18-point underdog beat them is something different. That is a body of evidence accumulating.

The lesson both carry is the same one the AL West is teaching today: “nobody saw this coming” almost always means “nobody who was paying attention to the right numbers.” A model that gave Kentucky 12% against A&M was not surprised that the upset happened. It was only uncertain about which game would produce it. Across a 49-game Saturday card, roughly five or six underdogs should win on a normal week. The list of 1-in-9 shots is long. Kentucky happened to be on it. Your job — and this paper’s job — is to treat a 1-in-9 result as exactly that: evidence the model was used correctly, not evidence it was wrong.

Notre Dame remained steady: 4–0, beat Purdue 49–10. Oregon beat #12 USC on the road, 41–27, with their backup quarterback. The teams that looked like the good teams in Week 1 are, with one notable exception (Oregon’s Week 2 stumble against Oklahoma State), still the good teams. That is what 21% predictive power from Week 1 looks like in practice: not certainty, but a prior that updates more slowly than the market does.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

The AL West champion is not a bad team that snuck through. They are a team that played in a weak division in a year when both the Astros and Rangers had meaningful injuries and roster turnover. Do not write the AL West off as broken. Write it as five teams that were bad at approximately the same level. The trophy still goes to whoever wins today. One bad division in one season is not a structural crisis.

Penn State is not in freefall. They lost one game at home, as a big favorite, to a Wisconsin team that has beaten teams like this before. One result is one result. Watch the next three before calling it a collapse.

Under-Reactions

Texas A&M at 1–3 is worth taking seriously. This is not a team having bad luck. They lost to Kentucky at home as a 16.5-point favorite, then lost to LSU by 29 on the road. The market repriced them aggressively and the market was right. Do not call this variance. Call it what the 35–6 score suggests: a team with real problems.

South Carolina at 0–2 in SEC play deserves attention. A 7.8-win projection assumes they beat the teams they should beat in conference play. A 31-point loss to Alabama is not “just the schedule.” The next game tells us more.
