Notre Dame Got a Week Off. It Bought About One Point.

Across more than 13,000 college games since 2005, a bye added about one point to a team’s final margin. Home field is worth three. And for a team coming off a loss, like the Irish, the week off was worth close to nothing.

Do bye weeks help? You hear it every fall. A team gets a week off. It heals, it studies film, and it comes back sharp. Notre Dame lost at BYU, 28–24. It sat out last Saturday, and it plays Navy this Saturday. So did the rest help?

Here is the answer, with its number. A bye adds about one point. We checked more than 13,000 games between major-college teams since 2005. The rested team did about one point better than its rating said it should. Home field, by comparison, is worth about three.

That should surprise you a little. Most fans talk about the bye like a weapon. It is a nudge. And here is the part that matters for the Irish: teams coming off a loss got almost none of it.

By the end of this, you will be able to price a week off yourself. You will also know why “they had two weeks to get ready” is mostly a story told after the game.

Start With the Trap

You cannot just count wins after a bye. Some teams that rest are great, and some are bad. In our games, the rested team was the underdog about half the time. So a raw win rate tells you who gets byes. It does not tell you what a bye does.

We needed a rating that knew nothing about rest. So we used the pregame Elo rating from CollegeFootballData. Think of it as a running score of how strong each team is. Then we asked one question: did the rested team beat its rating?

It did, barely. Teams off a bye won 49% of those games. Their ratings said they would win 49%. On the scoreboard, they came out about one point ahead. The likely range runs from under half a point to nearly two. We checked it a second way, rating each team by its season margin with that game left out. That gave about 0.8 points. Two methods, one answer: real, and small.

Look at the gold bars next to the blue ones. A week off is worth about a third as much as playing at home. One point decides very few games. That is why the win rates barely move, even though the points do.

The NFL Says the Same Thing

The pros get a bye too, and we have every game since 1999. There, a week off is worth about 1.3 points; home field is worth about 2.6. NFL teams off a bye won 54% of the time when their ratings said 50%.

Bettors already know this. The rested team beat the point spread 51% of the time, which is a coin flip. The market charges you for the rest before you can collect it.

The edge may be shrinking. From 1999 to 2010 a bye was worth about two points. Since 2011, the year a new labor deal cut practice time, it has been worth about half a point. But the two ranges overlap, so we would not bet on that story yet.

Price a week off at one point. Then go back to the question that matters: who is better?

Coming Off a Loss

Now split the college teams by how their last game went. After a win, a bye was worth about two points. After a loss, about a third of a point — the same as nothing. Teams that lost and then rested won 42% of the time. Their ratings said 43%.

Be careful with this one. The gap is big enough to notice and too small to swear by; it rests on about 1,100 games a side. But notice what it does not show. It does not show the wounded team that uses two weeks to heal and comes back angry. If that team exists, it is rare.

So what about Saturday? Notre Dame is 22–7 off a bye since 2005. That record says more about Notre Dame than about the bye. Navy is 2–5, and it played last week. Whatever the week off is worth, this game will not turn on it.

What to Take Home

The next time an announcer says a team “had two weeks to prepare,” price it at one point. Then ask the question that settles most games: how good is each team? Home field is worth three times as much. A good roster is worth far more than either.

And watch for the story that gets told afterward. When a rested team wins, the bye gets the credit. When it loses, nobody mentions it. That is how a one-point edge grows into a legend.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

College: every regular-season game between two major-college (FBS) teams from 2005 to 2025, from CollegeFootballData. A “bye” means 12 to 16 days since the team’s last game; a normal week is 6 to 9. Season openers and longer gaps were left out. Strength is the pregame Elo rating; we checked it against each team’s season margin with that game removed. Short weeks and home field were accounted for.

NFL: every regular-season game from 1999 to 2025, from nflverse, with rest days as that data records them. A bye means 13 or more days of rest.

What it cannot tell you: schedule makers choose where byes go, so a bye is not a coin flip. We cannot see injuries, so we cannot say whether rest helps a hurt team more than a healthy one. And the after-a-loss split is one of several cuts we looked at; treat it as a lead, not a finding.
