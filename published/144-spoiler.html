The Mets Cannot Ruin Anybody. The Athletics Can Ruin Four Seasons.

Both clubs are finished. One of them plays the Yankees, the Rays and the Phillies down the stretch; the other plays Seattle seven times. Only one of them still decides anything, and it is not the one you are watching.

The question: which eliminated team can still do the most damage?

The answer: the Athletics, and it is not close. Their remaining games can move 201 points of other clubs’ playoff chances. The Mets can move 93.

Here is the surprising part. We expected the Mets. They play the Yankees, the Rays and the Phillies, on national television, in the loudest market in the sport. They turn out to be the fourth-most consequential dead team in baseball.

What you get from it: a way to tell a game that is watched from a game that matters. Look for it tonight.

A spoiler needs somebody to spoil

Start with the thing that sounds obvious and is not. Beat a club that has already clinched and you change nothing; they are in either way. Beat a club already eliminated and you change nothing either.

A win only moves the standings if the other team’s season is still undecided. That is the whole of it. So the spoiler is never whoever plays the famous teams. It is whoever plays the undecided ones.

So we ran the rest of the season 80,000 times. For every game a dead club has left, we asked how far its opponent’s playoff chances move depending on who wins. Add those up and you have a number: how much wreckage each dead team can still cause.

Oakland has the schedule of an assassin

Count them. Twenty-one of the Athletics’ twenty-six remaining games are against clubs whose seasons are still in the balance. Seven against Seattle, who sit at 14%. Four against Houston at 64%. Three against Cleveland at 64%, three against Texas at 29%, three against Toronto at 13%.

A 53–83 club is about to play a month of games in which every single result matters to somebody. Those seven games with Seattle alone carry more weight than the Mets’ entire remaining schedule.

And the Mets have the schedule of a celebrity

Fifteen of the Mets’ twenty-six are against clubs whose fate is already sealed. Three with the Rays and three with the Yankees, both at 100% — those six games cannot alter anything at all. Three with the Marlins, three with the Nationals, three with the Giants, all effectively gone.

Even the four against Philadelphia, which look like the marquee, are worth less than they appear; the Phillies are already at 94%.

Eleven of twenty-six matter. The Mets will be on television constantly and will decide almost nothing.

Beat a team already in and nothing changes. Beat a team already out and nothing changes. All of the weight sits with the undecided.

The Professor

We got this wrong first

Worth admitting, because it is the same mistake the coverage will make. Setting out, we assumed the Mets were the story. Eliminated, playing contenders, in New York — the spoiler piece writes itself.

The arithmetic said fourth. Behind Oakland, behind Kansas City at 162 points, behind Colorado at 152. Three clubs almost nobody is watching, all of whom hold more of September in their hands.

What we had noticed was that the Mets play famous opponents. We took that for important ones. In late August those are opposite things. A famous club is usually good enough to have settled the question already.

What to take home: ask who is still deciding

When somebody tells you a fixture is important, ask who it is still undecided for. If the answer is nobody, it is an event, not a contest.

Carry the habit off the sports page. The meeting that matters is rarely the one with the senior people in it; it is the one where something is still open. A vote in a safe seat is a ceremony. A vote in a close one is a decision. Same ballot, same effort, completely different weight.

The Athletics will finish thirty games under .500 in an empty stadium, and they are about to decide who plays in October. Nobody will televise it.

Notes & sources

Standings and schedules from the MLB Stats API on the morning of August 30, 2026. Playoff probabilities come from simulating all remaining games 80,000 times, with team strength a blend of record and Pythagorean expectation regressed toward .500, log5 game probabilities and a .535 home-field adjustment; ties are broken at random rather than by the real tiebreaker rules.

Damage is measured by conditioning inside that single simulation: for each remaining game, the opponent’s playoff rate across the seasons where the home side won is compared with the seasons where it lost, and the absolute difference is summed across the club’s schedule. Conditioning this way is exact because games are independent given team strength, and far cheaper than re-simulating each game twice.

Totals for clubs under 1%: Athletics 201 points over 26 games, Royals 162 over 25, Rockies 152 over 26, Mets 93 over 26, Reds 66, Angels 61, Nationals 61, Giants 52, Pirates 26. Per game the Athletics average 7.7 points against the Mets’ 3.6.

A limit worth naming. This counts how much a club can move, not how much it will; a team that loses out moves nothing. It also treats all opponents alike, so it says nothing about who deserves to win. And the probabilities themselves shift daily — a club at 14% today may be settled within a week, and the leverage on its remaining games would vanish with it.
