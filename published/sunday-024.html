Six issues, zero forward forecasts — a week we spent teaching you how a backtest breaks and why home field disappeared before the empty stadiums. While we did that, Kentucky walked into College Station as a 1-in-9 shot. The final was 31–21. Here is how to think about a number like that.

The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. Six issues, no gradeable predictions: zero hit, zero missed, zero still pending from this week. Five of six were methods or historical analysis — not bets. Issue #175 made one forward-looking lean that is too new to grade. We are naming it now so it can be graded later.

Here is the surprising part. A quiet scorecard does not mean a quiet week. The most interesting thing that happened yesterday was not in any of our six pieces — it was on a football field in College Station, Texas.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story. When there is nothing to grade, we say so in one line and teach you something instead.

The Week in Review: Issues #170–175

  1. #170Sep 14 — Michigan Nearly Lost to Western Michigan. Eight Days Later It Beat Oklahoma. — How much does last week’s result explain this week’s? Three tenths of one percent. We measured it across 236 teams.
  2. #171Sep 15 — Nobody Wins 102% of the Time. — Home field in baseball is worth one constant: +0.14. Percentages cannot hold a constant and have it mean the same thing everywhere. Log-odds can. That is the whole reason the log is there.
  3. #172Sep 16 — Home Teams Stopped Winning in 2019. The Crowds Left in 2020. — NFL home teams averaged +2.4 points from 2016 to 2018. In 2019, with every seat sold, the edge was −0.14. The empty-stadium season that followed looked the same.
  4. #173Sep 17 — Oregon Lost One Game. Now Nobody Can Agree How Good They Are. — After one loss, one system has Oregon 5th. Another has them 76th. The gap between those two numbers tells you more than either ranking does.
  5. #174Sep 18 — Our Model Went 15 for 15. That Is How We Knew It Was Broken. — A perfect backtest is a bug report. Rebuilt so cheating was impossible, the method beat the spread 51.0% across 2,140 games. The confidence interval contains 50.
  6. #175Sep 19 — Mississippi State Won by 25. The Numbers Say Minnesota Is Just Bad. — Same game: ratings moved Mississippi State up 0.6 points and moved Minnesota down 4.9. Nearly all the information was in the team that lost.

The improbable thing that happened yesterday

Kentucky beat Texas A&M 31–21 in College Station. Texas A&M was favoured by about 20 points.

Turn that into a probability using our ratings, frozen Thursday before any of these games were played, and Kentucky had about a 10.5% chance — call it 1 in 9. Our frozen SP+ had Texas A&M at 24.2 and Kentucky at 6.2, a gap of 18 points before you add home field. The number of times an underdog that far back wins its own game is roughly one in nine.

So: a stunner. Now here is the part people get wrong.

The college football card on Saturday had roughly fifty rated games on it. Add up every underdog’s probability of winning its own game and you get a number — the number of upsets a normal Saturday should produce. One 1-in-9 shot is startling in isolation. Across fifty games, something in that range is close to inevitable. That is why every “nobody saw this coming” segment misses the point. Somebody did; roughly half a dozen somebodies, each wrong about which game.

The surprise was never that an upset occurred. The surprise is only ever which one.

What We Got Right

The Confession That Was the Whole Point

Issue #174 — “Our Model Went 15 for 15. That Is How We Knew It Was Broken” — was this week’s most important piece, and it did not make a prediction. It retracted one. A backtest that looked perfect turned out to have been cheating: the model could see future ratings while scoring past games. Strip that out and it beats the spread 51.0% of the time across 2,140 games. The 95% confidence interval runs from 48.9% to 53.1%. Break-even is 52.4%.

We said in the headline that we were wrong. We meant it. A newsletter that grades other people’s predictions cannot quietly bury its own.

Home Field: The Math Held

Issue #172 found that NFL home-field advantage vanished in 2019 — before empty stadiums in 2020. The historical pattern in our data is exactly what the log-odds model from Issue #171 would predict: when two evenly matched teams play, home field is worth more percentage points than when the gap between them is large. The edge disappears when the mental load of travel stops being the binding constraint. That is not a forecast. It is a measurement. It survived the week without contradiction.

What We Got Wrong (or Did Not Say)

Six Issues Without a Bet

We said last Sunday that the ratio of analysis to prediction had been running high, and we would fix it by being bolder. Then we published six consecutive pieces with no forward predictions. Four were genuinely not the kind of piece that carries a forecast: a backtest confession, a log-odds explainer, a historical survey of home field, a measurement of rating-system disagreement. That is legitimate.

But Issue #170 — the miss-persistence piece — found something interesting: last week explains three tenths of one percent of next week, across 236 teams. That is nearly zero. Notice that we said so without asking: if last week explains nothing, does this week’s opening number explain anything? We did not run that. We could have. A finding that small is often the entrance to a better question. We did not walk through it.

Issue #175 is the exception: it carries a specific one-week lean. We tracked it above. It will be graded next Sunday.

South Carolina: The First Real Evidence

The prediction from Issue #143 was that South Carolina was worth about 7.8 wins — better than their preseason ranking suggested. Yesterday they lost their SEC opener to Mississippi State 41–34. One loss in conference play is data, not a verdict. But notice what the loss is: it is to a Mississippi State team our own ratings have at 10.0 SP+ versus South Carolina’s 16.9. The lower-rated team won at home. That is exactly the kind of result the prediction needs not to keep producing. One loss is still well within the range of a 7.8-win season. Seven games remain. Watch carefully.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

Kentucky is not a top-ten team. They beat a top-ten team. Those are different things. The frozen SP+ had them at 6.2 — well below the top half of the league. A 31–21 win over Texas A&M does move Kentucky’s rating, but not to top-ten range. The win changes the evidence, not the history. One game raises the posterior; it does not erase the prior. The teams that have been good all year are still, on balance, the teams to worry about in December.

Treat yesterday the same way you treat any 1-in-9 event: update, but do not overwrite.

Under-Reactions

Texas A&M losing at home to a 20-point underdog is not a small event. Our own ratings absorb new results more slowly than the market does, which is a design choice — but the market will move A&M sharply this week. The team that opens next Saturday as an 8-point favourite will be a different A&M in the market’s eyes than the one that opened at −20 yesterday. That recalibration is worth watching, not because the market is always right, but because the speed of its adjustment tells you something about how seriously it takes yesterday’s evidence.

MLB playoffs begin September 29. The Brewers at 92–56 are running away with the NL Central and generating almost no national coverage. That will not last.

What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

Take this home from yesterday: a 1-in-9 result is not evidence that the rating was wrong. It is evidence that the rating was being used correctly. A model that never produces 1-in-9 upsets is not a good model — it is a model that refuses to commit to a number.

This week: we owe you a piece that is not a methods explainer. The queue has three pieces that carry actual predictions. One is about possession collapse in soccer — a team that controls the ball for 65% of a match and loses. Another looks at turnover persistence: whether teams that win the turnover battle in Week 1 keep winning it, or whether it regresses. The third examines the vig — the house edge embedded in every spread — and what it means that the public consistently pays it.

South Carolina plays again in three days. The prediction from Issue #143 gets its next data point. Watch for it here Sunday.

And then, when the baseball playoffs begin September 29, we have something we have been building since March: a log-odds projection for each series, made before it starts, graded publicly as it goes. That is the kind of bet the Sunday Edition exists to make and check.
