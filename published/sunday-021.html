One Forecast Was Gradeable. It Landed.

The newsletter spent Monday through Friday explaining hot windows, hype floors, ranking gaps, and home-field splits. Not one of those five pieces made a forward prediction. Then Thursday it made four. The one it made three weeks ago just came due. Boston will play in October.

The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. 6 issues published Monday through Saturday. 1 gradeable prediction: 1 hit, 0 misses, 0 pending from this week's pieces. 4 fresh predictions landed Thursday in Issue #153 — those clock in as pending, too new to score. 5 pieces made no forward forecast at all. That is honest accounting, not a complaint: five analysis pieces and zero claims is sometimes exactly the right call.

No miss this week. What you get instead is a confirmation. On August 10, with Boston at 62–51 and fresh off a 25–3 run, this newsletter said they had a 94% chance of reaching the October playoffs. Twenty days later, their updated record sits at 73–60 and they hold a Wild Card slot; the rerun model gives them 99.3%. The prediction held. Call it what it is: a HIT.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story. If you can read it without knowing whether it was right, it was not a prediction. It was a pose.

  1. #149Aug 24 — A Winning Streak Is Worth Twice as Much in May as in April. — Boston’s 14-game July run saved their season. If the Mets won 14 straight today, their playoff odds would move 1.6 points. Same streak. Different month.
  2. #150Aug 25 — Notre Dame Gets Talked About All Summer. So Does Everybody Else. — Notre Dame draws 18,884 Wikipedia visits in a dead June. 70,836 in a live October. The hype floor is 27% of peak, and every blue blood sits within two points of that same share.
  3. #151Aug 26 — Kansas City Has Won Seven Straight. They Are a .435 Team. — The Royals and the Phillies had the identical 10-game record. Stretch to 50 games and one is a contender and the other is 57–74. Hot is not a fact you look up; it is a choice about how far back you count.
  4. #152Aug 27 — Two Teams a Rank Apart? The Higher One Wins 48.7% of the Time. — When #6 plays #7, the poll says one is better. Twenty-five rating systems say it is a coin flip, and slightly the wrong way. You need ten places of separation before a ranking tells you anything.
  5. #153Aug 28 — Tampa Bay Leads the AL East by Six. By Runs Scored, They Are Third. — The Rays have outscored opponents by 47 runs. Boston and New York are near +100. The first-place team is the third-best team in its division — and it will still win the thing.
  6. #154Aug 29 — Home Field Is Worth Thirteen Ranks. Most of It Is the Road Team Losing Them. — A ranked team at home wins 77% of the time. The same team on the road wins 48%. Split that gap three ways and the crowd turns out not to be doing the work.

What We Got Right

The Boston Call

On August 10, with the Red Sox at 62–51 and having just run off one of the better 28-game stretches in the AL all season, this newsletter put 94% on their playoff chances. That number came from 200,000 simulated season finishes using a 50/50 blend of actual and Pythagorean winning percentage, regressed toward .500 by 69 games.

Notice what that prior does. It does not simply project the hot stretch forward; it asks what 62–51 means for a team whose underlying quality, weighted by their full-season rate, sits at a given level. The answer was 94%. Not 85%, not 99%. A specific claim with a specific number.

Three weeks later, the same model, updated with 20 more games of data, gives 99.3%. The direction and the magnitude both held. That is what a calibrated prior does when new data align with the initial estimate. You should expect this. That is the whole point of writing the number down.

“A prediction that gives 94% and confirms at 99.3% is not a hedge. It is a model working as intended.”

— The Sports Page, Sunday Edition No. 021

What We Got Wrong (or Needs Noting)

The Factual Update Worth Naming

Issue #153, published Thursday, noted that New York had passed Boston in AL East run differential — +104 to +101 as of August 27. That is correct. But the piece also acknowledged it in a footnote: “Two days earlier this piece said Boston had the division’s best run differential. New York has since passed them.” The argument pivoted to the Tampa Bay gap (which has not moved) rather than the Boston-Yankees spread (which changes daily). That is not a miss. That is the right adjustment.

What is worth naming plainly: this newsletter has run six consecutive weeks without a scoreable miss. That should make you suspicious, not reassured. Either the editorial calendar has leaned toward structural analysis because structural analysis is easier to grade kindly, or the models are well-calibrated. Both deserve the same scrutiny; you should hold this newsletter to the same standard it applies to hot-streak stories.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

Issue #151 opened with “Kansas City Has Won Seven Straight. They Are a .435 Team.” The framing is deliberately abrupt. Hot streaks are genuinely misleading, and saying so plainly is the newsletter's job. But the piece could have noted that the Royals' 7-game run was real-time confirmation of the methodology — not evidence against the model, but evidence for it. A team at .435 will win seven in a row occasionally. That is what .435 teams do. The piece said what it needed to say.

Under-Reactions

Issue #154 on home-field advantage ran Friday, the same day as CFB Week 0. The newsletter found that home field is worth thirteen ranking positions and that most of it comes from road team underperformance; then it published without noting that the season had just started. The structural finding got no tether to the live season. Issue #150’s Notre Dame hype-floor analysis had the same problem: the Irish open September 6 at Lambeau, and those attention numbers are about to get a real test. You will be able to check them yourself.

What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

College football is here. Week 0 ran Saturday — North Carolina opened in Ireland, USC handled San Jose State 42–26, Memphis beat UNLV in Charles Huff’s debut. Notre Dame does not play until September 6 versus Wisconsin at Lambeau. That gives you a week to read Issue #150 on hype floors and Issue #154 on home-field splits before the Irish take the field in an away game masquerading as neutral.

On the MLB side, the Issue #153 simulations give Tampa Bay a 72.1% chance to win the AL East with about a month left. You will watch that resolve over the next four Sundays; the model says the race is effectively over, and the standings say it is six games. Both things can be true at once. Watch which one moves.

The queue has work to do. Payroll arms races, hope curves, dark-horse contenders. September compresses everything: baseball enters its final stretch, football finds its rhythm, and this newsletter will finally have predictions to grade rather than structures to explain. That is the part of the season worth staying for.
