Houston went 81–81, won the worst division title in 57 years, then got swept out of the Wild Card in two games. We called the first part last Sunday. The second part is not a forecasting failure — it is a new game with new evidence. Full accounting below.

The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. Six issues, one gradeable prediction: zero hit, one missed, zero still pending. Five pieces made no forward forecast at all — they were analysis, retrospective, or methods, and that is legitimate work. Saying so out loud is not an excuse; it is the accounting.

Here is the surprising part. Issue #185 said Cleveland was where players go to die. The adjusted data showed Cleveland is in the top third of career preservation. We were wrong in a direction that flattered a bad story, and the piece said so on the record. That is the miss we owe you.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story.

  4. #187Oct 1 — Two Fans Spent the Same $4,000. One of Them Has $87,000. — What happens to a $4.55-per-week losing edge when you run it 40 years and ask what the money was doing instead. A losing edge does not drain you to nothing; it finds a level and holds you there.
  5. #188Oct 2 — The Mets Got One Day of Hope. The Cubs Got 126. — Hope-days: the area under the playoff probability curve. The Cubs banked 126 probability-days of belief from April to September. What We Got Right (Carried Forward)

The AL West Prediction: From 70% to 100% to History

Last Sunday: our Issue #182 prediction — 70% probability that the AL West champion would finish at .500 or worse — became 100% after Saturday’s results. The full resolution arrived this week.

Houston won their final game 9–0 over the Oakland Athletics. Texas lost. The Astros finished 81–81, winning the division by virtue of the tiebreaker. It is the first .500 division title in 57 years of baseball, across 270 division winners. Our model said this was 70% likely entering the final weekend. It was.

Then Houston was swept by the Chicago White Sox in the Wild Card, 2-0, losing 6–3 and 7–3. That is not a forecasting failure. We made no prediction about the Wild Card. It is new data: an 81-win club played a 90-win club and lost. One series tells you about nothing. Whether Houston deserved to be there was not what Issue #182 asked.

What We Got Wrong

The Cleveland Call

The original claim, made in a conversation that became notes for this paper: “Cleveland is where players go to die. And they die a horrible death, because they get overused. And I’ll tell you, the stats are going to prove me right about this.”

The adjusted survival analysis across 1,848 player-moves from 2015–2022 put Cleveland in the top third. When you hold experience, prior role, and year fixed, players who go to Cleveland are more likely to still be playing two years later than the league average, not less. Arizona is the bottom of the list, not Cleveland.

The mechanism we assumed has a selection problem: losing clubs sign players other clubs have finished with. Run the naive comparison and you confirm the theory by construction. You never actually measured anything. The piece explained that trap. We walked into it anyway.

This is the piece to read if you want to understand how a prediction can feel airtight and be completely wrong.

“We said the stats would prove us right. They proved us wrong. Committing to a direction before looking is the only way to say that honestly.”

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

The hat benchmark in Issue #184 was the right test. But three weeks is a small sample: all three strategies landing left of center could still be noise. A hat at 224 over three weeks is evidence. Over a full season it becomes a verdict. We built the right frame. We have not run the experiment long enough to fill it.

Under-Reactions

Issue #188 established that the Mets banked exactly one hope-day this season, against Colorado’s two. That number — one probability-day of genuine playoff belief, total, from mid-April to late September — deserved a harder look at why. The piece correctly identified that the Mets were mathematically finished by late June. We did not chase the follow-up question: what did that look like from Opening Day?

What Saturday Showed

Week 5 of the college football season was quieter than its predecessor. Three top-25 teams lost Saturday, down sharply from eight the week before. Only one result qualified as a genuine upset.

Missouri, unranked, beat No. 8 Florida 45–17 in Gainesville. The Tigers put up 560 yards of offense on a team that entered the game as a two-score favorite. Florida had been 4–0 and climbing the rankings. Missouri had been getting there too, and on Saturday the distance between them dissolved inside one quarter.

Notre Dame stayed unbeaten, beating North Carolina 37–26 to move to 5–0. The Irish have won 15 straight, a run that now covers the full length of the current coaching staff’s tenure. Miami rolled past Clemson 41–13. Ohio State beat No. 15 Iowa 31–14; Jeremiah Smith had a 57-yard touchdown and set an Ohio State receiving record in the process.

The upset arithmetic I cannot give you. Each Sunday this edition is supposed to sum every underdog’s pre-game win probability and compare it against the actual upset count. A 1-in-13 shot is startling in its own game and near-inevitable across fifty. The pre-game rating snapshot for week 5 was not taken before Saturday’s kickoff. Without it, any arithmetic I print grades games on ratings that have already absorbed the results — which is how you convince yourself you saw everything coming. I will not do that. The lesson holds even when I cannot show the math.

What to take home

Three things worth keeping from this week. First: commit to a direction before you look. The Cleveland piece only exists because the prediction was stated in advance. A wrong call discovered after the fact is data; a wrong call documented before is accountability. You can use the first to update. You can use the second to learn.

Second: the hat is the baseline, not the ceiling. Before you call any strategy good, ask what random assignment would score. Issue #184 showed that three weeks of confidence-pool data produced a clever rule, a cleverer rule, and a hat — in that order, worst to best. Most winning ideas have not been compared to nothing in particular.

Third: a prediction confirmed is not a prediction about what happens next. Houston went 81–81, won the division, and lost in the Wild Card in two games. The model called the first event. The second event was a new question with new evidence. Knowing one thing does not carry you forward; it tells you the cost of the next bet.
