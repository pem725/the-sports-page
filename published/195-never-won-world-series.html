Somebody Had to Be Last. It Didn’t Have to Be Seattle.

Four clubs have never won a World Series, and one of them, Seattle, has never played in one. Pure luck would leave about three clubs without a title, so the list is ordinary. Seattle’s wait is not: in 50 seasons it has reached the playoffs 6 times, where an equal draw would send it 13.

Is it just bad luck that some clubs never win a World Series? On October 28 the Brewers won their first, and left a short list. Four clubs are still on it: the Mariners, the Padres, the Rays and the Rockies. The Mariners have never even played in one.

Here is the answer, with its number. Suppose you pulled a champion out of a hat every year since 1903, with every club getting the same chance. The hat would leave about three of today’s 30 clubs without a title. Four or more happens 23 times in 100. So the list itself is ordinary luck.

That is the surprise: luck explains the list, but not who is on it. Seattle has reached the playoffs 6 times in 50 seasons. The hat would have sent it 13 times. By the end, you will be able to tell a curse from a team that rarely got its chance.

Somebody Has to Be Last

Start with the part that really is luck, because you can check it yourself. With 30 clubs and one trophy a year, droughts are guaranteed. We ran the hat 100,000 times, using the real list of clubs in every season, new ones and all. On average 2.6 clubs never win. About half the time, at least one club never even wins a pennant. So the fact that somebody has never been to a World Series is a coin flip. There is nothing to explain.

Now notice who is on the list. You may already see it: all four are young. The Rays have played 29 seasons and the Rockies 34; the Padres have 58 and the Mariners 50. Each year the hat gives a club about 1 chance in 30, and young clubs have had few years. A club with the Rays’ history comes up empty about 37 times in 100.

It Didn’t Have to Be Seattle

Here is where our hat stops working. A fair hat sends a club with Seattle’s 50 seasons to at least one World Series 97 times in 100. Seattle has not gone, and the reason is not October. It is the six months before it.

The Mariners have won 3,765 games and lost 4,108. That is a .478 record over 7,873 games. Flip a coin that many times and you will almost never land that far below half; a gap this big comes up about once in 20,000 tries. You cannot blame that on luck. It is a team that has, for most of 50 years, been below average.

So it got few chances: six playoff trips in 50 seasons, when an equal draw gives 13. The hat leaves a club with six or fewer about 1 time in 80. Look at the chart. Seattle sits near the bottom with the Marlins, the Nationals and the Rockies.

Once Seattle got in, though, we would call it merely unlucky. It lost the American League Championship Series four times: 1995, 2000, 2001 and 2025. In 2001 it won 116 games, tied for the most ever, and lost to the Yankees in five. Add up the odds of its six trips, and a fair draw leaves Seattle without a pennant about 22 times in 100. That is bad luck, but ordinary bad luck. Compare the Marlins: four playoff trips, two titles. A fair draw does that about 1 time in 20.

The Trap in the List

Here is a number you will hear called a curse. The Padres, Rays and Rockies have played in six World Series between them and lost all six. Flip a coin six times and it lands tails every time only 1 time in 64.

Do not fall for it. We picked these clubs, and you read about them, because they never won. A club that had won one of those six would not be on the list. Choosing by the result and then marveling at the result is called selection on the outcome, and it is how most curses are born.

Luck decides that somebody waits. The regular season decides who.

Where Luck Runs Out

Now look at the other end, where you would expect luck to matter just as much. In a typical run of the hat, the luckiest club wins about 11 titles. The Yankees have 27. In 100,000 runs, the hat never gave any club that many. Droughts are what luck does. Dynasties are what money and skill do.

And the Mets, for the record, have won exactly what the hat would hand them: five pennants against 4.9, and two titles against 2.4.

What to Take Home

When you hear that a club is cursed, ask two questions. First: would a fair hat have left somebody this dry anyway? For the list of clubs without a title, it would. Second: is this club actually getting to October? That part is played over 162 games, and it is where most droughts are made. Seattle does not need a better October. It needs more of them.

For how many wins it takes to get in at all, see Issue #67, on Jerry Dipoto’s 54%. For a club that has never minded its drought, see Issue #47, on the Rockies.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Data: the Lahman Baseball Database, Teams table, American and National Leagues, 1903–2025. The 2026 season is from the MLB Stats API: final standings and postseason results (the Brewers beat the Rays 4–1, final game October 28). Seattle’s six postseason runs were checked series by series against MLB’s game feed. Code: scripts/ws_droughts.py; data in data/ws-droughts/.

The hat: each season, every club in a league has the same chance at its pennant (1 divided by the number of clubs in that league), and every club in the majors the same chance at the title. There was no World Series in 1904 or 1994. Clubs are counted from 1903 or their first season, so older pennants (the Dodgers’ and Giants’ 1890s flags, for instance) are left out. 100,000 simulated histories.

Once in: each actual playoff trip is treated as an equal draw among that league’s playoff clubs that year. Wild-card game losers from 2012 to 2021 count as trips, which flatters them a little.

What it cannot do: the hat is a yardstick, not a model of baseball. Real clubs are not equal, and the playoff format has changed several times. The coin comparison for Seattle’s record treats games as independent flips, which they are not quite; the gap is wide enough that this does not change the answer.
