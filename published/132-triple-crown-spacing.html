Racing Fixed the Triple Crown’s Two-Week Problem by Moving It In Front of the Longest Race.

From 2027 the Preakness runs three weeks after the Kentucky Derby instead of two — on a Sunday, a first in the history of the series. It is being sold as giving horses more room. The window from Derby to Belmont is unchanged at 35 days. All that moved is where the squeeze sits, and it now sits in front of a Belmont that is simultaneously getting 20% longer.

The change is real and it is confirmed. The 2027 Preakness runs on Sunday 23 May, twenty-two days after the 1 May Derby rather than the traditional fourteen. It is the first Triple Crown race ever run on a Sunday, and from 2028 through 2031 it locks to the Sunday of Memorial Day weekend.

The reason is not in dispute either. Trainers stopped bringing Derby horses back on two weeks’ rest. In 2026 the Derby winner, Golden Tempo, skipped the Preakness altogether — the third time in five years the Derby winner has done exactly that. A middle leg that the best horse keeps declining is a problem worth solving.

So they gave the horses another week. Except they did not.

The Arithmetic

Traditionally the Triple Crown runs Derby, then Preakness fourteen days later, then Belmont twenty-one days after that. Thirty-five days end to end.

In 2027: Derby 1 May, Preakness 23 May, Belmont 5 June. Twenty-two days, then thirteen. Thirty-five days end to end.

The window did not grow by a single day. The extra week in front of the Preakness was taken from the gap before the Belmont — which is now shorter than the two-week turnaround everybody was complaining about.

And the Belmont Is Getting Longer Again

Here is the part that compounds. The Belmont has been run at Saratoga since 2024 at 1¼ miles — ten furlongs — while Belmont Park was rebuilt. In 2027 it returns to Belmont Park and to its traditional 1½ miles. Twelve furlongs.

So in the same year, the final leg gets 20% longer and the rest before it drops from twenty-one days to thirteen.

Put those together as rest per furlong of the race a horse is about to run — a crude measure, but the direction is not subtle. Before the Belmont it falls from 2.10 days per furlong to 1.08. A 48% cut, arriving at the same moment the race returns to the distance that made it famous for stopping Triple Crown bids.

The complaint was that two weeks is not enough rest before a middle-distance race. The solution was thirteen days before the longest race in American racing.

What This Does to the Record Book

Thirteen horses have won the Triple Crown, from Sir Barton in 1919 to Justify in 2018. Every one of them did it on the fourteen-then-twenty-one schedule. That structure is not incidental to the achievement; for three-quarters of a century it was the achievement. The distances were always manageable individually. The compression is what made the sweep rare.

From 2027 a horse can win all three races. It will be a magnificent thing and it will be called a Triple Crown. It will not be the same test. Not obviously easier — thirteen days before twelve furlongs may prove harder than fourteen before nine and a half — but different, in a way that makes the comparison to Secretariat a comparison between two different events that share a name.

Nobody will say so. The record book has one column.

What To Take Home

This newsletter has now run into the same problem three times in a month. The College Football Playoff went from four teams to twelve, and every “one loss ends your season” number quietly expired. March Madness goes to 76, and every Cinderella rate goes with it. Now the Triple Crown’s defining constraint moves, and 106 years of records become records of something else.

In each case the sport changed the structure and kept the scoreboard. The numbers carried on being quoted, because numbers do not come with expiry dates printed on them.

So it is worth making the check a habit. When someone hands you a historical comparison, ask what the rules were when the history was made. If they changed, you are not looking at a longer record — you are looking at two shorter ones stacked in the same column and hoping nobody notices the seam.

How this was computed Dates are the announced 2027 schedule: Kentucky Derby 1 May, Preakness 23 May, Belmont 5 June, checked against the traditional pattern of Derby on the first Saturday in May, Preakness two weeks later, Belmont three weeks after that. Gaps are simple date arithmetic. Distances are 1¼ miles for the Derby (10 furlongs), 1⅓⅙ for the Preakness (9½), and 1½ for the Belmont (12) — the Belmont having been run at 1¼ miles at Saratoga from 2024 to 2026 during the Belmont Park rebuild, returning to 1½ in 2027. “Rest per furlong” is days since the previous race divided by the length of the next one; it is a rough index of recovery demand, not a physiological model, and no claim is made about how horses actually recover.
