Two of Every Three Top-25 Pairs Have Nothing in Common

Through Oct. 31, 199 of the 300 pairs of teams in the AP Top 25 had never played each other or a common opponent. Among the NFL’s 12 best records it was 9 pairs in 66, and by January it is none. Saturday’s Miami–Notre Dame game, the biggest of the month, will connect just two of the 300.

When a college football ranking says one team is better than another, how often have they actually met on the field? Saturday night, No. 1 Miami plays at Notre Dame. The AP voters have the Irish fourth. The playoff committee, looking at the same games, has them eleventh. Somebody is reading the evidence differently, or there is not much evidence to read.

Here is the answer, with its number. Take the 25 teams in the AP poll and make every pair: 300 of them. Through Oct. 31, 199 of those pairs had never played each other and had not played a single team in common. In the NFL’s top 12 it was 9 of 66.

You might expect the big games to fix that. They barely move it. By the end, you will be able to tell which parts of any ranking are results and which are opinions.

A Trial and an Observational Study

Our editor puts it this way. The NFL runs a randomized trial: a league office draws the schedule from a formula, every club plays 17 games, and division rivals meet twice. College football runs an observational study. There are more than 130 major-college teams, each plays 12 games, and the games were mostly picked years ago, by conference and by choice.

So we sorted every pair of top teams three ways. Did they play each other? If not, did they play a common opponent? If neither, any order you put them in is a belief. It is not a result.

Look at the red. Two-thirds of the college pairs are belief, now, with a month to go. Even after the bowls and the playoff, about 4 in 10 still are; 2023, 2024 and 2025 all land between 38% and 42%. The NFL ends every one of those seasons at zero. Every club has played every other club, or someone who did.

The Game That Settles One Pair

Miami comes in 8–0 and No. 1 in both rankings. Notre Dame comes in 7–1, fourth to the AP and eleventh to the committee. Notice what the Irish have to go on. Of the other 24 teams in the AP poll, they have played one: BYU, and they lost 28–24 in Provo on Oct. 17. Miami has played one too, Wake Forest.

Now the surprise. The two had already played two of the same teams, North Carolina and Stanford. So Saturday’s game will turn one “common opponent” pair into a head-to-head pair, and give Miami its first link to BYU. That is 2 pairs out of 300, whoever wins. Of the 199 pairs with nothing in common, 198 will still have nothing in common on Sunday.

This is their second meeting in two seasons. The first was last year’s opener in Miami Gardens. Here it is, using only the numbers that ESPN and CollegeFootballData agree on.

Read it twice. Notre Dame gained more yards per play and lost. Miami won the turnovers 2–0, held the ball eight minutes longer, and won by three at home. One game settles who won that night. It does not settle who is better. A three-point game swings on a fumble, and it does not tell you what the score would be on the other team’s field.

Saturday is that other field. Watch the turnovers and the clock, because that is where last year was decided. If Notre Dame wins, the two meetings split one apiece, each won by the home team. That settles who won each night. It does not settle who is better.

What to Take Home

When you see two teams ranked side by side, ask one question: what connects them? A game between them is evidence. A common opponent is weaker evidence. Beating North Carolina by 11 and by 40 are both “wins.” Nothing at all means the order is somebody’s belief, and two honest people can hold different ones. That is how the same team is fourth and eleventh in the same week.

The same check works on any ranking built from games that were never played: hospitals, schools, restaurants. Before you trust the order, count how many of the pairs were ever compared directly.

We counted Notre Dame’s ranked opponents before, in Who Did They Play? This is the other half: not how hard the schedule was, but how much of it connects.

How this was measured, and what it cannot do

Data: every completed game and the AP Top 25 from CollegeFootballData, read Nov. 4; the “now” poll is the last one before week 10, and games run through Oct. 31. NFL games from nflverse through week 8; the top 12 is by record, ties broken by point differential. End-of-season counts use the final AP poll and every game, bowls and playoffs included, and the NFL’s full season with playoffs.

The measure: each pair is “played,” “common opponent only” (any opponent, including lower-division teams), or “neither.” It counts links, not how strong each link is. A common opponent beaten by 3 and by 40 counts the same.

The 2025 box score: ESPN and CollegeFootballData agree on the score, passing yards, first downs, turnovers and time of possession. They disagree on Miami’s total yards (339 or 332), its rushing (134 or 127), and third downs; we print a range or leave the number out. Yards per play comes out about 5.5 for Notre Dame and about 5 for Miami on either feed. Code: scripts/settled_on_field.py, with --game for any box score on both feeds.
