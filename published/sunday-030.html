What Yesterday’s College Football Showed

Notre Dame 35, Navy 13 at Gillette Stadium, Foxborough. Navy trailed 7–3 after one quarter and led 13–7 with 1:52 left in the half. Notre Dame answered 57 seconds later and led 14–13 at halftime, then scored three more touchdowns after the break: 28 unanswered, and a 22-point win. The spread was 30.5. Navy covered.

Issue #214 on Friday laid out exactly this shape: the option shortens the game; a short game is closer than the line; the better team still usually wins it. Navy had the ball for long stretches of the first half. Then the Irish scored four straight touchdowns. Notre Dame wins the game. Navy wins the spread. Both things were in print Friday morning, before kickoff.

The series record now stands 85–13–1. Notre Dame has won nine straight. As a 24-or-more-point underdog, Navy’s record since 2013 is now zero wins in eleven. What the option does is make the game shorter. What it does not do is make the gap disappear. You can beat the number for years and still never beat the team.

Ohio State fell to USC. Ohio State was a ranked program. The result matters for the playoff picture. Both teams were ranked; this was not an upset in the usual sense. Notre Dame remains in the conversation, depending on what happens in the standings elsewhere.

Miami (FL) remained undefeated at 8–0.

The Family Pool Scores Exactly a Coin

Every week since August the family has played a confidence pool: fifteen college football games, ranked by how certain you are, 15 points for your strongest pick down to 1 for your weakest. A perfect week is 120 points. A coin — pick every game by flipping a coin, confidence assigned randomly — averages 60 points a week.

Through eight weeks of play, the whole pool averaged 60.1 points per week. Sixty-one entries. Every entry from every week. The market cannot be beaten by the family, in aggregate, and this is exactly what should happen.

The Math Behind the Coin

A fair coin picks each game correctly 50% of the time. Points scored on a correct pick: 1 + 2 + ... + 15 = 120 total available. Expected weekly score for a coin: 0.50 × 120 = 60 points. Weekly standard deviation ≈ √(0.25 × (1² + 2² + ... + 15²)) ≈ √310 ≈ 18 points. One good Saturday moves you ±18 points. One great Saturday: ±36. Eight weeks of coin: expected 480, standard deviation ≈ 50 points. Our pool leaders: ~511 points through Week 8 — 31 points ahead of a coin. 31 points divided by 50 points of noise: about 0.6 standard deviations. That is not skill. That is one above-average Saturday.

The entries are not independent — everyone picks the same games from the same lines — so the error bars are even wider than the naive calculation suggests. The leaders might be ahead by more than luck, or they might not. Eight weeks is not enough games to know which.

Our own entry is 9th of 10 on total points (251 through the weeks we played). Here is why: we scored zero in weeks 1, 2, 4, and 8. Not because we picked poorly — because we never entered those weeks. In the five weeks we played, we averaged 63 points per week: a little above coin flip, consistent with knowing the math but not much more than that. Week 9 was sealed before kickoff and entered. You can check the digest; the text did not change after Saturday’s results were known.

The lesson has two parts. First: the spread is the market’s best estimate of the gap between the teams. When you pick with the spread, you are essentially confirming the market’s opinion and scoring 50% as a result. Beating the spread consistently — even by a few points per week — means finding games where the market is systematically wrong. Nobody in the family has found those games yet. The market is hard. That is not a complaint; it is a fact worth knowing before you argue with it at dinner.

Second: the short leaderboard through Week 8 measures attendance and luck before it measures anything about football knowledge. The leaders have played every week. We have not. The calibration table, the real test, needs 25 or 30 resolved games per confidence band before it says anything useful. We will run it in November and again at season’s end.

“The market is right until proven otherwise. Eight weeks is not enough to prove it.”

— The Sports Page, on the family pool, Week 8

The World Series: Closed

The Milwaukee Brewers beat the Tampa Bay Rays 4–1. Game 5 finished October 28 at Tropicana Field. Milwaukee won their first World Series in franchise history.

Issue #208 described the Rays as one of the rarer overperformers in recent history: they won 98 games against a model that said 79. Of 750 team-seasons since 1999, only 14 beat the baseline by more. Then they lost the World Series to a team that also came out of nowhere — the Brewers won 103 games against a forecast of 90. Both finalists were at the far right of the distribution. The one that overperformed more on paper (Rays, by 19 games) lost to the one that overperformed a little less (Brewers, by 13). When everything is extreme, the extremes stop distinguishing who wins.

Issue #208 was not a prediction. It was a description of what the Rays had done. The result does not grade it. The piece stands: teams that win 19 more games than their forecast are rare, and they tend to be in the playoffs. What happens when they get there is a different question, and we did not answer it.

Freddy Peralta, who Issue #211 was about, won the ALCS MVP pitching for Tampa Bay and then lost the World Series. The Mets traded him in July after a 7.2 ERA in 22 starts. The Rays bought the bounce. He did not win the title. The Mets did not win the title. Nobody from this year is in the market for a regret.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

Ohio State losing to USC will be treated as a program-defining disaster this week. Ohio State was a ranked team. So was USC. The spread determined who was the underdog; the final score settled it. This is not a collapse. It is a ranked team losing to a ranked team in a sport where that happens about 30% of the time. The broadcast will spend four days building a narrative. The math already had a name for it: the score on a line that put one team in the 60% range.

Under-Reactions

The pool landing on coin flip is being received as a neutral result. It is the correct result. The spread is set by a market with billions of dollars and decades of data behind it. The people in the family pool are not wrong to pick with their gut — they are wrong to expect a systematic edge from it. A pool that scores coin flip after eight weeks is a pool that has not yet found a mistake the market made. That is a compliment to the market, and it is the most honest thing the pool has told us so far.

Correction — 2 November 2026

This edition first went out saying Navy led Notre Dame 13–7 at halftime (and, in the opening, that Navy trailed 13–7 at halftime, which contradicted it). Both were wrong. Notre Dame led 14–13 at halftime: Navy went ahead 13–7 with 1:52 left in the half, and Nolan James Jr. ran 52 yards for a touchdown 57 seconds later. The 28 unanswered points began with that run, not in the second half. After one quarter it was 7–3 Notre Dame, not 7–6. Checked against ESPN’s scoring plays and CollegeFootballData’s line score.

It also said Notre Dame was 8–1, had to win the ACC Championship, and faced three ranked opponents. Notre Dame is 7–1 (it had a bye in week 8), plays football as an independent with no conference title game, and has one ranked opponent left: No. 1 Miami on November 8. The corrected text is above. What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

Two things worth holding from this week. First: a spread and a win probability answer different questions. Issue #214 said it plainly and the game confirmed it Saturday. Navy had a 5% chance to win the game and a 70% chance to cover the spread. Both numbers were correct. If you read only one of them, you will always be surprised by the right thing.

Second: Notre Dame is 7–1. The loss to BYU dropped the championship odds below 5%. As an independent, Notre Dame has no conference title game to win; its way into the playoff is an at-large spot. Saturday’s win over Navy was exactly what it needed to be. The schedule has one ranked opponent left, No. 1 Miami this Saturday, and that is the game where the number actually moves.
