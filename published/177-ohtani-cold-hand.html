A Coin That Hits Like Ohtani Slumps More Than He Does.

Shohei Ohtani went 3 for 20 in his first six playoff games, with no home runs. Pure chance makes a hitter with his exact talent go that cold about four times a season. The real Ohtani does it about once. Streaks are not something extra on top of chance. They are what chance looks like up close — unless something hurts.

Is Shohei Ohtani in a slump, or is this just baseball? Here is the answer, with its number. His first six playoff games: 24 trips to the plate, 3 hits, 7 strikeouts, no homers. A hitter with his talent, if every at-bat were a fair coin, would have a stretch that cold about four times a year. The real Ohtani has had about one a year since 2021.

That should surprise you. Most people think a slump is something added to chance — a cold hand, a lost swing, nerves. The numbers say the opposite. Chance makes more slumps than he does. By the end of this you will have a two-question test for any slump you hear about on the broadcast.

The Hot Hand and the Cold Hand Are One Argument

You have heard of the hot hand: the shooter who cannot miss. In 1985, Thomas Gilovich and two colleagues looked at basketball shots and found streaks no longer than a coin would make. Fans were furious. In 2018, Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo found a flaw in the way streaks had been counted. Fixed, it left a small hot hand — real, but far smaller than it feels from the couch.

Now notice something. Every argument about the hot hand is also an argument about the cold one. If a hot streak is mostly chance, so is a cold one. You do not get to believe in one and not the other.

So we ran the test on Ohtani himself. We took every game he played from 2021 through this September, nearly 4,000 plate appearances. Then we built a fake Ohtani. Same walk rate, same homer rate, same everything — but each at-bat is a fresh roll of the dice, with no memory. We played his real schedule 400 times over and counted how often he went this cold for six games.

Look at the left edge of the chart. The coin goes this cold in about 1 stretch out of 40. The real Ohtani does it in about 1 out of 130. Over a season of roughly 150 stretches, that is four slumps for the coin and one for the man. The middle of the two shapes is nearly identical. A dice-rolling Ohtani is streakier than the real one.

If a hot streak is mostly chance, so is a cold one. You do not get to believe in one and not the other.

But First, Check for a Sore Arm

Here is the hard part, and it should bother you. Chance is the default, not the verdict. On September 11 the Dodgers put Ohtani on the injured list with right biceps inflammation, back-dated to September 8. He came back on the 23rd. He has not hit like himself since.

We have seen this movie. In May, Cal Raleigh went 38 at-bats without a hit, then landed on the injured list with a sore oblique two days after it ended. Issue #63 argued the injury probably started the slump. A slump that is ordinary in size can still have a cause. The math tells you how often chance alone would do this. It cannot tell you whether his arm hurts.

Is He Clutch? The Data Cannot Say Yet

Some players seem to rise when it matters. Maybe a few do. But look at what it takes to show it. Ohtani has 184 career playoff plate appearances. His playoff OPS is about .87; in the regular season since 2021 it is about .98. That looks like a drop. It is not one you can trust. With 184 trips, the honest margin is about ±.12 either way. The gap fits inside it.

That is the sample-size problemi in one line. Missouri fans have it right: show me. Playoff samples are too small to show you anyone’s clutch gene. A league-wide look at who keeps rising, year after year, is coming soon.

Does a Slumping Ohtani Mean the Dodgers Lose?

Ask the Braves. Ohtani went 2 for 13 in the Division Series. The Dodgers won it three games to one. In the first two games against Milwaukee he went 1 for 7, and Los Angeles lost both. One hitter gets about one plate appearance in nine. He matters a great deal; he is not the whole lineup.

What to Take Home

When you hear that a player is slumping, ask two questions. First: how often would chance alone do this? For a great hitter over six games, the answer is “a few times a year.” Second: did anything change? An injury, a new pitch, a new role. If the answer to the second is no, the slump is weather. If the answer is yes, it may be climate.

Hold onto the first question longest. It works on hot streaks, on stocks, on a good month at work. Most streaks are the dice talking.

How we did it. Game logs from the MLB Stats API, regular season 2021–2026 (3,980 plate appearances). Six-game windows, overlapping. The coin-flip hitter draws each plate appearance on its own from Ohtani’s rates of singles, doubles, triples, homers, walks and outs. It plays his real game-by-game schedule, 400 times. OPS here leaves out sacrifice flies in the window figures, which shifts both shapes equally. Playoff pitching is tougher than regular-season pitching, so a cold October is a little less surprising than these numbers say.
