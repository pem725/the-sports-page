Six Issues, One Live Prediction, and a Deadline That Changed Everything.

This week the newsletter published five structural analyses and one live baseball probability. The deadline passed two weeks ago while the newsletter was writing about base rates and variance. The math held. The Red Sox acquired Adley Rutschman. The Mets stayed where they were. Accountability follows.
By The Sports Page · August 16, 2026 · Sunday Edition No. 019
Welcome to Sunday Edition No. 019. This week was a structural week — tournament design, Triple Crown spacing, payroll correlations, the mathematics of heartbreak. Only one issue made a live probabilistic forecast with a scoreable near-term outcome: the Boston Red Sox’s 94% chance of reaching October. That prediction gets a full update below. The rest of the week was analytical work that will be graded on longer timelines: March Madness 2027, the 2027 Triple Crown, the next three centuries of 12-over-5 upsets. The Week in Review: Issues #135–140

  1. #135Aug 10 — 109 Teams Have Been Exactly Where Boston Was on July 3. One Made the Playoffs. — The 25–3 run is a one-in-seventy-thousand event. Base rates, survivorship bias, and why the story we’re about to be sold about the Red Sox cannot teach what it claims to teach.
  2. #136Aug 11 — The Deepest Wound in Sports Is Always the Same Shape. — The 1993 Notre Dame field goal, the 2022 overtime coin toss, the 2011 World Series with two strikes twice. Three different sports. One wound. The math says why it always lands at the summit.
  3. #137Aug 12 — The NCAA Is Expanding March Madness to 76. The Arithmetic Says Go to 365. — Open the field to every Division I programme, run double elimination, and you get ten times the elimination games on 59% fewer total games — and a more reliable champion, not a worse one.
  4. #138Aug 13 — Racing Fixed the Triple Crown’s Two-Week Problem by Moving It In Front of the Longest Race. — The 2027 Preakness gets an extra week of rest. The Belmont gets 13 days, down from 21 — and gets 20% longer at the same time. The window is identical. The squeeze moved.
  5. #139Aug 14 — Every 12-Over-5 Number You Have Ever Heard Was Measured on a Bracket That Stops Existing in 2027. — Expansion pushes the 12-over-5 upset rate from ~33% to ~38%. Detecting that shift empirically requires roughly 300 years of March Madness. Nobody watching will ever know if we’re right.
  6. #140Aug 15 — The Marlins Spend $299 Million Less Than the Mets. They’re 22 Games Ahead. — Four seasons, 120 team-years, r ≈ 0.39. Payroll explains 15% of winning. The other 85% is what a check cannot buy.

What We Got Right

The Wins

The base-rate framing in Issue #135 is holding. Boston is still in a Wild Card slot. The 94% model was not predicting a specific game-by-game path; it was estimating the probability of an October appearance from that record. Six days later the record has wobbled (2–5 since the issue), but the destination probability has not materially changed. When the model says 94%, losing a short stretch while holding a playoff position is the expected experience, not a refutation of it.

The trade deadline update strengthens the case. Boston acquired Adley Rutschman — a genuine defensive upgrade at catcher and a more than passable bat. A team that was already 94% to reach October just improved its roster. If anything, the posterior probability is slightly higher today than it was when the model ran.

The payroll analysis from Issue #140 continues to describe the world accurately. The Mets are 53–69 on a $381M payroll. The core correlation figure (r ≈ 0.39, explaining ~15% of winning) is a structural estimate over four seasons and is not disturbed by any single month of play.

What We Got Wrong (or Aren’t Sure About)

Factual Updates and Honest Gaps

The newsletter published nothing about the trade deadline. The August 3 deadline was the most consequential day of the MLB mid-season — Dodgers acquired Tarik Skubal (two-time Cy Young winner), Red Sox got Rutschman, Cubs added Clay Holmes, Phillies got Luis Arraez. The newsletter was writing about tournament formats and base rates while the actual current roster-building moment of the season went unexamined. That is not a forecasting error; it is an editorial gap. The queue contains plenty of structural analysis. Timeliness on live deadline moves was sacrificed.

The Marlins–Mets payroll gap has narrowed. The article cited a 22-game lead for Miami as of July 18. Current available data suggests the lead has shrunk to roughly 8–9 games — the Mets have gone 12–12 over the past month while the Marlins appear to have gone about 10–15. Neither team is in playoff contention. The article made no prediction about the size of the gap in August; the narrowing is new data, not a miss. But it is worth noting that the “loudest example of payroll mismatch” has become less loud as the season progresses. Regression to the mean is doing what regression to the mean does.

Issue #135 claimed a one-in-seventy-thousand probability for Boston’s 25–3 run. That probability was not a prediction; it was a description of what had already happened. The run is now over — Boston went 2–5 in the six days since the issue published. This is not a miss. The article explicitly warned against drawing lessons from the exceptional case. The exceptional run ended at the moment the article described it. Nothing about that sequence violates the model.

The 94% was not a promise. It was a probability. Six games later, they’re still in. That is what 94% looks like from the inside — uncertain, then still in.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

None this week in the strict sense. Every piece was careful about the scope of its claims. The March Madness piece said the 365-team proposal was a thought experiment, not an advocacy position. The Triple Crown piece measured days and furlongs, not horse outcomes. The base-rate piece on Boston explicitly told you not to draw a lesson from the survivor. None of these overclaimed.

The editorial gap on the trade deadline is probably the closest thing. Publishing a piece on payroll mechanics (Issue #140) on August 15 — twelve days after the deadline shifted rosters across the league — was structurally fine but editorially late. A timely deadline piece would have used live data from August 3, not July 18.

Under-Reactions

The Dodgers acquiring Tarik Skubal deserved its own issue. Moving a two-time Cy Young winner from a contender (Detroit) to a better-resourced contender (Los Angeles) is exactly the kind of transaction that deserves Bayesian analysis: how much does a true-talent ace shift a playoff series win probability? That piece is not in the queue. It should be.

The Boston streak cooling (2–5 since Issue #135) is not a crisis, but it is a signal worth tracking. A team that was 94% to reach October on a historic 25–3 run is now navigating the regression the model always implied was coming. The 94% was calculated from a posterior that said their true talent was roughly .460–.540. A 2–5 stretch is completely inside that range. We are watching the model in real time.

The Road Ahead

The queue has a dense stretch of betting and money pieces: the origins of the point spread, how a sports bet became a liquid security on Kalshi, and the Notre Dame endowment myth. These are analytical pieces about structure — how money flows through sports, how probability estimates become prices. They sit well in August, when baseball is in its long final turn and football is three weeks from Week 1.

After those, a cluster of college football money pieces that will carry the newsletter into CFB season. The two-sport calendar — MLB pennant races and CFB opening weeks — is arriving on schedule. Expect more live predictions once the rosters are set and the games are real.

The Boston story resolves in late September. The model says 94%. The Red Sox say they’re 64–56 and have Rutschman now. We’ll all find out together.

One hundred forty issues. Three hundred sixty to go. The math keeps running whether we watch it or not.
