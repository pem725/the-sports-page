The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. Six issues, zero gradeable predictions from this week: zero hit, zero missed, zero new pending. All six pieces made no forward forecast — methods, history, and analysis rather than bets. That is worth saying out loud rather than quietly skipping, because it changes what accountability looks like here.

Here is the surprising part. There is no scoreable miss this week — and that is not a boast, it is a symptom. A purely analytical week is a safer week. The hardest thing to do is to make a bet and have it fail publicly. We did not do that. Next week has a piece where we admit our own backtesting model was completely overfit. That one will sting in the way this week did not.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story. You deserve to know which stories held up.

What We Got Right

The Real-Time Validation Nobody Ordered

On Friday we published a piece saying that Detroit’s one-run record — 67–79 overall, but Pythagorean expectation of around 84–62 based on a +63 run differential — is not a sign of a team with a genuine close-game problem. Year-to-year correlation of one-run records across the whole league is near zero.

Then, over the four-game series that ended Saturday, the Tigers lost three games by one run and won two by larger margins. Not proof. The sample is tiny. But it is the exact shape of the pattern the piece described: a team that runs up the score when it wins and loses the coin-flip games with roughly the frequency a coin flip would predict.

You cannot plan for validation that comes the next day. When it happens, you note it and keep going. The real check is whether the 2027 Tigers carry this year’s one-run record forward into next season. They won’t. That is the prediction.

Week 1 Signal, Two Weeks Later

Issue #165 found that a single Week 1 result adds seven percentage points of explanatory power to preseason ratings (from 14% to 21%). Notre Dame went from No. 4 to No. 3 after a dominant Week 1 win over Wisconsin at Lambeau Field, then beat Rice 52–0 in Week 2. Oregon, which drew suspicion after a close Week 1, fell to Oklahoma State in Week 2.

This is not a vindication of the model — it is two data points. But neither contradicts the finding, and the finding was that Week 1 is more informative than anyone expected going in. Hold it loosely. December is when you grade it.

What We Got Wrong (or Did Not Say)

The Mets, in Plain Terms

The Mets are 64–78 as of this morning. They are not close to a wild card spot. The two-trade retrospective in Issue #166 was about the historical cost of trading Ryan and Crow-Armstrong — not a forecast for 2026. But we have been covering this team all year, and the honest thing to say is that we never made a formal record prediction early in the season, so there is nothing to grade.

What we can say: they are exactly as disappointing as Pythagorean expectation would have let you expect given their performance. The surprises were not hidden in the math. They were hidden in the lineup.

The Babe Ruth piece does not become wrong because the 2026 Mets are 64–78. The trades it describes were wrong the day they were made, on any accounting that looked past the current roster.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

Notre Dame beat Rice 52–0 on Saturday night. Notice how fast the takes arrived. Rice. Notre Dame is No. 3 in the country and Rice is one of the worst teams in a rebuilt conference. The Week 1 signal piece said openers are informative because they pit competitive opponents against each other. A Week 2 home blowout against a 110th-ranked opponent is not an informative game. Hold the coronation.

The same caution applies to South Carolina at 2–0. Both wins were over teams from the lower half of the FCS and Group of Five landscape. The dark-horse prediction from Issue #143 is about December, not about shutting out Kent State in August.

Under-Reactions

Oregon fell to Oklahoma State in Week 2 — and most of the discourse treated it as a fluke. Look at the Week 1 issue again. The whole point of that measurement was that the hardest-to-predict opening week in three years suggests something about the calibration of preseason ratings, not just individual game variance. When a highly ranked team loses in Week 2, it is worth asking whether Week 1 was already telling you something.

The MLB playoff picture deserves more attention than the pennant-race coverage it is getting. The Brewers are 92–56 and appear to be running away with the NL Central. That is one of the better records in baseball. You have probably read three articles about the Braves this week and none about Milwaukee.

What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

The week ahead opens with something we are genuinely curious about. The returning-production piece asks whether Penn State returning 7.6% of its offense while winning 45–0, and Clemson returning 41% while losing by 41, is a signal or a coincidence. We ran eleven seasons of data. The answer is “mostly coincidence,” and it is more interesting than the alternative would have been.

Later in the week: log-odds. You have seen the home-field advantage figure, the single constant of +0.14. That constant is the whole reason the log is there — percentages cannot hold a constant and have it mean the same thing across the full range of team quality. This is one of those methods pieces that sounds dry and changes how you read a probability for the rest of your life.

And then — the one to read twice — No Edge. A perfect backtest is a bug report. Rebuilt so the method could not see the answers it was trying to predict, it beat the spread 51.0% of the time across 2,140 games. The 95% confidence interval contains 50. We will say in the headline that we were wrong, and we mean it.

CFB Week 3 starts Saturday. Notre Dame hosts a real opponent for the first time. South Carolina opens conference play. The dark-horse prediction starts generating real evidence. Watch for it.
