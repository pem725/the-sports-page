The question we ask every Sunday: what did we claim this week, and did reality agree?

The scoreboard. Six issues this week, 1 gradeable prediction: 0 hit, 1 missed, 2 still pending from prior weeks. Five pieces made no forward forecast at all, which is worth saying out loud rather than quietly skipping.

Here is the miss. Malachi Toney had ankle surgery and did not play Saturday night. We said two in three questionable players play. This one did not — and by the time he was ruled out for surgery, our own numbers (44% play rate for players who miss their last practice) pointed the other way. The headline claim led the other direction. That cost us nothing in money, but it was wrong.

Why we bother: a prediction nobody grades is just a story.

What Yesterday’s College Football Showed

Notre Dame beat No. 1 Miami, 31–24. Both teams entered 8–0. It was Miami’s first loss of the season.

The SP+ model, using ratings frozen before kickoff on November 5, had Notre Dame as a 3.0-point home favorite — a 57.2% win probability. The market opened Notre Dame at −7½ and settled at −7: a 66.4% win probability. Notre Dame won by 7. If the market’s number was exactly right, it matched the final margin to the point.

The Numbers, From the Frozen Snapshot

Notre Dame SP+: 25.2 Miami SP+: 24.7 Home-field edge: +2.5 (standard) Edge (ND home): 3.0 points P(ND win) per SP+: 57.2% P(ND win) per market: 66.4% (line: ND -7) Result: Notre Dame 31, Miami 24 (margin: +7)

The gap between the two models is 9 percentage points. SP+ rates the teams on 2026 play; the market weights recent form, home-field familiarity, and factors SP+ ignores. One of them was closer to right. With a sample of one game, you cannot tell which.

Also Saturday: No. 3 Utah over No. 9 BYU in the Holy War. SP+ gave Utah a 59.1% edge (3.8-point margin for a home team rated 18.0 to BYU’s 16.7). The market had Utah at 61.3% (−4.75). Utah won. The favored team won both games the model tracked — which is the expected result, not a vindication.

The Improbability Lesson

The broadcast will describe Miami’s loss as a shock. You should ask: a shock compared to what?

Miami entered the game at 8–0. To go undefeated through a 12-game regular season at 65% per game — a generous estimate for the best team in the country — the probability is 0.6512, which is about 1.2%. A perfect regular season is not a reasonable expectation; it is a near-impossibility. The long way to say the same thing: at 65% per game, a team will drop at least one game before the bowl in roughly 99 of every 100 seasons. Miami’s unbeaten run was not a prediction. It was a streak in progress, waiting for the game where the 35% would arrive.

The lesson is not about Notre Dame. It is about how we describe the inevitable. Every 1-in-13 event — last week’s biggest upset — feels surprising in the game it happens and near-certain in retrospect. Miami losing to any opponent at some point in this season was near-certain. We just did not know which game.

What Saturday told us that we did not already know: Notre Dame’s offensive line held a pressure-heavy defense for four quarters, the Irish secondary forced one turnover at the right time, and the committee now has a head-to-head result between its No. 4 and No. 1 teams going in. The committee weighs head-to-head results. That matters.

What We Got Right

The Wins

The back-to-the-wall piece (Issue #219) held up against the World Series, which finished before this edition. Teams facing elimination in a best-of-seven won 46% of those games, and teams that survived won the next game exactly half the time. The 2026 Rays followed the pattern: they won two elimination games and lost the World Series. No lift from the escapes, no extra momentum from surviving. A coin, as the piece described.

The QB wins structural argument (Issue #217) is a long-horizon claim, not a single-week prediction. This NFL season has not produced obvious exceptions: the teams allowing 28 or more points per game are losing at about the rates the 25-year historical data says they should. We will revisit this at season’s end.

What We Got Wrong

The Miss

Toney. The piece was structurally correct — two in three questionable players play is an accurate historical rate. What the piece did not do clearly enough was separate “questionable” from “questionable with surgery pending.” The piece stated that players who missed their final practice of the week played only 44% of the time. If Toney had missed that practice — which the reporting suggested — the piece’s own numbers pointed toward MISS, not HIT. The headline pointed the other way. That is a framing failure, not a modeling failure.

What it cost: nothing in this edition’s credibility except the scorecard entry. What it teaches: when a piece establishes conditional probabilities (67% overall; 44% for non-practitioners), lead with the conditional that applies to the case at hand, not the unconditional average.

“Two in three means one in three does not. We were the one in three this week. That is not a surprise. It is the data working correctly.”

— The Sports Page, on the miss

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

The broadcast will spend the next week treating Notre Dame’s win as proof the Irish are a championship contender. It may be true. It is not proven by one game. Notre Dame’s SP+ rating (25.2) and Miami’s (24.7) were essentially identical before kickoff. When two teams are that close, one of them had to win. The result tells the committee something about head-to-head; it does not tell us which program is better by the margin the final score suggests.

Under-Reactions

The blockbuster trades issue (Issue #221) may be under-read heading into Tuesday’s deadline. The Bears traded two first-round picks for Jeffery Simmons on October 29. The piece showed that trade-for-pick deals lose more than they win, and that one good game (“one game is not data”) says nothing about the full three-year window the data measures. Every team making a move Tuesday faces the same math. Read it before you decide how to feel about whatever your team does.

What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

Take home this: the questionable-player baseline (two in three play) is accurate as a population rate. When a specific player’s status changes from questionable to “ruled out for surgery,” that is a different question. The piece established the right conditional probability; the edition should have led with the conditional that applied.
