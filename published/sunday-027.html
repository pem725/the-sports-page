The Improbable Thing That Happened Yesterday

Oklahoma beat No. 1 Texas 27–24 in Dallas on a walk-off field goal. The Sooners entered as a 6.5-to-8.5 point underdog, depending on which line you read. The market-implied win probability for Texas ran between 65% and 70%. Oklahoma’s chance was somewhere between 30% and 35%.

The broadcast is calling it a shock. It is not a shock. A 35% event happens roughly one time in three. If you walked up to a coin that came up heads 65% of the time and it came up tails, you would not say the coin is broken. You would say: yes, that happens.

Here is the number that actually matters. There were roughly 90 rated college football games on the card yesterday. Add up every underdog’s chance of winning its own game and you get the number of upsets a normal Saturday produces. Yesterday’s card carried three top-ten teams who lost — Texas, Oregon (to UCLA, as underdogs around 25-30%), and LSU (to Kentucky in overtime, similarly priced). None of that is improbable if you think about the card as a whole instead of one game at a time.

The surprise was never that a top-ten team lost. The surprise is only ever which one.

The arithmetic I still cannot give you. Each Sunday this section is supposed to sum every underdog’s pre-game win probability, from our frozen ratings, and compare it against the actual upset count. That requires a snapshot taken before kickoff on Thursday; the Week 6 snapshot was not taken. Printing the arithmetic on ratings that have already absorbed Saturday’s results would be how you convince yourself you saw everything coming. I will not do that. The lesson holds even without the math. But the math would have shown you how ordinary yesterday was — and that is the part worth seeing. We are working to fix the snapshot cadence before next week.

Other results worth noting. No. 2 Georgia held off No. 6 Alabama 20–17. That is not an upset — the second-ranked team beat the sixth-ranked team. Indiana beat Nebraska 20–17. Notre Dame continues its winning streak; the Irish are now 6–0. The CFB Playoff picture tightens at the top every week that Georgia and Notre Dame both win, because they are on a collision course.

What to Watch Today — and Why Those Games

The NFL card today ranks by nearness to a coin flip, not by marquee matchup. The spread is the market’s best estimate of the gap between these clubs. A game at pick’em or −1.5 is two clubs the market genuinely cannot separate. Those are the games where a single play — a missed assignment, a lucky bounce, a Gene-level field goal — carries the most information about what these teams actually are.

Over-Reactions and Under-Reactions

Over-Reactions

The broadcast coverage of Oklahoma–Texas will spend the day building mythology. Oklahoma willed it. Texas “couldn’t handle the moment.” The walk-off field goal was destiny. None of that is wrong, exactly, but it is wrong about the category. A 35% shot coming in is not destiny. It is a sample size of one, drawn from a distribution. Three times in ten, Oklahoma wins that game. Two times in ten, they win it by more. Believing otherwise is the lesson Issue #195 was written to address, and it ran on Thursday.

Under-Reactions

Issue #192 established that the defense-versus-offense volatility gap disappears when you use the right measurement. The finding is not subtle — the interval crosses zero, which means we cannot claim defense is more improvable than offense once we control for scale. That is a direct challenge to one of the most common rebuild philosophies in football. Nobody is talking about it, because the right measurement produces a boring answer and the wrong one produces a compelling story.

“A 35% shot coming in is not destiny. It is a sample size of one. The surprise is only ever which game.”

What to Take Home, and the Road Ahead

The week taught two things worth holding onto. First: the right measurement matters more than the interesting one. Issues #192 and #193 both found that the first pass at a question — defense outpaces offense in improvement; new coaches struggle because of the roster — dissolves once you control for the obvious confounders. The boring answer is the honest one.

Second: a probability statement is not a guarantee, in either direction. Texas was 65% likely to win. Oklahoma won. The statement was still correct. Updating your belief after a single game — in either direction — is the main statistical error this paper exists to slow down.
