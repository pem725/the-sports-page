Another Usual Saturday in College Football. Join the Fun.

The headlines called it chaos: No. 4 UCLA, No. 8 Indiana and No. 14 Ohio State all lost. But a normal Saturday of 66 games holds about 16 surprises, and yesterday held 16. Nothing improbable happened. What caught everyone was who lost. Look at how those games were played, and you will say: yeah, I guess so.

The question: was yesterday really chaos, or did it only sound like chaos?

The answer: it was a usual day. Our ratings, frozen on Thursday before a ball was kicked, said the 66 games we rate should hold about 16 surprises: games won by the team we expected to lose. There were 16.

Why that is surprising: you heard the opposite all night. The surprise was never how many. It was which ones, and that is a different thing.

What you get: one question to ask the next time a broadcast says “nobody saw this coming.” How many surprises does a normal day hold?

The scoreboard. 6 issues, 2 gradeable predictions: 1 hit, 1 missed, 4 still pending. 4 pieces made no forward forecast at all, which is worth saying out loud rather than quietly skipping.

Here is the surprising part. Our own ratings fell for UCLA too. They gave UCLA 76 chances in 100 at Michigan. UCLA lost 18–16 in triple overtime. That is our week’s miss, and what it cost was a little pride. The interesting bit is below: by the measure we trust most, UCLA played like the better team and lost anyway.

What the headlines said

“Chaos takes over the Big Ten,” ran one headline overnight (TSN). Another called it “chaos in top-10” (Yardbarker). Three ranked Big Ten teams lost on the road to unranked teams. Every one of those facts is true. The word is the problem.

Chaos means something you could not have expected. So let us do the expecting.

What a normal Saturday looks like

Take each game on the card. Our ratings give the weaker team some chance: 1 in 4, 1 in 10, 1 in 20. Now add those chances up across all 66 games. That sum is the number of surprises a normal Saturday should hold. Yesterday it came to about 16.

Notice what that means. Sixteen times yesterday, the team you expected to lose was going to win, and no one could say in advance which sixteen. Even the count wobbles. Some Saturdays hold 10, some hold 23, and both are normal. Here is every Saturday this fall where we froze our ratings first:

Eight Saturdays, eight inside the normal range. Week 3 sat right at the low edge, a quiet day. Not one Saturday this season was chaos. Every one was college football.

Not how many. Who.

So why did it feel wild? Because three of the sixteen surprises happened to famous teams, on the same afternoon, in the same conference. That is the part you notice. It is also the part that is not rare: some surprise lands on a big name most weeks.

Now look at how those games were played. We use yards per play, adjusted for who each team has faced, because it is steadier than the score. One fumble swings a scoreboard by 7. It barely moves a team’s yards per play.

Three of the four losers gained more yards per play than the team that beat them. Penn State out-gained Rutgers by about 3 yards a play and lost by a point, with two turnovers to none. UCLA moved the ball better than Michigan, barely, and turned it over three times. Ohio State gained 6.8 a play, better than we expected, but ran only 49 plays to Nebraska’s 73. It could not keep the ball, and it lost the game it was winning per snap.

Only Indiana lost the way a real upset loses: Washington matched it play for play. That is the one game in the four where you could say the better team did not show up.

So: tough to win at Michigan? Yes. Michigan’s defense held UCLA to 4.0 yards a play, well under the 5.7 we expected. But Michigan gained even less. A game where both offenses crawl is a game a single turnover can decide. That is not chaos. That is what a low-scoring game is.

Where the uncertainty lives

Here is the trap in all of this. We talk about “Ohio State” or “Notre Dame” as if each were one solid thing. It is not. A team gets hurt, then gets healthy. A starter sits and a backup takes his reps. The team on the field in November is not quite the team that built its numbers in September, and a rating is an average over all of them. That is one honest reason a rating is never sure: it describes a team that keeps changing.

But watch what happens after a surprise. Everyone reaches for a story: the injury, the road crowd, the trap game. Some of those stories are true. The psychologist Paul Meehl warned that a room full of experts will invent a special reason for every case that does not fit, and that the special reasons mostly make predictions worse. A story told after the result always fits the result. So ask the story the same question you ask a number: would you have said it on Friday?

Is yards per play just a description of a good offense? Partly, yes. But it holds steady from week to week, which is what lets it look forward. Walking through this season one week at a time, it picked the winner about 65 times in 100, using only games already played. That is a measure worth trusting, and still far from a sure thing.

Notre Dame and Wake Forest

Notre Dame was surgical, then it stopped. The Irish led SMU 17–3 at halftime and won 24–3. They gained 7.2 yards a play, above the 6.5 we expected, and scored nothing in the fourth quarter. The defense is the story. SMU gained 327 yards and scored 3 points; it converted 6 of 17 third downs and managed 4.7 a play. Yards are not points until they cross the goal line.

Wake Forest keeps scoring. Wake Forest beat Georgia Tech 50–29 at 8.7 yards a play and is 10–1. That is 99 points in two weeks. Last Saturday it beat SMU 49–7.

So here is a tempting sum. Wake Forest beat SMU by 42. Notre Dame beat SMU by 21. Is Wake Forest three touchdowns better? Hold onto that thought, and then drop it. A common opponent is one game against each, a week apart, and you just saw how far one game can stray from how it was played. The two scores are honest. The subtraction is not.

What to watch today, and why

The same idea works on the NFL card. Ask two things about any game. Is it close to a coin flip? And could the result move a team in or out of January? You need both.

Baltimore at Carolina is a true coin flip with a season on it. Our ratings make it 50–50. Carolina makes the playoffs in 52 of every 100 seasons we simulate, the most unsettled number in the league; Baltimore sits at 14. Jets at Chargers is the opposite. It is a fair contest, and it decides nothing: both clubs make the playoffs in fewer than 1 season in 100.

What to take home, and the road ahead

What to take home: before you call a day chaos, ask how many surprises a normal day holds. On a college Saturday the answer is about sixteen, give or take six. Then look past the score to how the game was played. Most of yesterday’s famous losers played like the better team and lost the coin toss that every close game hides. That is not a reason to stop watching. It is the reason to watch.
